Take one thing off your bench — a microcontroller board, a motor, a
handful of resistors — and ask what it cost. The receipt says one
number. That number covers the copper, the silicon, the plastic, the
factory, the ship, the truck, and somebody's margin. It does not cover
the tailings pond, the river downstream of the refinery, the lungs of
the person who dismantled the last generation of the same board, or
the century the plastic spends not decomposing. Economists have a word
for a cost that lands on somebody outside the transaction: an
**externality**. It is not a synonym for "bad". It is a description of
where the bill goes when the price tag refuses to carry it.

| Stage of a board's life | On the price tag | Not on the price tag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mining and refining | Ore, energy, wages, equipment | Tailings, water use, air quality, who lives beside the mine |
| Manufacture | Fab time, packaging, testing | Solvents, process gases, industrial water |
| Use | Your electricity bill | The generation mix behind that electricity |
| Disposal | Sometimes a recycling levy | Informal dismantling, open burning, leachate |

The numbers at each end are worth knowing rather than feeling. The
United Nations' e-waste monitoring work puts global electronic waste
at roughly 62 million tonnes in a recent reporting year, with only
about a fifth of it formally collected and recycled. Cobalt for
lithium-ion cells comes overwhelmingly from one country, some of it
from artisanal mines with no meaningful safety regime. And the fact
that gets quoted in every one of these conversations is real: a tonne
of discarded circuit boards contains more gold than a tonne of gold
ore — which tells you the value is genuinely there, and that
recovering it is hard enough that landfill keeps winning anyway.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Put a number on it. Pick one component on your bench, find what it
   sells for, and find any published figure for the energy or
   materials behind it. How close can you get before the data runs
   out — and what does the point where it runs out tell you?
2. Who should pay for a device's afterlife: the company that made it,
   the person who bought it, or the municipality that collects it?
   Defend one, then say honestly what behaviour your choice rewards
   and what it punishes.
3. "Shipped overseas for recycling" can mean a certified facility or
   an open burn site next to a school. The container looks identical
   leaving port. What proof should be required before the good
   version is believed, and who should be made to produce it?
4. Your bench runs on a grid. Does the environmental case for any
   piece of electronics change depending on where it is plugged in?
   If it does, what does that mean for a claim like "this device is
   green"?
5. You will design and build things this semester. Does knowing all
   of the above create any obligation in *your* design decisions —
   part count, lifespan, repairability, choosing a part that is still
   made in five years? Or is that an unfair weight to put on one
   student's project?

> [!warning] Be careful with the numbers you carry out of here
> Every figure in this page is a published estimate with a method
> behind it, and estimates from different methods disagree —
> sometimes by a lot. Cite the source and the year when you use one,
> and say "roughly" when the source says roughly. An argument built
> on a number you cannot attribute is weaker than one built on no
> number at all, because the first can be dismantled and the second
> can only be discussed.

This is not a guilt exercise, and nobody is being asked to feel bad
about a resistor. [[Careers and the Environment]] carries the
background; what you decide here shows up later in what you choose to
build, what you choose to keep running, and what you are willing to
put your name on.
