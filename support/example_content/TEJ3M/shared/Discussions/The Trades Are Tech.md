Last year this argument was about respect: who gets called a
technology worker, and why "good with your hands" still gets said like
a consolation prize. That argument still stands. But something
concrete has happened since, and it changes the conversation from
opinion into evidence — you now do the work. You take measurements and
record them with units. You read a datasheet to find a limit rather
than guessing at one. You solder a joint that has to survive
vibration. Those are not school approximations of trade skills; they
are the trade skills, at the level a first-year apprentice is expected
to arrive with.

Look at what an electrician, an instrumentation and control
technician, or a powerline technician actually has to hold in their
head: circuit theory, load calculations, a code book that is revised
regularly and is legally binding, and a working respect for energy
that can kill in under a second. The math is real. The consequences
are real. The pay is real, and it arrives during training rather than
after it, because an apprentice earns while they learn — which is a
materially different financial proposition from four years of tuition
first.[^1]

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Name three things you did at this bench in the last month that an
   apprentice does in their first month on a job site. Now name three
   things a first-year engineering student does that you have not
   done. What does the comparison actually show — and what does it
   fail to show?
2. An apprentice earns from year one and finishes licensed; a
   university student pays for four years first and finishes with a
   degree and a debt. Both routes end in skilled technical work. Why
   is only one of them ever described as "settling", and who benefits
   from that framing?
3. The trades and the engineering professions both gate entry —
   apprenticeship hours and certification on one side, accreditation
   and licensure on the other. Are those gates protecting the public,
   protecting the incumbents, or both at once? What evidence would
   tell them apart?
4. "Automation will take the trades" versus "somebody has to install,
   calibrate, and repair the automation". Which claim survives contact
   with evidence, and what would each one need to be true? Be
   specific about *which* tasks, not which jobs.
5. The skilled trades in Canada remain heavily male while nothing
   about the work requires it. What keeps the numbers stuck — and
   which of the usual fixes (posters, pay, mentors, tools that fit
   more hands, sites where harassment gets people fired) would you
   bet actually moves them?

The pathways are concrete rather than inspirational.
[[Careers and the Environment]] maps which doors open from secondary
school, apprenticeship, college, and university — and the habits this
course banks are the first rungs of several of them. A student who can
produce a correct measurement with units, a legible schematic, and a
clean solder joint is not preparing for a technical career. They have
started one, and the paperwork is a formality that comes later.

[^1]: A Certificate of Qualification is provincial, but many trades
    carry a Red Seal endorsement, which is recognised across Canadian
    provinces and territories — meaning the same credential travels
    with you if the work does. Worth knowing before you choose where
    to train.
