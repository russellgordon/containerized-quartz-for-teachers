> [!abstract] At a glance
> Individually or in pairs · the final block of the course · a written
> proposal, a build log, and a working device of your own design ·
> demonstrated at [[The Engineering Showcase]]

## What you are making

A device that nobody specified for you. It has to combine all three
halves of this course — analogue circuitry you calculated, digital logic
or decision-making, and embedded code — into one object that does
something a person would actually want done.

Everything else you have built this semester was a job handed to you.
This one starts with a problem you chose and a proposal you had to
defend. That is the difference between a technician following a work
order and an engineer answering a question, and it is why this task is
worth the weight it carries.

Scope it honestly. A device that does one thing dependably, measured and
documented, beats an ambitious pile of wires every single time — and the
proposal stage exists so that we can tell you that before you spend two
weeks finding out.

## Milestones

- [ ] **Proposal**, in writing, submitted at launch. It contains: the
      problem in one sentence; who has it; a block diagram; a parts list
      with real prices and where they come from; the three things most
      likely to go wrong; and a test plan naming the measurements that
      will prove the device works.
- [ ] **Design review** with your teacher. Come with your calculations
      and your current budget, not just your idea.
- [ ] **Schematic and code structure** before the first component is
      soldered.
- [ ] **Build log**, kept as you go, not written afterwards: date, what
      you did, what it cost in time, what failed, what you tried, what
      the result was. This is a professional habit before it is a
      deliverable.
- [ ] **Test plan executed**, with the data recorded, including the
      trials that failed.
- [ ] **Demonstration** at [[The Engineering Showcase]], with a working
      run, a measured claim, and one honest failure mode.

## How it is assessed

The criteria below, weighted as [[How Marks Work]] sets out. Two things
are worth saying plainly. First, the build log is assessed as work, not
as paperwork — an engineer who cannot say what they did yesterday cannot
be trusted with tomorrow. Second, a device that does not fully work but
whose log shows disciplined, documented, well-reasoned engineering scores
well above a device that works by accident.

Safety runs through every bench period: separate supplies for loads,
diodes across anything inductive, current budgets checked before power,
and the standing agreement in [[Safety in the Lab]] applied without being
asked. [[When Good Enough Is Not Safe]] is the discussion this task is
the practical answer to.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like at your bench |
| --- | --- |
| A problem worth solving | The one-sentence problem names a person and their difficulty |
| A defensible design | Block diagram, calculations, and current budget all agree |
| Three strands, combined | Analogue circuitry, decision logic, and code, all yours |
| A log kept as it happened | Dated entries, failures included, written at the bench |
| A test plan executed | The measurements you promised, taken, including the bad ones |
| An honest account of limits | You can name what it does not do and why |
| Professional safety practice | Supplies separated, loads protected, no reminders needed |

## Reflect

Your [[Final Reflection]] takes this project as its main evidence, and
[[Showing Growth]] asks you to set it beside your first entry from Unit 1.
The question is not whether you got better — you did — but *what
specifically* you can now do that you could not do in September, and what
you would need next in order to do this professionally.

> [!warning]- If your scope is slipping and there are two periods left
> Cut, and cut early. Decide today which single function is the device,
> and make that one work completely — measured, logged, demonstrable.
> A finished half of an ambitious project is a project. An unfinished
> whole one is a story about what you meant to do. Write the cut and
> its reasoning in the build log; deciding what to abandon is
> engineering, and we will assess it as such.
