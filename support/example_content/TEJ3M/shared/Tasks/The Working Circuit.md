## What you are making

A small circuit that does one useful thing, built properly: a continuity
beeper, a low-battery indicator, a two-colour polarity checker, an
adjustable-brightness lamp, a simple light-activated switch. Your own
idea is welcome if you can state its job in one sentence and defend its
component values with arithmetic.

The difference between this and anything you built in Grade 10 is that
every value on your schematic has to be *justified before it is bought*.
You will hand in a page of calculations that predicted your circuit's
behaviour, and a page of measurements that either confirmed those
predictions or explained itself. [[Ohm's Law]] and [[Power and Heat]] are
the tools; [[Solder a Board]] is the construction standard.

## Milestones

- [ ] **One sentence.** What the circuit does, and who it is for.
- [ ] **Schematic with values**, drawn to the conventions in
      [[Reading Schematics]], with reference designators on every part.
- [ ] **Calculation sheet.** Every resistor value derived, not chosen.
      Every part's power dissipation checked against its rating.
- [ ] **Breadboard prototype**, measured against your predictions before
      anything is soldered. Fix the design here, not on the board.
- [ ] **Soldered board**, joints inspected, leads clipped, tested for
      continuity before power.
- [ ] **Measurement sheet**: predicted and measured side by side, with a
      named cause for every gap.
- [ ] **Photograph log** to the standard in [[Documenting Your Build]].

## How it is assessed

The criteria below are the whole of it, and they are visible at your
bench rather than inferred from a finished object. Bench periods are
class time, and using them well is part of the assessment — see
[[How Marks Work]]. Your [[Tech Journal]] carries the process: what you
predicted, what you measured, and what you changed as a result.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like at your bench |
| --- | --- |
| A circuit that does its job | The one-sentence job is done, repeatably, on demand |
| Values that were derived | Every resistor traced back to a calculation you can walk through |
| Ratings respected | Power dissipation checked for every part, before power was applied |
| A schematic that matches | The drawing and the board agree, part for part |
| Joints that will survive | Shiny, concave, mechanically sound, leads trimmed |
| Predictions reconciled | Each gap between predicted and measured has a named cause |
| Safe habits without reminders | Glasses on, iron in its stand, extraction running |

## Reflect

Your [[Tech Journal]] entry for this task is about one number: the
prediction you got most wrong, and what you now know that you did not
know when you wrote it. [[What a Strong Entry Looks Like]] shows the
shape of a strong entry; the content has to be yours.

> [!success]- If your circuit works and you have time left
> Make it worse on purpose, then measure. Swap in a resistor one
> standard value up and predict the new current before you power it.
> Run it for ten minutes and feel every part. Reverse a component that
> is safe to reverse and record what changes. A circuit you have only
> ever seen working is a circuit you do not yet understand.
