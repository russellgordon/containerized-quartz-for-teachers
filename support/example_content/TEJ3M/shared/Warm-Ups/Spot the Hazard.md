A photo of a bench goes up — staged the evening before, never a real
student's workspace — and the question has not changed since Grade 10:
what here could hurt someone, or something? What has changed is the
bench. There is a soldering iron in the frame now, and a supply that
can push real current, and a meter that can be set to destroy itself.
[[Safety in the Lab]] gets read in the first week; this routine is how
it stays read.

## How to run it

1. Show the photo. One quiet minute; everyone counts, nobody calls
   out. Counts get written down before any discussion.
2. Poll the counts. The spread between the lowest and the highest is
   the hook — somebody in this room is seeing things you are not.
3. Name hazards one at a time. Each one gets three answers: what is
   the harm, who or what gets hurt, and what is the fix?
4. Close on the sneakiest one nobody caught. There is always one.

## The bench sweep

This is the same list you should run over your own bench before you
energise anything. It takes about eight seconds once it is a habit.

- [ ] Iron in its stand, cord clear of the work, nothing resting on
      the tip guard
- [ ] Safety glasses on faces, not on the shelf — leads get clipped
      and offcuts fly
- [ ] Meter dial and jacks match what you are about to measure, and
      the leads are not still in the current jack from last period
- [ ] Supply set to the voltage you meant, with the current limit set
      low enough to protect the circuit rather than cook it
- [ ] Nothing energised that has not been checked by a second person
- [ ] Large capacitors on any board you are about to handle are
      discharged, not merely unplugged
- [ ] No drinks anywhere; walkways clear; sleeves and hair secured

> [!example]- A staged bench worth stealing
> An iron lying on the mat with its cord looped under the board; a
> meter with the dial on volts and both leads still plugged into the
> 10 A jack; a bench supply with the current limit wound fully open;
> safety glasses folded on the shelf; a power-supply board with one
> fat electrolytic capacitor, unplugged ten seconds ago; a can of
> pop beside the mat. Six hazards. Most classes find four. The two
> that get missed are the meter's jacks and the capacitor — because
> both look exactly like a safe bench in a photograph, and neither
> does anything until it does everything at once.

## One variation

Students stage the photo. Building a convincingly hazardous bench —
without creating a real hazard while shooting it, which is a rule of
the exercise and not a technicality — requires knowing every rule you
are pretending to break. The staging is worth more than the solving.

> [!tip] Fix beats find
> Spotting the hazard is step one; describing the safe version of the
> same bench is the prize. End every round with the fix, and pull in
> [[Anti-Static Habits]] whenever the missing strap turns up, and
> [[Soldering Safely]] whenever the iron does.
