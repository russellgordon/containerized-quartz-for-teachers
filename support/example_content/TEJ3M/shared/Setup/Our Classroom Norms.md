This course hands you instruments, components, and circuits that can
be wrong in ways nobody can see — and asks you to be confused in
public while holding them. That takes norms, and they bind everyone in
the room, including your teacher.

> [!danger] The core agreement
> Nobody's dead circuit gets laughed at. Every technician you would
> trust has destroyed a component, wired something backwards, blown a
> meter fuse, and asked for help with something they felt they should
> already know. That is not the exception in this trade. That is how
> the trade is learned.

## What we agree to

- **Predict before you power, with a number.** Saying out loud what
  you expect to measure — and being wrong — is the fastest route to
  being able to diagnose. A prediction that cannot be wrong is not a
  prediction, and "it should work" is not a number.
- **Measure before you argue.** Two benches disagreeing about what a
  circuit does is a question with an answer sitting on the bench.
  Get the meter. The instrument settles it, and neither of you loses.
- **Symptoms are information.** A hot component, a dim LED, a reading
  that will not settle — these are the circuit being as helpful as it
  knows how to be. We read them before we groan at them.
- **Roles rotate.** Hands, meter, recorder. The instrument changes
  hands every lab, with no exceptions for "but I am faster". Somebody
  who never drives the meter never learns to drive the meter.
- **Nothing is finished until somebody else could service it.** A
  build that works and cannot be understood scores below a tidy,
  documented build with an honest known-issues list. See
  [[Documenting Your Build]].
- **Honest about help.** A classmate's suggestion, a datasheet, a
  forum thread, an AI assistant — all completely fine **when named**,
  in your journal and in your write-ups, exactly the way professionals
  credit their sources. Presenting someone else's work as your own
  thinking is the one unforgivable fault in this room.
- **Technology serves people.** When we argue about e-waste,
  standards, repairability, and
  [[The Trades Are Tech|whose work counts]], we argue with ideas and
  never at people. Come ready to change your mind; it is a strength.

## Why this is not just being nice

A lab that shares half-working circuits learns faster, because faults
get found while they are still cheap. A lab where people admit
confusion finds design errors before they get soldered in. A lab where
help is credited can tell the difference between a student who
understands something and one who was helped — which matters, because
only one of those two is safe to trust with the next build.

None of these are school rules with a technical coat of paint. They
are the profession's own working norms, learned early. Every one of
them has an industrial equivalent with a more formal name and a longer
history.
