The last ten minutes belong to the room. There is another class four minutes
behind you, and the state you leave the studio in decides what kind of period
they get. This is the least glamorous page on the site and one of the two or
three that matter most.

## In this order

**Brushes first**, because they are the only thing on the table with a
deadline. Acrylic hardening in the ferrule ends the brush permanently.
Rinse, reshape the hairs with your fingers, and lay them flat or bristles
up — never standing in water.

**Lids on** before anything else dries out. A tube left open is a tube
somebody else cannot use next week.

**Wet work to the rack**, labelled with your name, your class, and the
date. Unlabelled work on a drying rack becomes anonymous within one day.

**Tools back where they live.** The shelf has a shape; put it back in that
shape. Sharps — blades, cutters, needles — go into the sharps container,
never a bin liner that somebody will lift by the bottom.

**Surfaces damp, floors damp.** Wipe tables with a wet sponge and mop rather
than sweep — sweeping dry clay or plaster dust lifts it into the air you are
still breathing. **Hands last**, properly, before you eat anything.

> [!danger] What never goes down the sink
> **Plaster and clay slurry** set inside the trap and stop the drain
> permanently — this is not caution, it is plumbing. **Solvents and
> oil-based ink** must never enter a drain at all. Acrylic rinse water
> goes into the settling bucket first; when the solids have dropped, the
> clear water is poured off and the sludge is binned dry. If you are
> holding a container and you are not sure, put it down and ask.

## Waste is a decision

Most of what leaves an art room did not have to. Cut from the offcuts box
before opening a fresh sheet. Keep newsprint and packing paper for the
next print run. Put reclaimable clay in the reclaim bucket while it is
still damp, because dry scraps take days to recover. Empty ink and paint
containers and anything labelled hazardous go to a household hazardous
waste depot, not the classroom bin.

None of this is tidiness for its own sake. A studio is shared equipment, and
looking after it is part of using it — the practice [[Materials and Safety]]
describes and [[Art and the Environment]] argues for.
[[How a Studio Period Works]] is where these ten minutes sit.
