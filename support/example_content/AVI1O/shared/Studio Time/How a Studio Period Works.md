Most periods follow the same shape, and after a fortnight you will run it
without being told. The shape exists for one reason: a seventy-five minute
period contains about fifty minutes of real working time, and the periods
that feel wasted are the ones where those fifty went on getting ready.

## The shape of the period

| Roughly | What is happening | What you should be doing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 min | Arriving, materials out | Set up before you sit down |
| 10 min | Demonstration, or work on the wall | Watching — not starting |
| 50 min | Studio time | The work, at your own pace |
| 10 min | Clearing up | See [[Clearing Up]] |

The demonstration is the part people try to skip by starting early, and the
part that saves them an hour later. You cannot un-cut a block.

## Using fifty minutes well

**Decide before you sit down.** Walk in knowing the one thing you intend
to finish today. A vague plan turns into twenty minutes of sharpening
pencils and looking at your own work from an angle.

**Start with something physical.** The first minutes of a working session
are the slowest, so begin with a task that has no decision in it — mixing a
ground, taping paper, transferring a design — and the thinking follows.

**Work on the largest unresolved area first.** Detail is comfortable and
composition is not, which is why unfinished work is so often beautifully
detailed in one corner and empty everywhere else.

**Stand up and look, twice.** Step back four or five paces early on and
again near the end. Problems of proportion and value are invisible at arm's
length and obvious from across a room, and the last five minutes are for
looking rather than adding.

## The room is shared

Talking while you work is fine and often useful — drift is the problem, not
noise. Tools go back where they live so the next person can find them, and
one borrowed from another table is not yours for the period. Materials are
shared, so wasting them is a decision about someone else's work too.
