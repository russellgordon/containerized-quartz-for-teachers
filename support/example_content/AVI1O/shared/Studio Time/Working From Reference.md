Nobody draws a horse from memory well, and artists have always worked from
something in front of them. A reference is a source of information —
photographs, objects on a table, your own hand in a mirror, a page of your
own earlier sketches. Using one is normal practice. Using one badly, or
using someone else's without saying so, is where the trouble starts.

## Using a reference effectively

A reference answers a question. What does the far side of a jaw do when
the head turns? How does a sleeve fold at the elbow? Go to the photograph
for that answer, then come back to your own composition. If instead you
begin by copying the whole photograph, you have inherited someone else's
framing, lighting, and cropping, and you have made none of the decisions
that make a work yours.

Two things are worth knowing about photographs. A camera flattens the range
of light, so shadows in a photo are blacker and flatter than the ones your
eye sees; and a lens exaggerates whatever is nearest it. Correcting for both
is often the difference between a drawing that looks copied and one that
looks observed.

Better still: use several references for one work, and take your own
wherever you can. Your own photograph gives you the angle and light you
wanted, and settles the ownership question before it is asked.

## Using a reference ethically

A photograph belongs to whoever took it, including the ones that are easy
to find and download. Copying an image to learn from it is completely
legitimate and always has been — practice has never been the issue.
Displaying that copy as your own invention is.

So the rule in this studio is credit, not prohibition. Write the source
next to the drawing in your sketchbook every time, including the ones you
only glanced at. If a finished work leans heavily on somebody else's
image, name it on the label. Where you need an image you can freely use,
public domain and openly licensed collections exist, and their terms are
written down.

> [!question]- Is this mine? Three questions (click to expand)
> Could someone find the original and recognise it instantly as the same
> picture? Did I make the choices about composition, light, and colour, or
> did I inherit them? Have I written the source down where my teacher can
> see it? A work that fails the first two and passes the third is a study
> — worth doing, worth keeping, labelled as what it is.
