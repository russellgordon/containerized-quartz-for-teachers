Most people look at a work of art for a few seconds, decide how they feel
about it, and move on. That first reaction is real and worth having — but
it is a beginning, and this page is about the second look, which is where
almost everything interesting happens.

## The first look is evidence

Your initial reaction tells you what the work does to a viewer, and you are a
viewer. Record it before you know anything about the piece: what it brought
to mind, what mood it carries, what puzzled you, what you disliked. Write it
down immediately, because you can never have a first reaction twice — once
you have read the label and heard three classmates, it is gone.

Be honest here rather than tactful. "It made me uncomfortable" and "it looked
like something in a hotel corridor" are both usable findings. "It's nice" is
not a reaction; it is a way of not having one.

## The second look is slower

Now look again, with the elements in hand. The question that unlocks most
images is simply: **where did my eye go first, and what put it there?**

> [!note] Sixty seconds, timed
> Give one work a full minute by the clock without speaking or scrolling.
> A minute is far longer than it sounds, and somewhere around the
> forty-second mark you will notice something you had not seen. Most of
> what people call a boring work is a work that got four seconds.

The answer is almost always an element doing something. A jump in value that
separates a figure from its background. A line that leads you in from the
corner. One saturated colour in a field of dull ones. Naming the cause
turns a feeling into a statement you can defend: not "it feels lonely" but
"it feels lonely because a small figure sits in a large, cool, empty space."

## Expect the two looks to disagree

The second look often contradicts the first, and that shift is the most
valuable thing you can report. A work you found boring may be quiet on
purpose; one you liked at once may have nothing else to give. Say what
changed your mind — that sentence is the difference between an opinion and
a judgement, and it does not make your first reaction wrong.

[[The Elements of Design]] and [[The Principles of Design]] are the
vocabulary this depends on. [[Describing What You See]] is the second look
practised alone, [[Interpreting a Work]] is what comes after it, and
[[The Critical Analysis Process]] is the whole method in order.
