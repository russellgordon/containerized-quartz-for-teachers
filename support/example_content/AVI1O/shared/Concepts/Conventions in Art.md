A convention is an agreement so old that people forget it was ever agreed.
When a picture shows a road running to a tiny point on the horizon, nothing
in the paint is far away — you have learned to read flat marks as distance.
Conventions are how images say more than they contain, and they cut both
ways: you use them to make, and you decode them to look.

## Four the curriculum names

**Symbol.** One thing standing for another by agreement. A dove, a crown, a
chain, an open door — none of these mean anything on their own. They mean
what a particular group of people has settled on, which is why a symbol can
be read instantly by one audience and be invisible to another.

**Metaphor.** Not a swap but an overlay: showing one thing *as* another so
that the qualities of the second come along with it. A city drawn as a
machine is making a claim about the city.

**Simile.** The gentler version, where the comparison stays visible — this is
*like* that, rather than this *is* that.

**Allegory.** A whole work running as an extended metaphor, each figure
standing for an idea rather than a person. Allegory has to be taught before
it can be read, which is why such works often arrived with a lesson.[^1]

## Reading is not guessing

A viewer reading conventions is doing something checkable. The evidence is in
the work: what repeats, what is enlarged, what sits at the centre, what is
left out. A reading that cannot point at anything on the surface is a guess
about the artist's mood — see [[Interpreting a Work]] for what to do instead.

And conventions expire. Symbols shift meaning between one century and the
next, and between one community and the next, so a work made to be perfectly
clear can reach us genuinely puzzling. That is a fact about audiences rather
than a failure of the work, and it is why [[Art and Its Society]] exists.

So borrow deliberately. A symbol your audience does not share will not do the
job you assigned it — worth checking in [[The Class Critique]] first.

[^1]: The curriculum also names *synectics* in this list — a way of generating
    images by forcing two unlike things together and seeing what the collision
    produces. It appears without a definition in the document's own glossary,
    so treat it as an idea-generating method rather than a convention a viewer
    reads.
