A mark is what happens when a tool meets a surface — material left behind, or
taken away. That is the whole definition, and it is smaller than most people
expect. Every drawing, print, and painting in this course is an accumulation
of marks, so the difference between work that looks alive and work that looks
dead usually starts here rather than in the idea.

## Four things you are controlling

The same pencil makes a dozen different marks depending on what your hand
does with it.

- **Pressure.** Light and dark from one tool, no other change required.
- **Speed.** A fast line has different edges than a slow one. You can see the
  speed of a mark, which means a viewer can feel your hesitation.
- **Angle.** A pencil on its side is a completely different instrument from a
  pencil on its point.
- **Surface.** Paper with tooth grabs; smooth paper lets the tool slide. Half
  of a material's character actually belongs to what it is sitting on — more
  on that in [[Surface and Texture]].

## Marks that do a job

Some marks are named because they solve a specific problem. Hatching — a
set of roughly parallel lines — builds up value without smudging.
Cross-hatching lays a second set across the first, and each extra layer
darkens and starts to suggest a surface turning away from the light. Stippling
does the same thing with dots. In watercolour, a thin transparent wash lets
the white of the paper come back up through the colour, which is how paint
manages to look lit from behind rather than merely pale.

None of these are tricks. They are ways of getting a particular effect on
purpose, which is the entire difference between a technique and an accident
you cannot repeat.

> [!success]- Try this — one page, one tool, twelve marks (click to expand)
> Take a single pencil and fill a sketchbook page with twelve marks that are
> unmistakably different from each other. No new tools allowed. When you run
> out of ideas at about mark seven, that is the point of the exercise starting
> — the last five are the ones that teach you something.

Keep the results. A page of marks you have actually made is worth more later
than any chart, because you know what your hand did to get each one.
[[Drawing]] and [[The Media Trials]] both build directly on this, and every
tool you meet has its own rules in [[Materials and Safety]].
