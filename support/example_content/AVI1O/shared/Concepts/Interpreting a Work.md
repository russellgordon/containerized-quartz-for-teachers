Interpretation is where you say what a work means, what it is for, and what
it is doing — and then show your reasoning. It is not a free-for-all and it
is not a hunt for the one right answer the teacher already has. It is an
argument, and arguments are judged by their evidence.

## Three questions, in this order

**What is the subject?** Not just what is depicted, but what the work is
*about*, which can be different. A picture of a table covered in food may be
about abundance, or waste, or the fact that somebody has left.

**What was it for?** Almost every work was made for a purpose and a place —
to be worshipped in front of, to sell something, to record a face before
photography, to make a wall less blank, to argue. A work removed from its
purpose can look strange or empty until you know what it was doing.

**What does it convey?** The meaning that reaches a viewer, which is not
always the meaning the maker intended, and both are worth naming.

## Evidence, or it is a guess

Every claim you make should be able to finish the sentence "...because in the
work, ___". The blank gets filled from [[Describing What You See]]: the
placement, the scale, the colour, the thing that repeats, the thing that is
missing. An interpretation with nothing in that blank is a statement about
your own mood, which is interesting but not shareable.

Historical works add a second source of evidence: what the people it was made
for already knew. The symbols in [[Conventions in Art]] were legible to them
and may not be to you, and the honest move is to say so rather than invent.

> [!tip] Two readings can both be good
> A work that supports two well-evidenced readings is usually a better work
> than one that supports only a single obvious reading. When your
> interpretation and someone else's collide in [[The Class Critique]], the
> question is never who is right — it is whose reading accounts for more of
> what is actually in the work. Sometimes that is neither of you, and you go
> back and look again.

Modern viewers do not read old works the way their first audiences did, and
pretending otherwise is a kind of dishonesty. Say what the work seems to have
meant then, say what it means to you now, and keep the two apart. That
separation is most of the grade in [[The Interpretation]], and most of the
craft in [[Writing About Art]].
