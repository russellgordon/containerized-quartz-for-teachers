Nobody makes a work out of nothing. Every object you will study came out of a
particular place, with particular materials to hand, for people who held
particular beliefs and had reasons to pay for it. Read a work carefully
enough and you are reading its society — not because artists set out to
document their world, but because they could not avoid it.

## What a work quietly reports

**What they had.** Pigments, stone, metal, clay, dyes, tools. Materials that
had to be carried a long way are evidence of trade and of wealth, because
somebody paid for the journey.

**What they could do.** Scale and precision report engineering. A structure
that required thousands of people working together for years is telling you
about organisation and labour before it tells you anything about beauty.

**What they believed.** The images a society repeats — what it centres, what
it makes largest, what it buries with its dead — state fairly directly what
mattered. The cave paintings and tomb paintings the curriculum points to are
read exactly this way.

**Who was in charge.** Somebody chose the subject and paid the bill. Ask who,
and a surprising amount of art history reorganises itself.

**What they feared.** Protective images, weapons in the hands of gods, walls
covered in warnings. Fear leaves as clear a trace as pride.

## Two cautions worth carrying

The first is survivorship. What we have is what survived — stone over cloth,
buried over exposed, treasured over ordinary. The record tilts towards
whoever could afford permanence.[^1]

The second is that societies are not single-minded. A work made for a
powerful patron tells you what the patron wanted said, which may be what most
people there did not think.

Your own work is doing it too: what you make this term will report on the
tools you had and what your community argued about, whether you meant it to
or not. [[Art and Society Study]] is where you follow one work back.

[^1]: This is also why the curriculum's own examples are mostly monumental and
    mostly ancient. Worth noticing as a limitation of the sample rather than
    a fact about the past.
