Making things uses things up. A studio consumes paper, paint, water, plastic,
electricity, and packaging, and it produces waste at a rate that surprises
people the first time they actually watch the bin fill. None of that is a
reason to stop making art. It is a reason to make it with your eyes open,
which is a skill in this course rather than a lecture attached to it.

## Where the impact actually lands

| Where it lands | What it looks like here | What to do instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paper | A fresh sheet for a two-minute idea | Both sides, and an offcuts bin |
| Water | Rinse water poured straight away | Let solids settle; ask first |
| Solvents | Fumes now, residue later | Water-based where it will do |
| Packaging | Wrap and card straight to the bin | Keep it — it is a surface |
| Cartridges | Empties in the classroom garbage | The depot your school uses |
| The show | Single-use mounts and reprints | Reusable mounts, proof first |

The pattern is the same in every row: the environmental question and the
frugal question have the same answer. Artists have always worked this way,
not out of virtue but because materials cost money and a scrap box is a
resource.

## Reuse is a legitimate technique

Found materials are not the cheap option — they are a whole approach. A
surface that has already lived somewhere brings its own texture, colour, and
history into the work, and it will do things a fresh sheet cannot. Cardboard,
offcuts, packaging, fabric, and objects with wear on them are all serious
material, and [[Sculpture]] leans on them hardest.

There is a limit worth naming: reusing something does not make a weak idea
strong, and "it's recycled" is not a substitute for a reason. The material
still has to earn its place in the piece.

## The end of a piece is part of the piece

Work eventually comes down. Mounts, backing boards, and hanging materials
outlive the show, so store them rather than binning them. Whatever cannot be
reused is disposed of properly — your teacher will tell you what the school
does with paint sludge, solvent, and empties. Never the classroom garbage.

[[Clearing Up]] is where this becomes routine, and [[Materials and Safety]]
covers the same materials from the other direction: what protects you, rather
than what protects everything else.
