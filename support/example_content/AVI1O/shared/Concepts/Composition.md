Composition is the arrangement of everything inside the frame. It is decided
before most people think they have started, because the first compositional
choice is the shape of the paper and where you put the first mark on it. By
the time you notice the arrangement is wrong, you have usually spent an hour
rendering something beautifully in the wrong place.

## The frame is part of the work

A drawing does not stop at the edge of the subject; it stops at the edge of
the page. The empty areas are not leftovers — they are shapes you made, and a
viewer reads them as hard as the ones you meant. Move a figure so its arm
touches the border and you have changed the whole picture.

Where the subject sits matters as much as what it is. Dead centre reads as
still, formal, sometimes confrontational. Off to one side reads as movement,
or as something about to leave. Cropped by the frame reads as a fragment of
something larger continuing outside the picture. None of these is correct.
They are different sentences.

## Deciding where the eye goes

A composition is a route. The viewer enters somewhere — usually at the
strongest contrast — travels, and either arrives somewhere worth arriving at
or wanders off the edge and leaves. Emphasis and movement, from
[[The Principles of Design]], are how you control that: lines that lead,
values that step from dark to light, shapes that repeat.

> [!tip] Look at it upside down
> Turn your work upside down, or hold it up to a mirror. You stop seeing *a
> face* and start seeing shapes, values, and weight — and the composition
> problem you have been staring past for forty minutes becomes obvious in
> about two seconds. Photographing it and shrinking the picture on your phone
> does the same job.

## Decide it small, first

Thumbnails are the cheapest tool in the studio. A composition sketched at the
size of a postage stamp takes thirty seconds and shows you the whole
arrangement at once, because at that size detail cannot distract you. Do
four, not one; the fourth is usually the one nobody expected, including you.

That habit carries into [[Working From Reference]], where the temptation is
to copy the framing you were handed, and into [[The Information Piece]],
where the arrangement *is* the message.
