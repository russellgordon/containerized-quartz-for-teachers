Description is the least glamorous part of looking at art and the part that
decides whether everything after it is worth anything. To describe is to say
what is actually there, in words another person could check against the work.
It sounds easy. It is the hardest habit here to build, because your brain
would rather tell you what a picture *is about* than what it contains.

## What counts as description

A description is checkable. Two people looking at the same work should
broadly agree on it, and if they do not, one of them has stopped describing.

- **Facts about the object.** Size, medium, surface, condition, shape of the
  support. A drawing the size of a stamp and the same drawing two metres tall
  are different works.
- **Subject.** What is depicted — figures, objects, a place, or nothing
  recognisable at all, which is itself a description.
- **The elements.** Where the darkest value sits, what the colours actually
  are, whether the lines are continuous or broken, how much space is empty.
- **The principles.** Where the emphasis falls, what repeats, whether the
  weight is balanced left to right.

That last pair is why [[The Elements of Design]] and
[[The Principles of Design]] came first. Without them you are stuck with
"there's a lot of stuff in the corner," which is not something anyone can
build on.

> [!warning] The moment you say "sad", you have stopped describing
> "The mood is sad" is a conclusion. What produced it — the low values, the
> figure turned away, the empty right half — is the description. Skipping to
> the conclusion feels efficient and quietly destroys the argument, because
> now you have nothing to offer anyone who reads the work differently. Save
> the conclusions for [[Interpreting a Work]]. They are stronger when they
> arrive last.

## Describe in an order

Whole before parts. Start with the overall structure — the biggest shapes,
the largest areas of light and dark, the general arrangement — and only then
move inward. Working outward from a detail you happened to notice describes
your wandering attention rather than the work.

Give it more time than feels necessary. Most first descriptions run dry after
ninety seconds and start again, better, at the third minute. That second wind
is where the useful observations live, and why [[Looking Twice]] exists.
