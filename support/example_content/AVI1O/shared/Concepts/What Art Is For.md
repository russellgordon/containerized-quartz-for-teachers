"Art" in a gallery, quietly lit, with a small label — that is one arrangement,
and a fairly recent one. For most of human history images were made to do
something, and the something was rarely "be admired by a stranger on a
Tuesday". Asking what a work is *for* is often the fastest way into
understanding it.

## Jobs images do

- **Record.** Faces, events, possessions, the appearance of a place. Before
  photography this was an entire profession.
- **Commemorate.** Mark a death, a victory, a founding. Monuments are
  arguments about what a community agrees to remember.
- **Worship.** Focus attention, house belief, teach a story to people who
  cannot read it. Devotional works are among the most common ever made.
- **Persuade.** Advertising and propaganda are the same craft aimed at
  different ends, and both are covered by
  [[Art That Tells You Something]].
- **Identify.** Signal who belongs to what — through emblems, dress,
  tattooing, the decoration of tools and buildings.
- **Criticise.** Satire, protest, and the political cartoon: images that
  argue against something, usually something powerful.
- **Transform a place.** Environmental and site-specific work, murals, and
  installations that change the experience of standing somewhere.
- **Give pleasure.** Not a lesser function. Beauty and skill have been reasons
  to make things for as long as things have been made.

Most real works do several of these at once, and the balance shifts over
time. A devotional painting can end up in a museum, where its original job is
no longer available to it and a new one — being looked at as art — takes
over.

> [!success]- Try this — five images, five jobs (click to expand)
> Think back over the last day and list five images you actually looked at: a
> logo, a thumbnail, a sign, a tattoo, a poster in a hallway. Name the job each
> one was doing and who was paying for it to be done. The uncomfortable
> discovery is how many were persuading you, and how few you chose to look at.

Knowing the function also protects you from a common error in interpretation
— judging a work for failing at something it never attempted. A protest
poster is not trying to be a quiet study, and saying so is not criticism.
[[Art and Society Study]] is where you follow this properly, and
[[Interpreting a Work]] is where the function becomes part of your evidence.
