An artist statement is the short piece of writing that stands beside your
work at [[The Exhibition]]. It is not a defence, an apology, or a summary
of the assignment — it is the one thing you get to say to somebody who is
about to look, and then it gets out of the way.

## What a visitor actually reads

Watch people at a show. They look for a few seconds, read about forty
words, look again, and move on. That is your whole audience. So:

- **About 150 words.** Longer is not read; it is skimmed and abandoned.
- **The first sentence carries the most weight**, because for many
  visitors it is the only one.
- **Plain words**, in the first person and the present tense. A visitor
  should not need a dictionary — and [[Where Visual Arts Leads]] is full
  of jobs where writing that plainly is the job.

## Stating an intention, not explaining the work away

Saying what you were after helps a viewer see more; announcing what the
work *means* leaves them nothing to do. Give what you worked from and what
you chose — not what the viewer should feel or what the symbols stand for.
Trust the piece to do its own work, which is what [[Interpreting a Work]]
describes from the viewer's side.

## A worked example

Here is a first draft, of the kind almost everyone writes:

> For this assignment we had to make a print based on a place. I chose my
> grandmother's kitchen because it is important to me. I used lino because
> we learned it in class, and my first plate ripped so I did it again. I
> hope you can feel how much the place means to me. It is about memory and
> family and how things stay with you.

> [!example]- What is wrong with it — decide before you open this
> It reports the assignment rather than the work; the medium happened to
> the writer instead of being chosen; the last sentence tells the visitor
> what to feel; and nothing in it describes anything you can see.

Same student, same piece, rewritten:

> This is my grandmother's kitchen, printed from a lino plate I cut three
> times. I kept only what I could remember without checking: the corner of
> the table, the door frame, the light from the left. Everything I was
> unsure of I left as flat black. Lino refuses small detail, and that
> suits the subject — you cannot carve a face you half remember. The white
> areas are the parts of the room I have lost.

## Before you hand one in

Read it out loud to somebody who is not in this course. If they ask what
you meant, it is not finished; if they look back at the work, it is. The
skill outlasts the course — see [[What You Take With You]].
