Sculpture is the first work you make that has no front. A drawing is seen
from one place; a sculpture is walked around, and every viewpoint is one you
are responsible for. So build one habit in the first ten minutes: turn the
work constantly, and never make many marks from the same standing position.

## Two ways to make a form

**Additive** means building up: coils and slabs of clay, wire bent and
joined, cardboard and papier-mâché, found objects fastened together. You can
add and remove freely, which is forgiving and easy to fiddle with forever.

**Subtractive** means taking away: carving a plaster block, a bar of soap, a
piece of rigid foam. Nothing comes back, so subtractive work is planned work
— draw the form from at least two sides, mark the block, and remove the
largest waste before you go near any detail.

## Armatures

An armature is the skeleton. Wire, dowel, or a wooden cross screwed to a
base holds material up while it is soft and stops a standing figure sagging
at the ankles. Two facts worth knowing before you build one:

- Clay shrinks as it dries and an armature does not, so clay left to dry on
  a wire armature cracks. Build over an armature you can remove, or accept
  that the piece will not be fired.
- **Nothing with an armature inside it goes in the kiln.** Kiln work must be
  hollow, with walls under about two centimetres, and vented with a pin
  hole. Trapped air and thick solid sections turn to steam and break the
  piece, sometimes taking its neighbours with it.

## Working in three dimensions

Check the silhouette — stand back and look at the outline against a plain
wall, because that is what a viewer reads first from across a room. Gravity
is a material too: a piece that leans wants to fall, and a base that is too
small will prove it. Build the base into the idea rather than adding one in
the last five minutes.

> [!warning] The three that actually hurt people
> **Carving tools** go away from your body and away from the hand steadying
> the work. **Plaster gets hot as it sets** — a chemical reaction, not a
> temperature you can guess, so nobody casts a hand, a finger, or any other
> body part in this room, ever. **Dry clay and plaster dust** is never
> swept; sweeping puts it into the air you breathe. Damp sponge, damp mop.
