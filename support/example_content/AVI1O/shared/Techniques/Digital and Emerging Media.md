The honest version first. A school studio may have a set of tablets, a
booked computer lab, one good camera, or none of those. What it certainly
has is a room full of people carrying cameras that were professional
equipment twenty years ago. Every digital task here is written so it works
with whatever we have, and so nobody is ranked by the price of a device.

## What layers are for

The one thing digital drawing gives you that paper cannot is layers. Not
filters, not brushes that imitate chalk — layers. Sketch on one, draw the
line work on a second, put colour on a third underneath, and each of those
decisions stays independent of the others. You can fade the sketch to a
ghost or throw the colour away without losing the drawing. A digital painter
still works big shapes first and detail last, exactly like one in acrylic,
but can change their mind at any point.

Undo cuts both ways. It makes experimenting free, and it also makes it
possible to adjust the same eyebrow for forty minutes. Set a rule before
you start: three attempts, keep the best, move on.

## Photography as a medium, not a record

A photograph is a set of decisions — where you stood, what you left out,
what light you waited for. Shoot in soft daylight rather than on-camera
flash, get closer than feels comfortable, and take far more frames than you
need. Photographing something yourself also settles a problem the whole
course runs into: a picture you took is a picture you own. See
[[Working From Reference]].

## What to expect from a screen

A screen is backlit and a painting is not, so the two never match exactly.
Colour on your phone, on the classroom monitor, and out of the printer will
all differ, and brightness is usually the culprit. Judge value with the
screen at a normal setting, and test-print small before printing large.[^files]

## Emerging means new, not magical

Whatever tool arrives next — and something will — treat it as a material
with a behaviour, the way you treat acrylic or lino. Ask what it does well,
what it does badly, and which part of the work is yours. If a tool made part
of an image you hand in, say so on the label: that is not a penalty, it is
the same credit rule as any source. See [[Copyright, Ownership, and Credit]].

[^files]: Keep the layered working file in the app's own format and export a
    flattened copy to hand in. A JPEG is a compressed copy that loses a
    little more every time it is saved over — fine as a final export, wrong
    as the place you keep the original.
