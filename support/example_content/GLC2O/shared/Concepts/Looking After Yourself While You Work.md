Stress is not a sign that something has gone wrong with you. A moderate
amount of it sharpens attention, which is why you can focus the night
before a test and not on a Tuesday in October. This page is about the
other kind: the sort that stays, and costs you sleep, marks, or people.

## Strategies that people actually use

None of these works for everyone, and none works every time. The point
is to have several, so that when one is unavailable you are not out of
options.

- **Physical.** Sleep, food you can afford and will eat, going outside,
  moving. Unglamorous and consistently effective.
- **Structural.** A written list of what is due, in the order it is due.
  Much of what feels like anxiety is an unsorted pile.
- **People.** Telling one person — a friend, a family member, a coach,
  an Elder or knowledge keeper, a teacher.
- **Cultural and spiritual.** Practices your family or community already
  uses. You may already have the most reliable strategy on this list.
- **Distance.** Time off a screen, music, a walk, a break that is
  actually a break and not homework in a different room.

## When stress stops being useful

The curriculum asks you to be able to recognise this, so here it is
plainly. The signs tend to show up in three places at once:

- **Thinking** — all-or-nothing conclusions, expecting the worst,
  persistent harsh thoughts about yourself, or thoughts of hurting
  yourself.
- **Feeling** — worry that does not switch off, sadness that stays,
  feeling flattened or hopeless, moods that swing further than usual.
- **Doing** — pulling away from friends, avoiding school or work, marks
  or shifts slipping, sleeping or eating differently, using more
  caffeine, nicotine, alcohol, or other substances.

One of these for a day is a bad day. Several of them, holding for more
than a couple of weeks, is the point at which a person needs more than a
strategy — they need someone else involved.

## Asking, and who to ask

You do not need the right words. "Something has been bothering me, do
you have a few minutes?" is enough, and so is asking someone else to ask
for you. Supports sit in three rings: people you already know, people at
school whose job this is, and services outside school — clinics, youth
wellness hubs, community and cultural organisations, and helplines you
can text or call.

If a friend comes to you, listen, then tell a trusted adult. Being a
good friend is not the same as being the help, and it was never your
job to be the help.

> These are national, free, and available at any hour, and they do not

>
> - **9-8-8 — Suicide Crisis Helpline.** Call or text 988, from anywhere

>   people, and for services delivered by Indigenous responders.
> - **Kids Help Phone.** Call 1-800-668-6868, or text CONNECT to 686868.
> - **Hope for Wellness Helpline**, for First Nations, Inuit, and Métis
>   callers, with responders trained in cultural humility:
>   1-855-242-3310.
>
> Your teacher's local list is still the one to use first for anything
> to do with this school. These are for when you need somebody now.
