**Unit 2. Individual, with a short presentation to a small group. Due in
week seven.** Three to four pages plus five minutes of talking.

You pick one field of work, find out what is actually happening in it,
and connect it to what you can already do.

## Choosing a field

Pick something you are curious about rather than something you are
supposed to be curious about, and keep it narrow. "Health care" is too
wide to say anything true about. "Paramedic services in this region" is
a field you can actually investigate in two weeks.

## What to hand in

**A trends section.** Two or three changes affecting this field, of the
kinds set out in [[Work Is Changing]] — technological, economic, or
social — with what each has done to the work
itself, to how it is done, and to workers' rights or conditions. Name
where you found each one.

**An interview.** The notes and outcome from your
[[The Informational Interview]], with what the person told you that the
written sources did not.

**A skills section.** The transferable skills this work actually
demands, taken from the interview and the postings rather than from a
list. Then your own position: which of them you can evidence today, from
[[The Skills Inventory Task]], and which you would have to build.

**A personal profile page.** One page — interests, values, skills,
strengths, and what you want to develop — written so that it stands on
its own and can be reused in [[The Application]].

**Five minutes to a group of four.** What you found and what surprised
you. No slides required.

## What earns the marks

- **Real research.** Sources named, and at least one of them a person.
- **Trends connected to consequences.** Not "AI is changing everything",
  but which task changed and what happened to the people doing it.
- **Honest self-assessment.** Gaps named as clearly as strengths.
- **A usable profile.** Something you would genuinely show someone.
- **A clear five minutes.** One finding your group remembers afterwards.

> [!warning] A field with no bad news is a field you have not researched
> Every occupation has a part people leave over — hours, physical
> demands, pay that starts low, seasonal work, emotional weight. If your
> investigation found none of that, you read marketing material rather
> than sources, and the fix is to ask a person who does the work.
