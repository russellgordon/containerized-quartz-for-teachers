Most people write about themselves badly for the same reason: they think
the job is to sound impressive, so they reach for adjectives. Adjectives
are exactly what nobody believes. The fix is mechanical, and once you
have it you will never write a bad opening line again.

## Replace the adjective with the situation

The rule is one sentence long: describe what you did, and let the reader
supply the adjective. If they arrive at "reliable" on their own, they
believe it, because they worked it out.

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| "I am a hard worker" | "I opened the store at six on Saturdays for a year" |
| "I have excellent communication skills" | "I explained the new booking system to twelve volunteers" |
| "I am a natural leader" | "I ran the schedule for a team of nine, and two people asked me to do it again" |
| "I am passionate about helping people" | "I sat with the same resident every Tuesday for eight months" |
| "I am detail-oriented" | "The float balanced every shift I counted it" |

Every entry in the right column is checkable, which is the property that
makes it worth writing. Get these out of your inventory from
[[The Skills Inventory]] — that is what it is for.

## Say the ordinary thing plainly

You do not have to have done anything remarkable. Looking after a
sibling, holding down shifts around school, keeping a group chat
organised enough that a fundraiser actually happened — these are real
and they read as real. What does not read as real is the same sentence
inflated by three adjectives.

## Cut the throat-clearing

Delete the first sentence of whatever you wrote. Nine times out of ten
it was "I am writing to apply for the position advertised", which the
reader already knows. Start with the second sentence.

## When to name your circumstances

Applications sometimes ask what has shaped your choices, and scholarship
and program applications often ask directly. It is legitimate to name
the things that are not about your skills: money, distance, family
responsibilities, being the first in your family to consider this,
having to work while studying. Say them plainly, once, and connect them
to a decision you made. That is an explanation. Repeated at length,
without a decision attached, it becomes an apology, and you have nothing
to apologise for.

## Read it aloud

If you would not say the sentence out loud to an adult you respect, do
not send it. That test catches almost everything.
