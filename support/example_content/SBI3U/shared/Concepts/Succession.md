Leave bare ground alone and it does not stay bare. What arrives first,
what follows, and why the order is so predictable is one of the few
places in biology where you can watch a community assemble itself.

## Two starting points

**Primary succession** begins where there is no soil at all: bare rock
after a glacier retreats, a new lava flow, a road cut. The first
colonisers are lichens and mosses — organisms that need almost nothing
and can hold onto rock. They are doing something specific and slow:
breaking rock chemically and physically, and adding organic matter when
they die. That mixture is soil, and making it takes decades to
centuries.

**Secondary succession** begins where soil already exists but the
community has been cleared: after a fire, a windthrow, a flood, or an
abandoned field. It is far faster, because the hard part is already
done — the soil is there, and often a seed bank with it.

## The sequence, and why plants drive it

| Stage | Who arrives | What they change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pioneer | Lichens, mosses; then annual weeds | Make or stabilise soil; hold moisture |
| Early | Grasses and fast perennials | Shade the ground, add organic matter |
| Mid | Shrubs, then fast-growing trees — poplar, birch | Shade out the sun-lovers that made their arrival possible |
| Late | Shade-tolerant trees — maple, beech, hemlock | Seedlings survive under a closed canopy, so the community persists |

The engine is the same at every step: **each stage changes conditions in
a way that suits the next stage better than itself.** Poplars need full
sun and cannot regenerate in their own shade; sugar maple seedlings can.
So the forest replaces itself with a different forest, and eventually
with one that can regenerate under its own canopy.

That is why plants, rather than animals, drive succession. They alter
light, soil, moisture, and temperature — the conditions everything else
lives in — which is the same argument [[Plant Structure]] and
[[Transport in Plants]] make at the scale of one organism.

## What it means for biodiversity

Diversity is not highest at the end. It usually peaks somewhere in the
middle, where early and late species overlap and the habitat is patchy.
A mature forest can be less species-rich than the mid-successional stage
before it, and both matter.

Two consequences worth holding onto:

- **Disturbance is not the opposite of a healthy ecosystem.** Fire,
  windthrow, and flooding reset patches and keep a landscape as a mosaic
  of stages, which supports more species than any single stage would.
- **"Restoring" land means choosing a stage.** A meadow left alone
  becomes a forest; keeping it a meadow requires intervention. Neither
  is more natural — but they support different species, which is the
  decision [[Biodiversity and Why It Matters]] frames.

> [!question]- Is there really a stable end point?
> The old idea of a fixed "climax community" is out of favour. Real
> landscapes are disturbed often enough, and climate shifts fast enough,
> that most communities are somewhere on the way to somewhere. What
> survives from the idea is the useful part: the *direction* of change
> is predictable, even when the destination is not.
