Every task in this course that is not done at a bench is done from
sources, and the marks are mostly in the choosing rather than the
reading. This page is the method.

## Where to look, in order

1. **The course's own pages**, to get the vocabulary right. You cannot
   search well for something you cannot name.
2. **A textbook or a reference work**, for the settled version of an
   idea. Settled is what you want as a foundation; it is not where new
   findings live.
3. **Government and public-health bodies** — Health Canada, the Public
   Health Agency of Canada, Environment and Climate Change Canada,
   Statistics Canada, the provincial ministries. Real data, plainly
   presented, and free.
4. **Professional and scientific organisations**, and the museums and
   universities that publish for the public.
5. **The research literature itself**, through your school or public
   library's databases. Read the abstract, then the discussion, then the
   methods; the results section will make more sense afterwards.

A public library card gives you database access from home. That single
fact is worth more to a student researcher than any search technique.

## Judging what you find

Five questions, in this order — the first one that fails ends the
assessment:

| Question | What you are checking | The tell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who wrote it? | Named author or organisation with relevant expertise | No author at all |
| What is the evidence? | Data, a study, a citation — not assertion | "Studies show", with no study named |
| When? | Currency, which matters enormously in genetics and public health | No date anywhere |
| Who benefits? | What the publisher gains if you believe it | A product on the same page |
| Does anyone independent agree? | Whether it is one voice or many | Every source traces back to the same press release |

That last row catches more bad research than any other. Three sites can
look like three sources and be one, and following each back to its
origin is the check that reveals it.

**Bias is not the same as being wrong.** A pharmaceutical company's
paper on its own drug can be correct and still needs a second opinion; a
conservation group's report on a species it campaigns for can be
excellent evidence. Note the interest, look for independent
confirmation, and say in your own writing what you found.

## Recording as you go

Keep a running list from the first source, not at the end. For each one:
the author or organisation, the date, the title, where you found it, and
**one sentence in your own words** about what it gave you. That sentence
is the difference between a bibliography and a working set of notes, and
it is what stops accidental copying later — you have already written the
idea in your own language.

When you use a source, name it where you use it, not only in a list at
the end. "Health Canada's 2024 guidance says…" tells a reader what your
claim rests on at the moment they need to know.

> [!tip] The test for whether you have enough
> Can you state your conclusion in one sentence, and name which source
> supports each part of it? If a part has no source, you have found the
> next thing to look up — not the moment to write more carefully around
> the gap.
