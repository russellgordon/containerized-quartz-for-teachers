A string is a chain of related problems, served one at a time:
$2^3 \times 2^2$, then $2^5 \times 2^0$, then $2^7 \times 2^{-2}$,
then $2^{1/2} \times 2^{1/2}$. Each answer is a stepping stone to the
next — and in Grade 11, the strings are quietly rebuilding the
exponent laws from scratch.

## How we play

1. One problem at a time. Solve it in your head; thumb when ready.
2. A few people defend their methods; each goes on the board.
3. Before computing the next from scratch, ask: what can I reuse?

> [!example]- The string, walked
> - "$2^3 \times 2^2$: eight fours — no wait, eight times four, 32.
>   Which is $2^5$. The exponents added."
> - "$2^5 \times 2^0 = 32$ only works if $2^0 = 1$. Not zero — one."
> - "$2^7 \times 2^{-2}$: if the law still holds it is $2^5 = 32$
>   again, so a negative exponent must *divide*."
> - "$2^{1/2} \times 2^{1/2} = 2^1$. Whatever $2^{1/2}$ is, it
>   multiplies by itself to make 2. It is $\sqrt{2}$."
>
> Nobody was taught a rule that day. The string cornered each one,
> and [[Exponent Laws]] just wrote down what the room had proven.

## One variation

Run the string with money: $200 \times 1.05$, then
$200 \times 1.05^2$, then $200 \times 1.05^{10}$ — a balance
compounding before anyone has said the word. The last step of the
original string is the front door of [[Rational Exponents]].

> [!tip] Lazy, in the best way
> Mathematicians refuse to compute what they can deduce. If a problem
> feels brand new, look back along the string — it rarely is.
