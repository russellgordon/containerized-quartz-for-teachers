A quantity goes on the board — a stack of doublings, a balance left
to grow, the angle of the sun over the parking lot — and two
duellists commit to estimates before anyone computes. Each defends a
reason. Then the class brackets: surely too low, surely too high,
squeezed until cornered.

## How we play

1. Commit in writing first. A number without a reason scores nothing.
2. Defend: what did you compare it to, and which way did rounding push you?
3. Bracket as a class, then measure or calculate the reveal.

> [!example]- One duel: $100 invested at 8%, compounded yearly, for 50 years
> - "More than $500 — that is what plain $8-a-year interest would
>   pay, and compounding only ever beats it."
> - "Less than $10 000 — at 8% the balance doubles roughly every
>   nine years, and fifty years is only about five and a half
>   doublings."
> - "Five doublings of $100 is $3200; six is $6400. I said $4500."
> - The reveal: about $4700. The doubling argument had it cornered.

## One variation

Doubling duels: how thick is a sheet of paper folded in half twenty
times? Commit before you scoff — the estimates in the room usually
span a factor of a thousand. Your eyes get their verdict during
[[Folding Paper to the Moon]], once [[The Exponential Function]] can
compute what intuition refuses to.

> [!tip] Bracketing is a life skill
> An answer you cannot bracket is an answer you cannot check. Naming
> "too low" and "too high" first is [[Checking Your Own Work]] done
> in advance — before the mistake instead of after it. Exponential
> answers need it most, because intuition is linear and they are not.
