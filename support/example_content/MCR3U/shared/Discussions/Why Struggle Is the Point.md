If mathematics class only ever asked you to do things you already knew
how to do, it would be a photocopying service. The learning happens in
the stretch — standing at a whiteboard with your random group, holding
a Ferris wheel problem none of you can model yet. But not all struggle
is equal. *Stuck-and-thinking* means you still have a next move to
try: test a number, sketch the graph, shrink the problem to one
period. *Spinning* means repeating the last move harder and hoping.
The first builds mathematicians; the second just builds frustration —
and telling them apart from the inside is a skill this course teaches
on purpose, with [[Getting Unstuck]] as its field guide.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Recall a problem that finally gave in after real struggle. What did
   the moment of giving-in feel like — and would being handed the
   answer have produced anything like it?
2. What are your personal tells that you have slipped from
   stuck-and-thinking into spinning? Who should notice first: you,
   your group, or your teacher?
3. Why does this class put you in *random* groups at *vertical*
   whiteboards? What do standing up and a shared marker do to struggle
   that a worksheet at a desk does not?
4. The group beside yours is stuck on a problem you have cracked.
   When does helping them actually take something from them — and
   what does good help look like instead?
5. Where is the line between productive struggle and genuinely being
   out of your depth? Is admitting the second a failure, or a move?

This is why every practice set here folds its answers away and why
nothing is ever timed: the callouts protect your struggle from your
own curiosity. Attempt first, peek second — the order is the whole
point.
