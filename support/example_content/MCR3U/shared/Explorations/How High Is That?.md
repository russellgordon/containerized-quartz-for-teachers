Somewhere on school property stands something you cannot climb, touch
the top of, or lower a tape measure from: the flagpole, the gym
ceiling, the big tree by the parking lot. Your group's job is to
measure it anyway.

## The task

You get a tape measure, a protractor with a straw and a washer on a
string — a homemade clinometer — and thirty minutes outdoors. Produce
a height, in metres, by **two independent methods**, and return with
a board-ready account: a labelled diagram for each method, the
measurements you actually took, and an honest interval — *the height
is between this and that* — with your reasoning for the width. If
your two methods disagree, do not average and hide it: say which one
you trust more, and why.

> [!tip]- Facilitation notes — for the teacher
> Send groups out before teaching anything; Grade 10 trigonometry and
> proportional reasoning are both in the room already. Common second
> methods: shadows and similar triangles, a metre stick held at arm's
> length, pacing plus the clinometer from two different distances.
> The two-distances approach quietly sets up the oblique-triangle
> work to come — flag those boards for the consolidation. Expect the
> measured angle to be the weak joint: a two-degree wobble moves the
> flagpole by half a metre, which is exactly the conversation you
> want. Groups who claim centimetre precision should be asked to
> re-measure and watch their own hands.

## What mathematics tends to surface

Every method hides the same skeleton: a right triangle with a known
side and a known angle, and a ratio connecting them. The tangent
ratio does most of the work whether or not anyone says its name. The
interval question surfaces error propagation honestly — small angle
wobble, large height wobble. [[Special Angles]] then sharpens the
tool: some angles give exact answers, no calculator required.

## Where it leads

The field method breaks the moment the triangle loses its right angle
— measuring across a ravine, or a cliff from the far side of a river.
[[The Sine Law]] and [[The Cosine Law]] are built for exactly that
failure, and the two-distances trick your groups improvised is their
opening move.
