Desmos is free, runs in any browser, and is the fastest way in the
world to ask a graph a question. Treat it as a thinking tool — a place
to test conjectures — not an answer machine you obey.

## Sliders on the transformation recipe

Type $y = a \sin(k(x - d)) + c$ and Desmos offers to make $a$, $k$,
$d$, and $c$ adjustable. Before you drag anything, **predict aloud**:
what will $k$ do? Which way will the wave slide when $d$ grows? Then
slide, and watch the graph agree or object. Two minutes of sliding
builds the intuition behind [[Transformations of Functions]] faster
than an hour of hand-plotting — and when the graph surprises you (the
$(x - d)$ sign trap surprises everyone, and $k$ working *against*
instinct surprises the rest), you have found the exact edge of your
understanding. Prediction first is what keeps Desmos a thinking tool;
sliding without predicting is just television.

## A table of data and a candidate curve

Paste in a table — hours and water depths, years and balances — and
the points appear on screen. Now type your candidate model over top
and tune its parameters *by hand* until the curve deserves the data:
axis first, then amplitude, then period, as [[Sinusoidal Functions]]
recommends. Hand-tuning is the entire modelling loop of
[[The Tide Problem]] and [[Double or Nothing]] run at high speed —
and every parameter you adjust asks you what that knob *means*, which
is the standard [[What Makes a Model Good]] holds models to.

## When your head is faster

Desmos shines when the question is about *shape and behaviour*:
families of exponentials, a wave against its axis, a conjecture
checked on ten cases in ten seconds. It is slower than your head for
$2^5$, for the exact ratios of [[Special Angles]], for judging
whether an answer is the right size, and for everything
[[Estimation Duels]] trains. Reaching for a tool you do not need is
its own kind of slow — the skill is knowing which moment you are in.
