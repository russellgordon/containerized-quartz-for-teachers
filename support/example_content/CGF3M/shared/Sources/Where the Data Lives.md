Almost every number this course needs is published, free, by an agency
whose job is to collect it. Going to that agency instead of to a summary
of it is the single habit that most improves a piece of geographic
writing, because you get the definition, the units and the uncertainty
along with the figure.

| What you need | Where it actually lives |
| --- | --- |
| Weather and climate records, normals | Environment and Climate Change Canada, [climate.weather.gc.ca](https://climate.weather.gc.ca/) |
| Streamflow and water levels | Water Survey of Canada, [wateroffice.ec.gc.ca](https://wateroffice.ec.gc.ca/) |
| Great Lakes levels | Fisheries and Oceans Canada, [tides.gc.ca](https://www.tides.gc.ca/) |
| Earthquakes in Canada | Earthquakes Canada, Natural Resources Canada |
| Drought conditions | Canadian Drought Monitor, Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada |
| Wildfire extent and status | Canadian Interagency Forest Fire Centre; NRCan's national fire database |
| Disaster events and losses | [Canadian Disaster Database](https://cdd.publicsafety.gc.ca/), Public Safety Canada |
| Topography, imagery, elevation | [Toporama](https://atlas.gc.ca/toporama/en/index.html), [geo.ca](https://geo.ca/), [Ontario GeoHub](https://geohub.lio.gov.on.ca/) |
| Flood-plain and regulated areas | Your local conservation authority |
| Global climate assessment | IPCC; World Meteorological Organization |

All checked in August 2026. Agencies reorganise their websites more often
than they change their data, so if a link fails, search the agency name
and the dataset name rather than assuming the data is gone.

## Primary and secondary, and why the distinction bites

**Primary** data is the observation itself: a gauge reading, a satellite
scene, your own measurements at the shoreline. **Secondary** is somebody
else's processing of it — a published statistic, a news chart, an atlas
plate, a textbook figure. Secondary sources are not inferior; they are
further from the definition, and the definition is usually where the
argument is hiding. "Area burned" can mean the fire perimeter or the area
actually burned inside it, and those differ by a lot.

## Three questions before you use a dataset

**What exactly was measured, and where?** A station 40 km away in a
different landform is not your site. **Over what period, and is it
complete?** Gaps and station moves create fake trends. **Has it been
revised?** Preliminary figures for a current fire season or a recent
disaster are revised upward and downward for months afterwards, so cite
the figure *and* the date you retrieved it.

One more thing to notice about that table: every row is somebody's job.
Hydrologists, seismologists, meteorologists, fire behaviour specialists,
GIS analysts and agrologists produce these datasets, and most of them
entered through a geography or earth-science background —
[[Skills You Are Building]].

Judging who published it is [[Judging a Source]]; working the climate
record specifically is [[Reading Climate Data]].
