Everything you find was made by somebody, for a reason, at a moment, with
money from somewhere. Four questions — **who, why, when, who paid** —
sort almost anything, and none of them requires you to already know the
science.

None of the four tests truth. A reinsurance company's catastrophe report
can be entirely accurate; a government page can be badly out of date.
What the questions tell you is what a source is *for*, and therefore what
it is likely to leave out.

## Four questions on four real kinds of thing

| The artefact | Who and why | What it will not tell you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A federal agency's dataset | A monitoring body publishing its own observations | The limits of its station network, unless you open the metadata |
| A peer-reviewed paper | Researchers, reviewed by others in the field | Whether later work overturned it |
| An insurer's loss estimate | A firm quantifying its own exposure | Everything that was uninsured |
| A news story on a disaster | A journalist on a deadline, early in the event | Revised counts, which almost always change |

## The failure that catches everybody

The commonest error is not believing something false. It is **believing
something that was true.** Casualty counts, area burned and cost
estimates for a recent event are provisional and get revised for months.
A figure without a retrieval date is not a figure you can defend.

The second commonest is treating a number as more certain than the body
publishing it does. If the agency writes "1.7 °C, likely range 1.1 to
2.3", the range is part of the finding and dropping it misrepresents the
source. Where two authorities differ, name both and say which you are
using and why — that is stronger writing than pretending there is one
number.

## Where this course sets a higher bar

**Indigenous nations' positions** come from the nation's own published
statements, not from a summary, a government page, or a news paraphrase.
Nations differ from one another, and a position can be more layered than
"for" or "against".

**Text produced by generative artificial intelligence** has no author, no
funder, and no date of observation, and it will state figures fluently
without having checked them. It can help you find a source; it is never
the source. If you cannot trace a claim to something published with an
author and a year, you do not have it yet.

## The habit, and the format

Before a fact goes into your work, write the author or agency, the title,
the publisher, the date of publication, and the date you retrieved it.
This course accepts any consistent accepted style — author–date, notes,
or a numbered reference list — provided a reader could find the source
again from what you wrote. Then ask the last question: **would somebody
who disagrees with me accept this source?**
