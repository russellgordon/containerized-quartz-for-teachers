Climate data is generous and easy to misuse, because a long record can be
sliced to say almost anything. This page is about the slicing.

## Getting the record

Environment and Climate Change Canada's historical data search returns
daily, monthly and hourly observations by station, downloadable as CSV,
plus the **climate normals** — thirty-year averages over 1991–2020,
computed for stations with at least fifteen years of data in that window.
Normals are the comparison; the daily record is the evidence.

Pick your station carefully. Elevation, distance from water and land
cover all shift the numbers, and an airport station on open ground is not
the same environment as a wooded valley 20 km away. Check the station's
period of record before you use it: a station that moved or closed
produces a step in the series that looks exactly like a trend.

## Building a climograph, and reading one

Monthly mean temperature as a line, monthly total precipitation as bars,
both on the same twelve-month axis. What to read off it, in order: the
annual temperature range, which tells you continentality; the timing of
the precipitation maximum, which tells you the circulation regime; and
whether any month is dry enough to limit growth.

Two rules keep the comparison fair when you put two stations side by
side. Use the **same vertical scales** on both, or the eye will invent a
difference. Use the **same period**, or you are comparing eras rather
than places.

## Anomalies, trends, and honest claims

An **anomaly** is the departure from a stated baseline, and it is usually
more informative than the raw value because it removes the station's own
character. Always name the baseline: 1991–2020, 1961–1990 and 1850–1900
are all in common use and give different numbers for the same year.

A trend needs a period long enough to exceed the noise. Choosing a hot
year as your start point and a cool year as your end point is
cherry-picking, and it is the commonest form of dishonesty in climate
argument in either direction. State the period, plot every year in it,
and let the reader see the scatter.

> [!question] What would a single record-breaking day prove?
> On its own, very little. Records are broken in every long series by
> chance. What is diagnostic is the **ratio** of hot records to cold
> records over decades, and the shift of the whole distribution. Write
> that sentence into any argument built on one extreme day — including
> the ones in [[Drought and Heat]] and [[A Warming Planet]].
