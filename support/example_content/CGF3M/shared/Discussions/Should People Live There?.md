People farm the slopes of active volcanoes, build cities on floodplains
and deltas, and keep rebuilding on coasts that are visibly retreating.
The lazy reading is that they do not understand the risk. Almost always
they understand it perfectly and have made a trade you have not priced.

## Why anyone lives in a hazardous place

The land is productive, or the harbour is sheltered, or the water is
there, or the work is there. Family, language, burial grounds and treaty
territory are there. Moving costs money the household does not have, and
the safer place is more expensive precisely because it is safer. The
hazard is rare and the rent is due monthly. And for a great many people
worldwide, there is nowhere else that is both affordable and available.

Every one of those is a reason, and only the last is powerlessness. A
paper that treats residents as ignorant has skipped the analysis.

## The positions

The **resident** weighs a known risk against a known life. The **planner**
holds a mandate to keep new development out of mapped hazard and no
mandate to move what is already there. The **conservation authority**
maps the hazard and restricts building in it, which lowers the value of
somebody's land. The **developer** has a legal use for legally zoned
land and a queue of buyers. The **insurer** withdraws or reprices. The
**taxpayer** funds the road, the pumping station and the eventual
recovery. The **First Nation** whose territory this is may hold rights,
title and a relationship to the place that pre-date every other party in
this list and is not represented by any of them.

> [!example] Two cases to hold side by side
> A subdivision proposed today on a mapped floodplain, and a shoreline
> community that has been there for generations while the lake rose and
> fell through a metre and a half — [[The Great Lakes Water Level Cycle]].
> The same words, "should people live there", mean something very
> different in each. Any principle you propose has to survive both.

## Preparation

Come with a position and the evidence for it. Come also with the strongest
version of the opposite case — you will be asked to state it before you
are allowed to argue against it. Useful groundwork:
[[Hazard, Exposure, Vulnerability]] for the vocabulary,
[[Using the Physical Environment]] for the benefits column, and
[[Mapping a Hazard]] for what a hazard line does and does not mean.

Then the harder follow-up: if the answer is no, who moves, who decides,
and who pays — [[Who Should Pay to Rebuild?]].
