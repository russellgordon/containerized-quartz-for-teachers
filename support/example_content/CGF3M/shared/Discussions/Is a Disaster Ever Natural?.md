Many hazard researchers now avoid the phrase "natural disaster"
altogether. Their argument is short: the hazard is natural, the disaster
is what happens when that hazard meets choices about where people live,
how they build, what they are warned about, and who can afford to leave.
On that reading, calling the outcome natural quietly excuses the choices.

This is a seminar rather than a lecture, because the argument is not
settled and the counter-case is serious.

## The case that the word should go

Two earthquakes of similar magnitude can produce death tolls that differ
by orders of magnitude, and the difference is building standards,
enforcement, income and warning — all human. The same storm that damages
basements in one town destroys a community in another. If the physical
input is constant and the outcome varies enormously, the variable is not
nature.

The word also does political work. "Natural disaster" implies nobody is
answerable, which is convenient for whoever approved the subdivision, cut
the maintenance budget, or declined to map the hazard.

## The case that the word is doing something useful

Some events genuinely overwhelm any preparation a society could
reasonably have made. Distinguishing a hazard that originates in the
physical environment from one that originates in a human failure —
[[Human Impact on Physical Systems]] treats both — is a real distinction
that emergency management and insurance both need to make.

There is also a risk of the reverse error. Insisting that every disaster
is a human failure can shade into blaming the people who were hit,
particularly poorer communities that had the least power over the
decisions being criticised.

## Where the middle ground actually is

Most researchers who reject the phrase are not denying the physics. They
are relocating the question from "what happened to them" to "what
decisions produced this outcome, and who made them". That is a shift in
where you point the analysis, and you can accept it without pretending
tectonics is a social construct.

> [!abstract] What the seminar has to produce
> Not a vote. By the end you should be able to state, in one sentence
> each: the strongest version of both cases; one real event that fits
> each; and what you would call the thing instead, if anything. Bring
> the events from [[The Disaster Case]] and be ready to be asked which
> part of the outcome was physical and which part was decided.

Groundwork: [[Hazard, Exposure, Vulnerability]] for the vocabulary, and
the official reporting on your own case study — read how the responsible
agency itself describes the causes, and notice which of them it treats as
avoidable.
