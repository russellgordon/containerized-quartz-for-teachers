A flood takes forty houses. Some were insured, some were not, and some
were built lawfully in a place that is now mapped as hazardous. Rebuilding
costs more than anyone in the room has. The seminar question is not
whether the loss is sad; it is who owes what to whom, and why.

## The positions, held by real people

**The owner** bought a house that was legal to build and legal to sell,
paid taxes for decades, and had no way to price a risk nobody disclosed.

**The insurer** priced a policy on stated risks. Overland flood coverage
is a relatively recent and unevenly available product in Canada, and a
premium that reflects true flood risk in a high-hazard location is
unaffordable by design — which is the market saying something.

**The municipality** approved the subdivision, maintains the road and the
sewer, and collects the taxes. It also has a shrinking budget and other
neighbourhoods.

**The province and the federal government** run disaster assistance
programmes that pay for uninsurable losses after the fact, funded by
everyone.

**The neighbour on higher ground** paid the same taxes, faces none of the
hazard, and is being asked to fund a rebuild in a place they would not
have chosen. **A downstream community** may have taken the water
precisely because upstream development sent it there faster.

## The argument underneath

Paying to rebuild in place restores a community and preserves the
exposure. Paying to relocate ends the exposure and ends the community as
it was, and costs more today than anyone wants to spend. Paying nothing
transfers the whole loss to whoever is least able to move — which is a
decision, not the absence of one.

> [!question] Four to argue in the seminar
> 1. Does knowing about a hazard change what you owe someone who is
>    harmed by it? Does it change what they owe themselves?
> 2. Should public disaster assistance be available a second time for
>    the same property in the same place?
> 3. Who should have told the buyer, and what should the disclosure
>    have said?
> 4. If a buyout is offered and refused, what happens next time?

## Preparation

Bring one real Canadian example with a source and a figure — the
Canadian Disaster Database and Insurance Bureau of Canada are starting
points from [[Where the Data Lives]]. Bring the distinction from
[[Hazard, Exposure, Vulnerability]], because most of the disagreement
in this room will turn out to be about vulnerability. Be ready to argue
a position that is not yours; you will be asked.

The related question of whether anyone should have been there is
