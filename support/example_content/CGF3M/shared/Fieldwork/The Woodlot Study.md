The woodlot is a remnant of the forest that covered this part of the
Mixedwood Plains before it was cleared, and it is the best place within
walking distance to see soil, slope and microclimate behaving as one
system. Small distances here produce large differences, which is exactly
what makes it worth measuring.

Read [[Fieldwork Safety]] first — ticks, uneven ground, and low branches.

## Three transects, one gradient

We work a transect from the open edge into the interior, and a second one
down a slope. At stations along each, in the same order every time:

1. **Soil.** A hand auger or a small pit to about 30 cm. Record the
   horizons you can see, their depth, colour and texture, and how deep
   the organic layer runs — the vocabulary is in [[Soils]].
2. **Slope and aspect.** Gradient with a clinometer or a phone
   inclinometer, and the compass direction the slope faces.
3. **Microclimate.** Air temperature at 1.5 m, surface temperature,
   relative humidity, light reaching the floor, and wind — all at the
   same moment at each station, or the comparison is worthless.
4. **Vegetation.** Canopy species and rough cover, understorey, ground
   layer, and depth of leaf litter.
5. **Evidence of process.** Rills, exposed roots on a slope, deposition
   at a slope base, windthrow, standing water, animal disturbance.

## Why the same day and the same hour matters

Microclimate readings are comparisons, not absolutes. A reading in the
interior at two o'clock and one at the edge at three o'clock differ for
reasons that have nothing to do with the woodlot. Take paired readings,
note the time on every one, and repeat a station at the end to see how
much the whole site drifted while you worked.

> [!note] What we expect, and why we might be wrong
> Interior stations are usually cooler by day, warmer at night, more
> humid, less windy, and darker than edge stations, because canopy
> intercepts radiation and slows air. North-facing slopes here are
> cooler and moister than south-facing ones. Write your prediction
> before the first reading. If the data disagree, the interesting work
> starts — look for the edge effect, the gap, the drainage line, or the
> instrument error.

## What it feeds

The soil and slope data support [[The Process Study]]; the microclimate
data give you a local, measured example of [[Climate and Its Controls]]
operating at a scale of metres; and the vegetation pattern tests the
biome claim in [[Ecosystems and Biomes]]. The management question — how a
small woodlot is kept functioning at all — is taken up in
[[Renewing What Is Damaged]].
