A shoreline is where you can watch physical geography happen at a rate
you can measure in an afternoon. Waves supply energy, that energy moves
material, and the shape you record today is the current position of a
process rather than a permanent feature.

Read [[Fieldwork Safety]] first. You do not enter the water, and you stay
back from undercut banks.

## What we do

Working in pairs along a marked stretch:

1. **Survey a profile.** A side-on section from the water's edge up to
   the vegetation line, with measured distances and height changes. Mark
   every point where the surface material changes.
2. **Describe the material.** At three stations, record composition —
   sand, gravel, cobble, boulder, bedrock, fill — with a size range for
   the dominant fraction, and note roundness and sorting. Those two
   properties are your evidence about transport, per
   [[Weathering, Erosion, and Deposition]].
3. **Find evidence of movement.** Undercut banks, exposed roots, toppled
   trees, debris lines from high water, sediment accumulating against an
   obstruction, and any armouring and what is happening in front of it.
4. **Record the energy setting.** Which way does this shore face? What is
   the **fetch** — the distance of open water the wind crosses to reach
   here — in the direction of the prevailing wind?
5. **Photograph with purpose.** Note where you stood, which way you
   faced, and put something of known size in the frame.

## Observation on the left, inference on the right

Two columns, always. "Tree roots exposed 30 cm above the present surface"
belongs on the left. "The bank has retreated" belongs on the right. Marks
in this course come from keeping them apart, and from noticing when a
single observation supports more than one inference.

> [!tip] A rough number beats no number
> Paces are a measurement if you know your pace length. A photograph
> with a metre rule in it is a measurement. Precision matters less than
> having something you can compare against in January, when we come
> back and measure the same stretch again.

## Questions we return to

- What process dominates here — removal, transport, or deposition?
- Where did this material come from, and where is it going next?
- What has been built to stop it, and what is that doing downdrift?
- What would this shore look like after the largest storm on record?

We return in Unit 4 with the hazard question rather than the process
question, so keep this notebook — see [[Your Field Notebook]].
