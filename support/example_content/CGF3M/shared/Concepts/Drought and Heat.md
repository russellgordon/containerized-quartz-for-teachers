Drought is the hazard with no moment. There is no sound, no boundary, and
no obvious day on which it begins — which is exactly why it is so often
under-prepared for and so hard to declare over.

## Four droughts, one word

*Meteorological* drought is a precipitation deficit against the local
normal. *Agricultural* drought is a soil-moisture deficit at the stage a
crop needs it, which can occur in a season with average total rainfall
if the rain came at the wrong time. *Hydrological* drought is low
streamflow, lake level and ground water, and lags the rainfall deficit by
months. *Socio-economic* drought is when demand cannot be met. A region
can be in one and not another, so always say which you mean.

Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada publishes the **Canadian Drought
Monitor** monthly, rating areas D0 to D4:

| Rating | Category | Roughly how often |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D0 | Abnormally dry | Once in 3–5 years |
| D1 | Moderate drought | Once in 5–10 years |
| D2 | Severe drought | Once in 10–20 years |
| D3 | Extreme drought | Once in 20–25 years |
| D4 | Exceptional drought | Once in 50 years |

Those return periods are the Monitor's own framing, and they are
statements about frequency, not about severity of consequence. A D2 in a
region with reservoirs and irrigation is not the same event as a D2 where
there are none.

## Heat is the deadlier half

Extreme heat kills more people in Canada than any other weather hazard,
and it does so invisibly — the deaths appear in coroners' statistics
weeks later rather than on the news that afternoon. Vulnerability is
concentrated in older adults, people living alone, people with chronic
illness, and people in top-floor units without cooling. Cities amplify
the hazard through the **urban heat island**: dark, dry, hard surfaces
absorb and re-radiate heat, and the effect is strongest overnight, which
removes the recovery period the body needs.

Heat and drought reinforce each other. Dry soil cannot evaporate water,
so more of the incoming energy goes into heating the air instead, which
dries the soil further. That feedback is why heat waves are hotter in
droughts, and why [[Wildfire]] seasons and drought years coincide.

The June 2021 heat dome over western North America is the Canadian case.
Environment and Climate Change Canada recorded **49.6 °C at Lytton,
British Columbia on 29 June 2021**, a national record broken on three
consecutive days, and calls the heat wave the deadliest weather event in
Canadian history. The BC Coroners Service later reviewed **619
heat-related deaths** from 25 June to 1 July — 98 per cent of them
indoors, two-thirds of the dead aged over seventy, and more than half
living alone. That count was published nearly a year afterwards.

Read those two facts together. The temperature was known within the hour;
the toll took months, and it was concentrated exactly where
vulnerability predicts. Longer, hotter dry spells are among the
better-supported projections in [[A Warming Planet]], and what that same
summer did to a whole region's harvest is [[The 2021 Prairie Drought]].
