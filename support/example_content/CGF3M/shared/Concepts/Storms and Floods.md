Storms are the atmosphere moving energy around, and floods are what
happens when the water they deliver arrives faster than the ground and
the channel can take it. The two are usually studied apart and almost
always occur together.

## What makes a storm severe

Three ingredients, in every case: moisture, instability, and something to
lift the air. Warm moist air rises, cools, condenses, and the
condensation itself releases heat that drives the rise further. Add
**wind shear** — wind changing speed or direction with height — and a
thunderstorm can organise into a rotating supercell that lasts hours
instead of minutes and can produce a tornado.

Tropical cyclones need the same fuel on a vastly larger scale: sea
surface temperatures around 26–27 °C through a deep layer, low shear,
and enough distance from the equator for the Coriolis effect to spin
the system. They weaken over land or cool water because you have removed
the fuel. Reaching Canada, a hurricane usually becomes **post-tropical**
— it draws energy from temperature contrast rather than from warm water —
and post-tropical does not mean weakened, as
[[Hurricane Fiona in Atlantic Canada]] shows.

Canada rates tornadoes on the **Enhanced Fujita scale**, adopted here in
April 2013; events up to 2012 keep their original F ratings, so a
long-run tornado count mixes two scales and you must say so when you use
one. Western University's Northern Tornadoes Project now investigates
events in areas where nobody used to be looking, which raises the counted
number without necessarily raising the real one — a detection artefact
worth naming in any trend claim.

## Four ways to flood

Riverine flooding, when a channel receives more than it can carry.
Flash flooding, when intense rain on steep or sealed ground concentrates
in minutes. Urban or pluvial flooding, when the drainage system is
exceeded and water never reaches a river at all. Coastal flooding, when
wind and low pressure pile water against a shore as a **storm surge**.
Add a fifth for Canada: **ice-jam flooding**, when breaking ice dams a
river and the water backs up behind it, which is why spring flood
warnings here are not simply about snowmelt volume.

> [!warning] A watch is not a warning
> Environment and Climate Change Canada issues a **watch** when
> conditions are favourable for severe weather, and a **warning** when
> it is occurring or imminent. Tornado warnings and some severe
> thunderstorm warnings also go out through Alert Ready to phones,
> radio and television. Knowing which word you just heard decides
> whether you are watching the sky or already moving.
