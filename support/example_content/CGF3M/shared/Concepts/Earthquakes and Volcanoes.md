Both are the surface expression of processes at plate boundaries, and
both are mapped by Natural Resources Canada's Earthquakes Canada
programme for this country. Their hazards behave very differently: an
earthquake gives no warning and lasts under a minute, while most
volcanoes give weeks of warning and stay dangerous for years.

## Magnitude is not intensity

| | Magnitude | Intensity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measures | Energy released at the source | Shaking felt at one place |
| Scale used | Moment magnitude (**M**) | Modified Mercalli, I to XII |
| Number per event | One | A different value at every location |
| Depends on | The rupture | Distance, depth, and the ground beneath you |

Moment magnitude is logarithmic: each whole step is about 32 times more
energy released. That is why a M6 and a M7 are not neighbours. The
Richter scale still named in older texts and in the curriculum's own
examples is a different, earlier measure that saturates for large
events; seismologists report moment magnitude instead, and reputable
agencies publish it as **M**.

The intensity column is where geography enters. Soft, water-saturated
sediment amplifies shaking and can **liquefy**, losing strength entirely
and letting buildings settle or tip while remaining intact. Two
identical buildings a kilometre apart can therefore have opposite
outcomes, decided by what they were founded on.

## What a volcano actually does

Lava flows are the least of it — they are slow and rarely kill. The
killers are **pyroclastic density currents** of hot gas and ash moving
faster than a car, **lahars** of ash mobilised by rain or melted snow
that travel down valleys long after an eruption, ash fall that collapses
roofs and stops aircraft, and gas. Eruption style depends on magma
chemistry: silica-rich magma is viscous, traps gas, and explodes;
silica-poor basalt runs.

> [!note] The bargain volcanoes offer
> Volcanic ash and lava weather into some of the most fertile soils on
> Earth, and geothermal fields provide heat and electricity. That is why
> the slopes of active volcanoes are farmed and settled — a cost against
> a benefit, not an irrational choice. [[Using the Physical Environment]]
> takes that trade seriously; [[Should People Live There?]] argues it.

Ontario is intraplate and has no volcanoes, but it does have earthquakes:
the Western Quebec seismic zone and the Charlevoix zone are monitored
continuously by the Canadian National Seismograph Network, and Ontario's
building code carries seismic provisions for that reason. Search the
national record yourself through [[Hazard and Disaster Records]].
