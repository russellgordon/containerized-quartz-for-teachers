The physical environment repairs itself, on its own schedule, without
being asked. Understanding that schedule is what separates a claim that
something is renewable from a wish that it were.

## Natural renewal has a clock

Leaf litter becomes soil over decades to centuries. Silt eroded from one
place is deposited, buried, compressed and cemented into sedimentary rock
over millions of years. The carbon, nitrogen and water cycles move matter
between living and non-living parts of the environment continuously, and
those cycles are why life has continued for billions of years on a planet
with a fixed stock of atoms.

So "renewable" is really a statement about rate. A forest is renewable at
the pace it grows; harvest faster and it is not. Ground water is
renewable at the rate it recharges; pump faster and you are mining it.
Minerals do form naturally, over spans so long that treating them as
non-renewable is the honest accounting. The test is always the same: is
the rate of use above or below the rate of replacement, and over what
period are you measuring?

> [!success]- Is fire damage or renewal? (click to expand)
> Both, and the distinction is intensity and frequency. A low-intensity
> fire at intervals the ecosystem evolved with recycles nutrients, opens
> serotinous cones and resets succession. A very hot fire outside that
> range can sterilise soil, kill seed banks and let a different community
> take over. Same process, different rate — the same test as above.
> See [[Wildfire]].

## Rehabilitation is not restoration

Industry-led repair comes in grades, and the words are not
interchangeable. *Reclamation* makes a site safe and stable. *Rehabilitation*
returns it to some productive use, not necessarily the original one.
*Restoration* returns the original ecosystem, and is rare, slow and
expensive. A closed open-pit mine that becomes a stable lake with
controlled water chemistry has been rehabilitated; it has not been
restored.

Assessing any of these requires criteria set in advance. Useful ones:
does water leaving the site meet standards, is the landform stable
against erosion, has native vegetation established without continuing
intervention, and who holds the liability in fifty years?

Individual choices sit at the other end of the same scale — consumption,
reuse, waste, and what you put down a drain — and they are small
individually and not zero in aggregate. Both ends of that scale get
argued in [[The Stewardship Argument]], and the national and
international layer of rules appears in [[Sharing a Watershed]].
