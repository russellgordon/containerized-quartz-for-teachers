Plate tectonics is the single idea that makes the distribution of
mountains, volcanoes, earthquakes and ocean trenches stop looking
random. Rigid plates of lithosphere move over a weaker layer beneath,
and almost everything dramatic happens at their edges.

## Three kinds of edge, three kinds of landscape

| Boundary | Motion | What it builds | Where to see it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Divergent | Plates move apart | Mid-ocean ridge, rift valley, basalt | Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East African Rift |
| Convergent | Plates move together | Trench, volcanic arc, fold mountains | Cascadia, the Andes, the Himalaya |
| Transform | Plates slide past | Offset streams, fault scarps, no volcano | San Andreas, Queen Charlotte Fault |

Convergence has sub-cases worth knowing. Ocean crust is denser than
continental crust, so where they meet the ocean plate **subducts**;
water carried down with it lowers the melting point of the mantle above,
and the melt rises to build a line of volcanoes parallel to the trench.
Where two continents converge neither subducts easily, so the crust
thickens and folds instead — which is why the Himalaya has earthquakes
and very few volcanoes.

## Why the theory was accepted

Continental drift was proposed long before it was believed, because it
had no mechanism. What changed the argument was sea-floor evidence:
magnetic stripes on either side of the mid-ocean ridges recording
reversals of Earth's magnetic field in matching, mirrored patterns, and
ocean-floor ages that increase outward from the ridge in both
directions. Add earthquake foci that deepen along a plane sloping away
from a trench and you have a mechanism, a prediction, and a test.

> [!question] What would falsify it?
> A useful habit for the whole course. If sea-floor ages were random, or
> if deep earthquakes occurred uniformly under continents, the theory
> would be in serious trouble. Naming what would break an explanation is
> how you tell an explanation from a story.

Ontario sits well inside a plate, which is why our geology is old and
our earthquakes are modest — but "inside a plate" does not mean
aseismic, as [[Earthquakes and Volcanoes]] takes up. What the plates
built here was later ground down and buried by ice, and the results are
what you will read in [[Reading a Landscape]].
