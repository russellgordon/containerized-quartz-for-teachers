Modifying a landscape is a physical act with physical consequences, and
those consequences are usually felt somewhere other than where the change
was made. That displacement is the reason so many environmental disputes
are really arguments about geography.

## Six modifications and what each sets off

| Modification | Immediate effect | What follows, elsewhere or later |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paving and roofing | Water cannot infiltrate | Higher, faster flood peaks downstream |
| Damming a river | Water stored, flow regulated | Sediment trapped, delta starved, fish blocked |
| Draining wetlands | Land made farmable or buildable | Flood storage lost, water quality falls |
| Clearing forest on slopes | Roots gone, soil exposed | Landslides, gullying, sediment in streams |
| Straightening a channel | Water leaves faster here | It arrives faster somewhere downstream |
| Mining and quarrying | Rock removed, water table cut | Acid drainage, subsidence, altered drainage |

Read the right-hand column as the point of the table. Every one of these
is done for a reason that made sense to somebody; every one exports part
of its cost to a person or a place that did not decide.

## When modification makes a disaster worse

The curriculum's own examples are worth taking seriously because each is
documented. Coal mining inside Turtle Mountain is understood to have
contributed to the Frank Slide in Alberta in 1903. Land reclaimed and
filled in Kobe harbour liquefied during the 1995 Great Hanshin
earthquake, damaging structures that stood on it. Levees at New Orleans
failed during Hurricane Katrina in 2005, and much of the flooding was a
consequence of that failure rather than of the storm surge alone.

The pattern is consistent: a modification that reduces risk under normal
conditions can increase it at the extreme, because it moves people and
value into a place they would otherwise not have been. Engineers call
this the levee effect, and it is one of the few genuinely
counter-intuitive findings in hazard geography.

## Physical processes and human disasters

The arrow also runs the other way. Where a human disaster releases
something — oil, radioactive material, smoke — winds, currents and
precipitation decide who receives it. Predicting the affected area is a
physical-geography problem before it is an emergency-management one,
which is why the culminating [[The Local Hazard Assessment]] asks you to
map it.
