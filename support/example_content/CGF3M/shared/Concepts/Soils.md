Soil is where the four spheres are least separable. It is broken rock
(lithosphere), water and air in the pore spaces (hydrosphere and
atmosphere), and the living and dead remains of organisms (biosphere) —
mixed, layered, and slowly changing. It also takes centuries to build
and an afternoon to lose, which is the fact the rest of this page is
really about.

## Reading a profile

Dig a pit and the layers appear as **horizons**. The Canadian System of
Soil Classification names them from the top: an **O** horizon of organic
litter where one exists, an **A** horizon darkened by humus and depleted
by water moving downward, a **B** horizon enriched by what that water
carried down, and a **C** horizon of barely altered parent material.[^cssc]

[^cssc]: The Canadian System of Soil Classification is maintained by
Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada and sorts Canadian soils into orders
such as Luvisolic, Podzolic, Brunisolic and Gleysolic. Melanic Brunisols
are common in southern Ontario and Québec. Use the national system, not
an American textbook's, when you name an Ontario soil.

Five factors decide which profile you get: parent material, climate,
organisms, relief, and time. Change any one and the soil changes. That
is why the woodlot's soil differs from the field beside it despite
identical bedrock — the organisms and the land management differ, and a
century is long enough for that to show.

## Texture, structure, and why farmers care

Texture is the proportion of sand, silt and clay, and you can estimate it
between your fingers: sand grates, silt feels like flour, clay ribbons
when moist. Texture sets drainage, water-holding capacity, and how easily
roots push through. Structure — how those particles clump into aggregates
— sets how easily water infiltrates rather than running off, and it is
the property that compaction from machinery destroys first.

Soil loss is erosion by the definition in
[[Weathering, Erosion, and Deposition]], only faster than it forms. The
practices that slow it — leaving residue on the surface, planting cover
crops, keeping a vegetated strip between field and watercourse, working
across a slope instead of down it — are things you can look for directly
on [[The Farm Visit]], and assess as rehabilitation in
[[Renewing What Is Damaged]].
