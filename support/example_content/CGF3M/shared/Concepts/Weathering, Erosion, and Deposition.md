Three words that students use interchangeably and geographers do not.
**Weathering** breaks rock down in place. **Erosion** picks the pieces up
and moves them. **Deposition** sets them down again. Keeping them
separate lets you say where in the sequence a landform sits.

## Breaking down in place

*Physical* weathering makes smaller pieces of the same material. Water
enters a crack, freezes, expands by roughly nine per cent, and levers the
crack wider; repeat that through a Canadian spring and the rock fails
along its own weaknesses. *Chemical* weathering changes the material
itself — carbonic acid in rainwater dissolves limestone, oxygen rusts
iron-bearing minerals, and water reacts with feldspar to make clay.
*Biological* weathering is roots and lichens and burrowing animals doing
both jobs at once.

Climate decides which dominates. Freeze–thaw needs temperatures crossing
zero repeatedly with water present, which describes southern Ontario in
March almost perfectly. Chemical weathering is faster where it is warm
and wet, which is why tropical soils are deep and Shield soils are thin.

## Moving and dropping

Every erosional agent — running water, waves, ice, wind — carries
material only while it has the energy to. Slow it down and it drops the
coarsest load first, then finer, then finest. That single rule explains
graded beds in a river bank, the sorting of a beach, and why a delta has
sand at its head and clay at its toe.

Glacial ice is the exception that proves it useful: ice does not sort,
because it is not a fluid. Till dumped directly from ice is a jumble of
clay and boulders together, while material carried away by meltwater
*is* sorted. Telling till from outwash in a road cut is therefore a
statement about which agent last touched the sediment.
