Every province and territory burned in 2023, at the same time, for
months. The season is the clearest recent case of a hazard that was not
unusual in kind — Canada's forests have always burned — and was far
outside the normal range in scale.

## The national figures

Natural Resources Canada states that in 2023, "Over 6,800 wildland fires
burned more than 14.6 million hectares (ha) of Canada's forest area", and
that "Over 230,000 persons were evacuated because of potential dangers to
life and health".[^nrcan]

[^nrcan]: Natural Resources Canada, *Forest fires*, page last modified
10 July 2026, retrieved August 2026. Area-burned figures for any recent
season are revised as mapping is completed, so quote the retrieval date —
[[Hazard and Disaster Records]].

Set that against the same agency's stated normal: "Over 8,000 wildland
fires occur annually and burn an average of more than 2.1 million ha". On
NRCan's own baseline, 2023 burned roughly **seven times the average area
with fewer fires than usual**. The number of ignitions was not the
anomaly. What each ignition was able to do was.

> [!warning] Two official area figures, and neither is an error
> The Canadian Interagency Forest Fire Centre gives a final 2023 total of
> **17,203,625 ha** — about 2.6 million more than NRCan. CIFFC totals the
> **fire perimeters** agencies report, which enclose lakes and unburned
> islands. NRCan's National Burned Area Composite is **satellite-mapped
> burned area** with most of those unburned patches removed, and its
> 14.6 million is restricted to **forest**. Different definitions,
> different jobs, both correct. Say which one you used. And retire the
> 18.4 million hectare figure still circulating: that was a mid-season
> operational estimate, superseded by both bodies' final numbers.

## Why the fires got so large

Fuel, weather and topography set fire behaviour, per [[Wildfire]]. In
2023 the fuel side was primed by an unusually warm, dry spring across a
very wide area, so fires were burning in May in regions that normally do
not carry fire until July. A long, hot, dry summer kept fuel moisture low
for months rather than weeks.

Two consequences followed from the scale rather than from any one fire.
First, **simultaneity broke the sharing system**: Canadian agencies
normally lend crews and aircraft to whichever province is worst hit, and
when every jurisdiction is committed at once that mutual aid has nothing
to lend, which is why international crews were brought in. Second, smoke
travelled far beyond the fires, degrading air quality across large parts
of Canada and the United States and turning a forestry event into a
public-health event for tens of millions of people who never saw a flame.

## What it teaches about the vocabulary

Evacuation is a preparedness success and a disaster impact at the same
time. Canadian Forest Service researchers put the season at approximately
232,000 evacuees across 282 separate evacuation events — the most of any
fire season since records began in 1980. That the warning system worked
and that 232,000 people were displaced are the same fact. In the
Northwest Territories roughly 70 per cent of the territory's population
left, including all three of its largest communities. The people
displaced most often and for longest are in remote and northern
communities, including many First Nations communities, where there is one
road or no road — the vulnerability argument in
[[Hazard, Exposure, Vulnerability]] in its starkest Canadian form.

Eight people working on wildfires in Canada died during the season,
according to CIFFC and to the Canadian Forest Service. No official
consolidated list of their names has been published.

Longer, hotter fire seasons are among the better-supported projections in
[[A Warming Planet]]. Say that as a statement about probability and
season length, with a source, rather than attributing this particular
season to a cause. Trace the burn scars yourself in
[[Remote Sensing and Imagery]].
