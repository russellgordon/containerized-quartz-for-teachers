In late June 2013 a storm system stalled against the eastern slopes of
the Rockies and rained on the headwaters of four rivers at once. What
followed is the event that made flood risk a mainstream political
question in Canada, and it is unusually well documented — which makes it
a good place to practise reading a record rather than a headline.

## The record, as Public Safety Canada holds it

The Canadian Disaster Database entry for this event, retrieved from the
federal open-government portal in August 2026, reads:

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Event | Flood, Southern Alberta, 19–28 June 2013 |
| Dead | 4 |
| Injured | 0 |
| Evacuated | 100,000 |
| Total cost | \$2,715,742,000 |
| Federal disaster assistance payments | \$1,015,742,000 |
| Insurance payments | \$1,700,000,000 |

That record was last updated in September 2013, and it shows. The
Government of Alberta's own review refers to "the tragic deaths of five
Albertans" — the fifth was confirmed after the federal record was
written. **Use five, and cite the province.** A database entry is a
snapshot of what was known on the day it was edited, which is why
[[Judging a Source]] tells you to find the date every time.

The database's narrative adds the detail: twenty-nine local states of
emergency; the Bow and Elbow flooding in Calgary and forcing 75,000
residents out; 4,000 businesses affected and 3,000 buildings flooded,
including the Saddledome and the Calgary Zoo; High River and Canmore also
badly hit. It records the largest evacuation for a natural disaster in
Alberta's history to that date, and a \$550 million reduction in southern
Alberta's gross domestic product in 2013 dollars.

## Why the water arrived all at once

Environment and Climate Change Canada's account is that an intense,
slow-moving moist upper low "stalled and sat over the mountains for days
due to a massive high-pressure ridge to the north that blocked it from
moving east and pinched it up against the Rocky Mountains". Air forced up
the eastern slopes cooled and dropped its water — orographic
precipitation, as in [[Climate and Its Controls]] — for three days over
the same catchments. The storm maximum was 345 mm at Burns Creek, west of
High River, against 68 mm over 48 hours at Calgary.

Antecedent conditions decided how much of that reached the channel.
Ground was already saturated from a wet spring, partly still frozen, and
the warm rain melted lying snowpack at elevation, adding meltwater to
rainfall. Steep headwater basins shed the lot quickly — per
[[Water Moves Through a System]] — and the Bow, Elbow and Highwood
delivered their peaks to the same reaches at close to the same time. The
Bow at Calgary peaked at 1,740 m³/s, above the Government of Alberta's
previously recorded maximum of 1,520 m³/s in 1932. The total was not
extraordinary; the combination was.

## Two totals, and neither is wrong

The federal database totals \$2.72 billion. The Government of Alberta
puts the event at approximately \$6 billion, "of which approximately \$5
billion was non-insurable".

Those are not rival estimates of the same quantity. The federal figure
**sums the payments it can identify** — federal assistance plus insurance
— and leaves provincial and municipal costs blank. The provincial figure
**estimates total damage and recovery**, insured and not. Quote whichever
answers your question, name it, and say what it leaves out. Insurance
alone was "in excess of \$1.7 billion" according to the Insurance Bureau
of Canada in September 2013, and the IBC called that preliminary at the
time — the point [[Hazard and Disaster Records]] makes about insured loss
being a floor rather than a total.
