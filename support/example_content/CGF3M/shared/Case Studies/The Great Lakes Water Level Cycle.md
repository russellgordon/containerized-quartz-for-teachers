This is the slow one, and it is happening at the shore you walk to. No
tide, no single event, no day on which it begins — just a lake surface
that rises and falls through more than a metre over years, and a
shoreline that adjusts to whichever level it is currently at.

## The range, from the people who measure it

The Canadian Hydrographic Service publishes the Monthly Water Level
Bulletin for the Great Lakes, with a record running from 1918. Its
recorded extremes of monthly mean level, in metres above International
Great Lakes Datum (1985), are:

| Lake | Maximum | Minimum | Range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Superior | 183.86 m (2019) | 182.96 m (1926) | 0.90 m |
| Huron | 177.46 m (2020) | 175.78 m (1964) | 1.68 m |
| Erie | 175.13 m (2019) | 173.45 m (1934) | 1.68 m |
| Ontario | 75.80 m (2019) | 74.14 m (1934) | 1.66 m |

Read the years, not only the numbers. Three of the four maxima fall in
2019–2020 and two of the minima in the 1930s. The levels are measured by
a binational network — the Canadian Hydrographic Service monitors 33
stations on the Canadian shoreline — and coordinated by the
Coordinating Committee on Great Lakes Basic Hydraulic and Hydrologic
Data, so the Canadian and American numbers are the same numbers.

## Why a metre matters so much

On a gently sloping shore, a small vertical rise moves the waterline a
long way horizontally, and it lets waves break higher up the bank than
they have for years. The damaging combination is a high-level year, a
large storm, and a winter without an ice foot to absorb the waves — the
processes in [[Weathering, Erosion, and Deposition]] arriving all at
once on material that has not been tested at that elevation recently.

Levels respond to the water balance of the basin: precipitation over the
lakes and their catchments, runoff, evaporation, and outflow. Evaporation
is largest in late autumn and early winter when cold dry air crosses
still-warm water, which is why the seasonal cycle peaks in summer rather
than at snowmelt.

## What it does to the decisions people make

A property owner who armours a bank in a high year has paid for
protection they may not need in a low decade — and, as
[[Using the Physical Environment]] argues, has moved sediment away from a
neighbour who did not choose that. A municipality maintaining a shoreline
road must decide against a level range rather than a level. A planner
setting a building setback is drawing a line for a lake that will be
somewhere else within a working lifetime.

That is why the honest framing here is a **range with a probability**,
not a number. Check the record before arguing from any single year — the
bulletin is public, the data go back to 1918, and one bad autumn is
evidence of very little. Take your own measurements at
[[The Shoreline Study]], and build the argument in
[[The Local Hazard Assessment]].
