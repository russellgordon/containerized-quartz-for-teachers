Fiona is the case that breaks the assumption that a hurricane stops
mattering once it stops being a hurricane. Everything below comes from
the National Hurricane Center's *Tropical Cyclone Report* on Fiona,
published 23 March 2023 — a document worth reading in full, because it
shows what a properly sourced account of an event looks like.

## What happened, in the report's own terms

Fiona formed on 14 September 2022, crossed Guadeloupe, made landfall in
Puerto Rico and the Dominican Republic, and peaked at 120 kt on
21 September. On 23 September it merged with an approaching front and
became an intense extratropical cyclone, then made landfall on the
southeastern coast of Nova Scotia at about 0700 UTC on 24 September with
maximum winds near 85 kt and a minimum pressure of 931 mb.

Three pressure figures circulate, and they measure different things. The
931 mb above is the NHC's analysed central pressure at landfall. A buoy —
Environment and Climate Change Canada's 44488 in eastern Chedabucto Bay —
read **931.2 mb**, but a buoy is at sea. The lowest reading **on land**
was **932.7 mb**, at an automated station on Hart Island, Nova Scotia.

That distinction is the whole of the record claim. The NHC writes that
"Fiona was the deepest cyclone on record (by minimum pressure) to make
landfall in Canada"; ECCC's claim is narrower and about the land reading —
"the deepest barometric pressure for a storm ever recorded over land in
Canada", against a previous Canadian record of 940.2 mb set in 1977. Use
932.7 mb for the land record.

> [!warning] Post-tropical does not mean weakened
> The NHC calls Fiona at Canadian landfall "a very strong extratropical
> cyclone", and Canadian forecasters call the same system post-tropical.
> Both mean it had stopped drawing energy from warm water and started
> drawing it from temperature contrast — see [[Storms and Floods]]. It
> was also, by that point, very large, which is why the surge was so
> destructive. Every hurricane season somebody in Atlantic Canada reads
> "no longer a hurricane" as "no longer dangerous". This is the event
> that proves otherwise.

## The damage was water, not only wind

Sustained winds of 68 kt gusting to 86 kt were recorded at Beaver Island,
Nova Scotia. But the report attributes the devastating coastal flooding to
storm surge and waves: a Canadian Hydrographic Service gauge at Port aux
Basques, Newfoundland measured 2.3 ft above Higher High Water Large Tide,
and the measurements "suggest widespread storm surge inundation of 3 to
5 ft" above ground level. Over 100 homes were destroyed along the coast
from Port aux Basques eastward to Burgeo, and more than 500,000 customers
lost power across Atlantic Canada.

## The counts

Fiona caused **7 direct deaths** — two each in Puerto Rico, the Dominican
Republic and Canada, and one in Guadeloupe — and **22 indirect deaths**,
21 of them in Puerto Rico. Canada's two were a 73-year-old woman swept
out to sea at Port aux Basques and an 81-year-old man at Lower Prospect,
Nova Scotia.

On cost, watch the figure move. The Insurance Bureau of Canada first put
insured damage at \$660 million in October 2022, then restated it in
January 2023 as **over CAD \$800 million**, calling Fiona "the most costly
extreme weather event ever recorded in Atlantic Canada". Aon's *Global
Catastrophe Recap*, which the NHC cites rather than endorses, put combined
Caribbean and Canadian damage above US\$3.09 billion.

The IBC's own explanation of the gap between insured and actual loss is
the best sentence available on this: many of those affected "were located
in high-risk flood areas and floodplains where residential flood insurance
coverage is not available", so "the overwhelming majority of costs for
this disaster will be borne by government". Insured loss is a floor —
[[Hazard and Disaster Records]] — and here it is a floor with a known
reason. Take that to [[Who Should Pay to Rebuild|Who Should Pay to Rebuild?]].
