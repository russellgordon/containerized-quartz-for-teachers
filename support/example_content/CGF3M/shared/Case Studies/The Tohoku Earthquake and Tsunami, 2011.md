On 11 March 2011 a subduction-zone rupture off northeastern Honshu
generated the largest earthquake ever instrumentally recorded in Japan
and a tsunami that overran defences built by the country that had thought
hardest about tsunamis. It is the case that most clearly separates the
hazard from the disaster.

Two agencies, two numbers. The United States Geological Survey gives
**Mw 9.1** at a depth of 29 km; the Japan Meteorological Agency gives
**Mw 9.0** at 24 km. They use different velocity models, and neither is a
correction of the other — cite each to its own body. The USGS also
revised its own figure upward twice in the days after the event, from 8.9
to 9.0 to 9.1, which is worth remembering the next time you see a
magnitude quoted on the day.

Names matter too. The USGS event name is "2011 Great Tohoku Earthquake,
Japan"; "Great East Japan Earthquake" is Japan's name for the *disaster*,
not for the seismic event. Using them interchangeably blurs the
distinction this whole course is built on.

## Three heights, all correct

| Measure | Value | Where, and who measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tide-gauge height | ≥ 9.3 m | Soma, Fukushima — JMA. The "≥" is theirs: the gauge was destroyed and later waves may have been higher |
| Run-up, highest grade-A point | 39.2 m | Aneyoshi, Miyako City, Iwate |
| Run-up, highest grade-B point | 40.0 m | Ryōri south bay mouth, Ōfunato |

Those come from the Tōhoku Earthquake Tsunami Joint Survey Group — 299
researchers from 64 institutions — whose released database holds 5,907
surveyed points, each carrying the group's own reliability grade. Grade A
means a clear mark with small error; grade B means a reliable witness but
no clear mark; grade D means marginal and highly uncertain. The largest
number in the file is 55.5 m, and it is grade D.

That is why figures between 39 and 55 metres all circulate for this
event, and why the honest citation is **about 40 m, and 39.2 m at
Aneyoshi as the best-evidenced measurement.** Quote a number with its
grade, or you have quoted the dataset's noise. The group's own condition
of use says as much: quality "can vary with location and surveyor. Please
check degree of quality of each data."

## The count, and the part of it that teaches

Japan's National Police Agency reported **15,901 dead and 2,519 missing**
across twelve prefectures as of the end of February 2026. Separately, the
Reconstruction Agency counted **3,810 disaster-related deaths** as of
31 December 2025 — people who died later of injuries or of the burden of
evacuation life. The two sets do not overlap, and no Japanese body
publishes a combined total; if you want the sum, present it as a sum and
name both sources.

Then the fact that should end any argument that a disaster is only
physics. In **Fukushima prefecture, disaster-related deaths (2,350)
exceed direct deaths (1,614)**. The evacuation and its aftermath killed
more people there than the earthquake and the wave did. Fukushima's count
is still rising; Iwate's and Miyagi's are static.

At Fukushima Daiichi the accident was first rated at INES Level 5 by
Japan's own Nuclear and Industrial Safety Agency, then raised to **Level
7** roughly a month after the tsunami, once early release estimates were
re-evaluated. The rating was assigned in Japan, not by the IAEA — but
which Japanese body made the Level 7 decision is reported inconsistently,
and both the agency's pages and the IAEA's were unreachable when this was
checked in August 2026. Where a source cannot be confirmed, say so rather
than picking the version you like.

That is also why no damage cost appears on this page. Figures circulate;
none of them could be traced to a Japanese government publication. Most
of the sources here publish in Japanese only, so cite the issuing body
and the date rather than pretending to an English original —
[[Judging a Source]]. Compare this event with another in
[[The Disaster Case]].
