Drought has no moment and no boundary, so it is usually measured by what
it removes rather than by what it does. In 2021 the Canadian Prairies
gave the clearest recent demonstration of that, and the measurement is
unusually clean because Statistics Canada counts the harvest every year
in the same way.

## What the harvest showed

Statistics Canada's crop production release of 3 December 2021 reported
that "extremely hot and dry weather impacted crop development during the
growing season in Western Canada", producing the largest year-over-year
yield decrease on record for several crops.

| Crop | 2021 production | Change from 2020 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wheat | 21.7 million tonnes | down 38.5% |
| Canola | 12.6 million tonnes | down 35.4% — lowest since 2007 |
| Barley | 6.9 million tonnes | down 35.3% |
| Oats | 2.6 million tonnes | down 43.0% |

The provincial detail is where the geography sits. Saskatchewan wheat
fell 48.1 per cent to 8.3 million tonnes and Alberta wheat 42.9 per cent
to 6.3 million tonnes, while Manitoba wheat fell 28.9 per cent — a
gradient across three provinces facing the same broad weather pattern.
Ontario corn for grain, meanwhile, rose on favourable conditions in the
same year. One country, one growing season, opposite outcomes.

## Which drought, and how it was rated

Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada's Canadian Drought Monitor rates
conditions monthly on the D0-to-D4 scale set out in [[Drought and Heat]],
and its archive of monthly maps runs from 2002 to the present, so you can
watch this event develop and retreat month by month rather than reading
it as a single figure. Do that before you write about it — it is the best
available demonstration that a drought has a shape in time as well as in
space.

Note what the crop figures are and are not. They measure *agricultural*
drought — soil moisture failing when the crop needed it — which is not
the same as the precipitation deficit, and not the same as the low
streamflow and reservoir levels that follow months later.

## Heat and drought together

The same summer brought the western North American heat dome of late
June. Environment and Climate Change Canada recorded 49.6 °C at Lytton,
British Columbia on 29 June 2021 — a national record — and the BC
Coroners Service later attributed 619 deaths to the heat event of 25 June
to 1 July. Heat and drought reinforce each other by the feedback
described in [[Drought and Heat]]: dry soil has no water to evaporate, so
incoming energy heats the air instead, which dries the soil further.

The consequences did not stop at the field. Reduced feed forced livestock
sales, water sources for stock failed, and the same conditions fed the
fire season — [[Wildfire]]. Whether the physical shortfall becomes a
crisis for a particular farm depends on irrigation, insurance, debt and
land tenure, which is exactly the vulnerability argument in
[[Hazard, Exposure, Vulnerability]] applied to a business rather than a
household. Take the water-sharing question to [[Sharing a Watershed]].
