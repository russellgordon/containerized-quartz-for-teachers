**Launched in the second week of Unit 1, due before the unit ends.**
Individual. About 800 words plus a labelled diagram or profile drawn from
your own field data.

One physical process, working at one place you have stood on, explained
from cause to landform to consequence.

## Choose a real pairing

Wave erosion and deposition at the shoreline. Slope processes and soil
development in the woodlot. Runoff, infiltration and compaction on the
farm. Freeze–thaw weathering on an exposed rock face or a road cut.
Whichever you take, you must have your own measurements from
[[Your Field Notebook]] — this is not a library exercise.

## What it must contain

1. **The setting**, located precisely: coordinates or grid reference,
   the landform, and the material.
2. **The process**, explained physically. What supplies the energy, what
   is moved, and what controls the rate.
3. **Your evidence**, presented as data — a profile, a table of
   measurements, a photograph with scale — and labelled so a reader can
   see what supports what.
4. **A climate or weather link**, using published data from
   [[Reading Climate Data]] rather than an impression. Show that the
   conditions this process needs actually occur here, and how often.
5. **The wider distribution.** Where else in the world does this process
   dominate, and what do those places have in common with this one?
   [[Reading a Landscape]] and [[Climate and Its Controls]] are the way in.
6. **What happens next**, if the process continues at the rate you have
   inferred — with the uncertainty of that inference stated.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | The physics of the process is right and is explained, not named |
| Thinking | Observation and inference kept apart; rate and uncertainty addressed |
| Application | Your own data is used as evidence, not as illustration |
| Communication | Diagram labelled and readable; terminology used precisely |

> [!warning] The commonest failure is describing the landform
> A description of what a beach looks like is not a study of a process.
> The examiner's test is whether a reader could predict what the site
> would look like after another decade — and if your writing does not
> support that prediction, it has described rather than explained.
