**Launched early in Unit 2, due mid-unit.** Individual. About 1000 words
plus one map and one figure.

Two disasters, compared from a geographic perspective. The comparison is
the task — a pair of separate descriptions stapled together earns very
little.

## Choosing the pair

Choose two events that share a hazard type and differ in something you
can analyse: level of development, warning, building standards, timing,
or the modification of the landscape beforehand. The six worked cases on
this site are legitimate choices, and so is a pair of your own if the
records are good — [[Hazard and Disaster Records]] tells you what "good"
means here.

Pairs that work well include an earthquake in a high-income country
against one of similar magnitude elsewhere; a Canadian flood against a
flood on a different continent; a storm that struck a prepared coast
against one that did not.

## What it must contain

1. **The physical event**, for each, in physical terms — magnitude,
   extent, duration, and the process that produced it.
2. **The impacts**, short-term and long-term, across social, economic,
   political and environmental dimensions. Attribute every figure to the
   body that published it and give the date you retrieved it.
3. **The comparison itself**, organised by theme rather than by event.
4. **The explanation.** Why did outcomes differ? Separate the physical
   differences from the differences in exposure and vulnerability —
   [[Hazard, Exposure, Vulnerability]].
5. **What either place changed afterwards**, and whether the evidence
   suggests it worked.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | Both events described accurately, with the physics right |
| Thinking | Differences explained causally, not listed |
| Application | Sources are the responsible agencies; figures carry dates |
| Communication | Structured by theme; map and figure both do work |

> [!warning] Casualty figures are provisional for a long time
> Early reporting on any disaster is wrong, and it is wrong in both
> directions. Use the authoritative body's current count — a national
> police agency, a coroner's service, a statistical office — say which
> count it is, and note where a different body gives a different number
> and why. A range with sources beats a single confident figure.

Take the argument about what to call these events to
[[Is a Disaster Ever Natural?]].
