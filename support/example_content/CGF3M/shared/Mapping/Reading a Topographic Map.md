A topographic map is the only map that tells you the shape of the ground.
In a course about physical processes that shape is not background — it is
the variable. Slope decides where water goes, where sediment stops, how
fast fire runs, and what a landslide would reach.

## Contours

A contour joins points of equal elevation, and the **contour interval**
between successive lines is printed in the margin. Every fifth line is
heavier and labelled; count from that **index contour**. Three rules do
most of the work:

- Contours never cross, because a point cannot have two elevations.
- Contours close on themselves — a ring inside a ring is a hill, and a
  ring with ticks pointing inward is a hollow.
- Where a contour crosses a stream it bends into a **V pointing
  upstream**, uphill against the flow.

That last rule gives you drainage direction on a sheet with nothing
labelled at all, which is exactly what you need to delineate a watershed:
trace the height of land around a chosen outlet, keeping the divide on
the ridge crests, and you have the catchment.

## Spacing is slope, and slope is process

Close spacing means steep ground: fast runoff, thin soil, high erosion
potential, expensive to build on, and — in fire — a fast uphill run.
Wide spacing means gentle ground: slow runoff, deeper soil, wetness
likely, deposition rather than erosion. Read the map as a map of energy
and you can predict most of what
[[Weathering, Erosion, and Deposition]] describes before you arrive.

Profiles are worth the ten minutes they take. Draw a straight line across
the feature you care about, mark where each contour crosses, plot height
against distance, and you have a cross-section that shows terrace
levels, valley asymmetry and floodplain width far better than the plan
view does.

## Scale, grid, symbols

**1:50 000** means one unit on the map is 50 000 of the same units on the
ground — 1 cm to 500 m, 2 cm to the kilometre. Photocopying wrecks the
ratio and not the printed bar scale, so trust the bar. Canadian sheets
carry a numbered grid: a four-figure reference names a square, eastings
before northings, and a six-figure reference splits it into tenths and
lands you within roughly 100 m.

[Toporama](https://atlas.gc.ca/toporama/en/index.html) renders current
federal topographic data in a browser, free and without an account
(accessed August 2026). [Ontario GeoHub](https://geohub.lio.gov.on.ca/)
carries provincial elevation, hydrography and imagery layers for the same
ground.

Take this to the field in [[The Woodlot Study]] and put other data on top
of it in [[Using a Web GIS]].
