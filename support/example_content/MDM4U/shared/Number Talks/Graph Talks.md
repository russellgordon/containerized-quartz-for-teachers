A chart appears on the board with no title, no numbers on the axes,
no source — just a shape. Three questions, always in this order: what
do you notice? what do you wonder? and the one this course adds —
what would you need to know before you repeated its claim out loud?
Every chart is an argument somebody chose to make, and the choosing
happens in places the chart never mentions: where the axis starts,
which years made the cut, who got counted.

## How we play

1. Thirty seconds of silent looking. No hands yet.
2. Notice only what is *there*: a long tail on one side, a cluster, a
   gap, two points sitting far from everyone else.
3. Then name the claim the chart is making — and one thing you would
   have to check before you would make that claim yourself.

> [!example]- Three readings of one histogram with a long right tail
> - "Most values bunch up on the left and a few stragglers run far to
>   the right. Those stragglers drag the mean toward them and leave
>   the median behind, so the mean here must sit to the *right* of
>   the median. Anyone quoting 'the average' is quoting the number
>   the stragglers moved."
> - "It could be incomes, or how long people waited on hold, or how
>   long viewers watched before quitting. All three pile up small and
>   trail off long — and none of them should ever be summarized with
>   a normal curve."
> - "Before I believed it: who is missing? If the numbers came only
>   from people who finished the video, everyone who quit in the
>   first second was never in the data at all. The shape cannot show
>   you a bar that was never allowed to exist."

## One variation

Same rules, but the chart is a bar chart whose vertical axis starts
at 96 instead of 0. Two bars, one visibly towering over the other —
and the numbers behind them are 97 and 99. Nothing on the chart is
false; every bar is exactly as tall as the data says. The lie lives
in the axis, and it is the most common lie in public life. The
follow-up question is the sharp one: *who benefits from that
impression?* That question opens [[The Graph Gallery]], where your
group hunts real examples and reconstructs what each one was hiding.

> [!tip] Shapes have names
> Skewed, symmetric, bimodal, clustered, gapped, outlying — using the
> right name for the right feature is half of every reading, and
> [[One-Variable Statistics]] spends real time making those names
> exact. The scatter-plot version of this talk is the same game one
> dimension up: a cloud that drifts upward has a *direction*, a
> *strength*, and a *form*, and [[Two-Variable Statistics]] insists
> you name all three before you say the word "correlation".
