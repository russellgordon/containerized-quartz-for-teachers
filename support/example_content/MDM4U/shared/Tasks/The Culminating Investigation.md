## What you are making

Every other task in this course hands you the question. This one does
not. You choose something you actually want to know, find or collect
data that could answer it, analyse that data with the tools this
course gives you, and defend a conclusion in writing and out loud in
front of the class.

It is the largest single piece of work in the course, and it is
deliberately not a final-week project. It is launched in Unit 2, it
collects a milestone every few classes, and by the time the analysis
starts you should already be bored of your own data set. That is the
plan working.

Your question may be one substantial problem, or two smaller ones —
one about probability and one about statistics. Both routes are
allowed and both are marked the same way.

## The milestone schedule

| Checkpoint | What is due |
| --- | --- |
| Unit 2, Day 5 | Two candidate questions, with variables and possible sources |
| Unit 2, Day 6 | One question approved, in a sentence, with a named source |
| Unit 3, Day 3 | Collection under way — a third of the data in hand |
| Unit 3, Day 6 | Data collected, cleaned, organized, and cited |
| Unit 4, Day 4 | Analysis worked, with feedback taken and acted on |
| Unit 4, Day 5 | The written report handed in |
| Unit 4, Days 6–7 | Presented and critiqued at the symposium |

Miss a milestone and the work does not disappear — it lands on top of
the next one, which is exactly how projects fail. Come and see me
before the checkpoint, not after.

## Choosing a question you can actually answer

The single most common way this project goes wrong is a magnificent
question with no reachable data. "Does social media cause anxiety in
teenagers?" is a research programme, not a semester project. "Do
students in this school who report more than three hours of screen
time on a school night sleep fewer hours?" is answerable, with a
survey you could run, and it is *more* interesting because you might
be wrong about it.

Three honest tests, before you commit:

1. **Can you name the data?** Not "there must be statistics on this" —
   an actual data set, from an actual place. Start at
   [[Where to Find Real Data]] and check what the columns really are
   using [[Reading a Data Dictionary]].
2. **Can you get it before the collection deadline?** A survey of your
   own school is realistic; a survey of a city is not. Secondary data
   from Statistics Canada is realistic; data a company keeps private
   is not.
3. **Could the answer come out either way?** If you already know the
   answer, you have chosen a topic, not a question.

[[Choosing a Data Set]] walks through this in more detail, and I would
rather approve a small, sharp question than admire a large, doomed
one.

## What a defensible report contains

- **The question**, stated precisely enough that somebody else could
  test whether you answered it.
- **Background**: what is already known, and where you looked.
- **The plan**: population, variables, sampling method, sample size,
  and the sources of bias you anticipated — in the form you agreed to
  at the approval checkpoint.
- **The data**: how you [[E1.3|gathered and organized]] it, cleaned
  honestly, with every source cited per [[Citing Your Sources]].
- **The analysis**: numerical and graphical summaries, each chosen for
  a reason you can state — a probability model, a normal model, a
  regression, a correlation, an expected value, as the question
  demands.
- **The conclusion**: what the data supports, in a sentence the data
  can carry.
- **The limitations**: sample size, bias, missing groups, confounding
  variables. This section is not an apology; it is the part that
  proves you understood your own study.
- **Follow-up**: the next question, and what you would do differently.

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]], the strength of the argument is the product.
An investigation with a small sample and a carefully limited
conclusion outscores one with impressive data and a claim the data
never earned. Two failures are fatal in a way nothing else is: data
that cannot be traced to its source, and a conclusion stated with more
confidence than the evidence supports.

Your [[Math Journal]] runs alongside the project — the question you
abandoned and why, the day the data turned out to be messier than the
plan, the analysis that surprised you. That record is evidence, and
[[Showing Growth]] explains what makes it count.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| A question worth asking | Precise, answerable, and genuinely open |
| A plan that anticipated trouble | Bias and limits named before collection |
| Traceable data | Every number leads back to a citable source |
| Analysis chosen, not sprayed | Each summary justified by the question |
| A conclusion the data carries | Claims sized to the evidence behind them |
| Limits owned | Weaknesses named by you, before anyone asks |

> [!tip]- If your data turns out to be worse than you hoped
> This happens to real researchers constantly, and it is not a reason
> to start over in the last week. A smaller honest analysis of flawed
> data — with the flaws documented and their effect on the conclusion
> argued — is worth far more than a rescue attempt with invented
> numbers. Fabricated or "adjusted" data ends the project, so bring
> the problem to me the day you find it and we will resize the
> question together.
