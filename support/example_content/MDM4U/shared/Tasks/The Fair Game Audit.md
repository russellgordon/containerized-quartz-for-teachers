## What you are making

Somebody makes money from every game of chance you have ever played,
and they did the arithmetic first. Your pair either **audits** a game
that already exists — a carnival wheel, a scratch card, a loot box, a
raffle, a mechanic buried in a board game — or **designs** one and
audits your own. Either way you hand in the same thing: the complete
sample space, a probability for every outcome, an expected value per
play, and a verdict.

The verdict is the point. "This game is unfair" is a claim; the price
that *would* make it fair is an argument. Every game you audit has a
number attached to it — the amount the player loses, on average, each
time they play — and your job is to find that number and say what it
means to somebody who plays a hundred times.

$$E(X) = \sum x \cdot P(X = x)$$

A worked shape, so the standard is clear: a wheel with eight equal
sectors — one paying \$10, two paying \$3 each, five paying nothing —
returns \$2 per spin on average. Charge \$2 and the game is fair.
Charge \$3, as most operators do, and the house keeps a third of every
dollar wagered. Both sentences come from the same table.

## Milestones

- [ ] Game chosen, rules written out exactly as a player would meet
      them — including the ones printed in small type
- [ ] Sample space listed completely, and the counting method used to
      build it named
- [ ] Probability of every outcome computed, verified to sum to $1$,
      and shown in a table
- [ ] Expected value per play computed in dollars, with the fair price
      stated and justified
- [ ] The honest paragraph: what your model ignores — the fun, the
      operator's costs, the fact that nobody plays exactly once

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]], the reasoning is the product. A modest game
audited airtightly outranks a spectacular game whose probabilities do
not sum to $1$. On the due date your pair defends the verdict out loud
in three minutes, and the first question will always be *where did that
probability come from?* Your [[Math Journal]] entry on the moment your
first outcome table turned out to be incomplete finishes the evidence —
that moment happens to almost everyone, and hiding it costs you more
than it saves.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| A complete sample space | Every outcome listed; nothing quietly merged |
| Probabilities that hold up | The column sums to $1$, and you can say why |
| An expected value, not a guess | Each payout weighted by its own chance |
| A defended price | The fair price stated, and the gap explained |
| Honest limits | What the model leaves out, named plainly |

> [!success]- If your probabilities refuse to sum to one
> Two causes, almost always. Either an outcome is missing — the
> near-miss, the replay, the "nothing happens" case nobody writes down
> — or two of your outcomes overlap and one event is being counted
> twice. List the outcomes as physical results a bystander could see,
> not as prizes, and the overlap usually shows itself.
