> [!abstract] At a glance
> The whole class, across the last two days · six minutes to present,
> three to be questioned · every person critiques three investigations

## What you are making

Two things, and the second one is not a formality. You present your
[[The Culminating Investigation|culminating investigation]] to the
room, and you critique three other people's — in writing, by the
protocol below, constructively enough that the author is glad you did.
Critiquing well is assessed here exactly as presenting well is,
because a statistician who cannot examine somebody else's study is
only half trained.

Guests are invited: last year's students, teachers from other
departments, sometimes a parent who works with data. Assume nobody in
the audience knows your topic and everybody can smell an overstated
claim.

## The presentation: six minutes

| Minute | What the room needs from you |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | The question, and why it is worth asking |
| 2 | Where the data came from, and how it was collected |
| 3–4 | The analysis — the summaries that mattered, shown not listed |
| 5 | Your conclusion, sized to the evidence |
| 6 | Limitations, and the follow-up study you would run |

Then three minutes of questions from the floor. Slides are optional; a
single well-chosen graph beats nine slides of bullet points, and every
graph you show gets asked "what does the axis start at?" Rehearse
once, out loud, with a timer — six minutes is shorter than it sounds.

## The critique protocol

For each of your three assigned investigations, you write four things
on the critique sheet. Nothing else counts as a critique.

1. **What the evidence earns.** One specific claim the data genuinely
   supports, and why it does.
2. **One question the analysis leaves open.** Something a careful
   listener still cannot tell from what was presented.
3. **One suggestion.** A different summary, a check for bias, a
   comparison group — something the author could actually do.
4. **The strongest counter-reading.** If the same data could tell a
   different story, say what that story is.

Two rules. Critique the work, never the person: "the sample only
reached students in one grade" is a critique; "you were lazy about
collecting data" is not. And be specific enough to be useful — "good
job" and "needs more data" are both worth nothing to the person
holding the sheet.

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]], your presentation is marked on whether an
outsider could follow the argument and whether your claims stayed
inside your evidence — not on polish, and certainly not on nerve. Your
three critique sheets are marked on specificity and usefulness. The
authors read them, so write what you would want to receive.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| Clear for outsiders | A stranger could restate your question afterwards |
| Evidence shown | Graphs and numbers on screen, not merely described |
| Honest sizing | Claims that stop where the data stops |
| Questions handled | Answers that concede a fair point cleanly |
| Critique that helps | Specific, actionable, about the work |

> [!note]- If a question in the period exposes a real flaw
> Say so, out loud, and say what it would take to fix it. Conceding a
> fair point is not a loss of marks — it is the single clearest
> evidence that you understand your own study. Defending an
> indefensible claim, on the other hand, costs you the thing the whole
> course has been building.
