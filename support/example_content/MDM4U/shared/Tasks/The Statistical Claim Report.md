> Individual · launched in Unit 4, due two classes later · one claim
> from the news, traced back to its data and given a verdict

## What you are making

Somewhere this week a headline turned a number into a claim: a food
causes a disease, a policy cut a rate in half, a generation is more
anxious than the last, a team's new signing is a bargain. Pick one.
Then do the thing almost nobody does — **find the study the reporter
read**, and decide whether the claim survives contact with it.

Your report has three parts. First, the **claim as published**, quoted
exactly, with the article cited. Second, the **trail back to the
data**: the original study or data set, who produced it, who funded
it, how the data was collected, how many people or cases it covers,
and what was compared with what. Third, the **verdict**, in one of
three words — *supported*, *overstated*, or *not proven* — followed by
the evidence that forces it.

Somewhere in part two you will meet at least one of the tricks from
[[The Graph Gallery]] or one of the confusions from
[[Correlation and Causation]]. Name it when you meet it. If the claim
turns out to be perfectly sound, say so with the same confidence — a
verdict of *supported* is a real result, and it is worth exactly as
much.

## Milestones

- [ ] Claim chosen, quoted verbatim, and the article cited
- [ ] The original source located — the study, the release, or the
      data set the article was built from
- [ ] Method audited: who was measured, how many, how chosen, and
      compared against what
- [ ] Any graph in the article reconstructed honestly, or the reason
      it cannot be reconstructed stated
- [ ] Verdict delivered in one of the three words, with the two or
      three pieces of evidence that decide it

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]], the verdict earns nothing on its own; the
trail earns everything. A report that says *not proven* because the
original release genuinely never gives a sample size is stronger than
one that says *overstated* because the topic felt suspicious. Where
the trail runs out — some articles cite nothing findable — that dead
end **is** the finding, reported plainly. Your [[Math Journal]] entry
on what you believed about the claim before you traced it, and what
changed, comes in with the report.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| A claim, not a topic | One sentence, quoted, that could be false |
| A real trail | The original source found and cited, or its absence proven |
| Method examined | Sample, selection, comparison, and funding named |
| Statistics used, not waved | The specific number that decides it |
| A proportionate verdict | Confidence matching the evidence, either way |

> [!question]- If the article cites no source at all
> Then you have already found something. Search for the researcher,
> the institution, or the phrase in quotation marks; check whether a
> press release exists behind the article; check whether other outlets
> cite the same study with more detail. If the number cannot be traced
> after a genuine hunt, your verdict is *not proven* — and your report
> documents the hunt, because an untraceable statistic in a national
> outlet is a finding worth stating.
