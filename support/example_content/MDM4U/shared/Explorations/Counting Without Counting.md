Ontario licence plates in the current passenger format carry four
letters and three digits. How many different plates is that? You may
not list them, and the province is not going to wait while you try.

## The task

Four problems, in this order, and your group writes a *number* for
each one plus the argument that got you there.

1. A cafeteria offers $4$ mains, $3$ sides, and $5$ drinks. How many
   different meals? Now suppose one main comes with its side fixed —
   what happens to your count, and why?
2. Four letters and three digits on a plate. How many plates? Then:
   how many if no letter may repeat? Then: how many contain no vowel?
3. A pizza place advertises $12$ toppings and claims "over four
   thousand combinations". Is the claim true, generous, or an
   understatement? Decide what they must be counting.
4. Six people, six chairs in a row. How many seatings? Now the chairs
   are around a circular table and only who-sits-beside-whom matters.
   Is the answer the same? Defend it.

The point is not four answers. It is that by problem three your group
should have stopped listing anything, and should be able to say what
you replaced listing *with* — and exactly when that replacement is
allowed.

> [!tip]- Facilitation notes — for the teacher
> Make problem one small enough to list exhaustively; groups that
> list all $60$ meals and then notice the shape of their own list are
> the ones who own the principle rather than receive it. The fixed
> side in the second half of problem one is the first place where the
> naive multiplication breaks, and it should break in public.
>
> Problem two's "no vowel" variation reliably produces two different
> defensible readings — vowels among letters only, or the digit
> question left alone — and the disagreement is worth ten minutes.
> Problem three separates *choices* from *arrangements*: the pizza
> claim is about which subsets of toppings exist, not about order, and
> a group that reaches $2^{12}$ has independently found something the
> course will not formally name for two more classes. Problem four is
> the deliberate trap: the circular table breaks the pattern, and
> groups that argue their way out of it are ready for permutations.
>
> Listen for "times" versus "plus". Ask any group that adds to say
> the word *or* out loud in their sentence, and any group that
> multiplies to say the word *and*. That distinction is the whole
> unit in one breath.

## What mathematics tends to surface

The multiplicative counting principle, invented at the board rather
than announced: independent choices multiply, and the whole method
collapses the moment one choice constrains another. Alongside it the
additive principle, and the argument about which is which. Groups
regularly discover repetition-versus-no-repetition on their own, and
at least one group always finds the subset count in the pizza problem,
which is the door into combinations.
