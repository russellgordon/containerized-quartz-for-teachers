Here is the question: **what fraction of the students in this school
have a paid job during the school year?** You have twenty minutes and
you may not ask everybody. Design a method, run it, and report a
number with your confidence in it attached.

Then comes the part nobody expects to be the assignment.

## The task

Your group designs a sampling method, writes it down *before*
collecting anything, and predicts the answer it will produce. Then you
go and get roughly forty responses. Bring back your number.

When every group has reported, the numbers on the board will not
agree. That disagreement is the exploration. Working from the methods
alone — not the answers — your group now argues:

1. **Who could your method never have reached?** Name them
   concretely: the student in a co-op placement, the one who eats
   lunch off campus, the one in the class you did not visit.
2. **Would those people have answered differently?** This is the
   whole game. A method that misses people at random is inconvenient;
   a method that misses people *who differ on the thing you are
   measuring* is broken.
3. **Which way does your number lean, and by roughly how much?** Not
   "it might be off" — a direction and a rough size, defended.
4. **What would you do differently with a week and a class list?**

Finish by ranking the class's methods from most to least trustworthy,
with reasons — and be ready for your own to be ranked by everyone
else.

> [!tip]- Facilitation notes — for the teacher
> Require the written method *before* collection, or the reflection
> becomes rationalization. Clearing the sampling with the office in
> advance is worth doing, and it models the ethics review that Unit 3
> talks about.
>
> The distinction to hunt for is *population* versus *sampling frame*:
> the population is every student in the school; the frame is the set
> of students your method could possibly have reached. Almost every
> group will conflate them, and the moment they separate is the moment
> the day pays off. Groups that survey their own friends have produced
> the most instructive result in the room — treat it as a finding, not
> a failure, and let them work out which way it leans.
>
> The gap between group answers is your best evidence for variability;
> keep every number on the board. If the class is ready, ask what
> would happen to that spread if every group had collected four
> hundred responses instead of forty — the answer previews margin of
> error in Unit 4.
>
> Fast groups: ask them to design a stratified sample by grade, and to
> say what information they would need before they could weight it.

## What mathematics tends to surface

Population against sample against sampling frame. The five sampling
techniques, arrived at as solutions to problems the room actually hit.
Non-response and voluntary-response bias, discovered from the inside.
Variability between samples of the same size, on the board in the
form of five different answers to one question. And the habit this
course keeps returning to: *who is missing from this data?*
