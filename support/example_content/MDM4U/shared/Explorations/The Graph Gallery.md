Twelve charts are up on the walls. Every one of them is built from
real numbers, and not a single number on any of them is false. Every
one of them is also lying to you, and each does it differently.

Your group walks the gallery with a marker. Name the trick.

## The task

At each chart, write three things on the sticky note beside it:

1. **The impression** the chart creates in the first two seconds.
2. **The trick** — the specific design decision that creates the
   impression.
3. **The fix** — how the same honest data should have been drawn.

Some tricks the room usually finds: an axis that starts somewhere
other than zero; a pictogram scaled in both directions so doubling a
value quadruples the ink; a time window that begins exactly at the
convenient year; percentages with no denominator; two vertical axes
scaled until the lines appear to touch; unequal bin widths in a
histogram; a pie chart tilted into three dimensions so the near slice
swells; a "record high" that is a record only because the record began
in the same decade.

When the walk is done, your group builds one of your own. Take an
honest data set — one you have already worked with — and produce a
pair of charts from it: one drawn to mislead, one drawn honestly. Then
present the misleading one to another group and see how long it takes
them to catch you.

The closing question is the sharp one, and it is not about graphs:
**who benefits from each impression?** A truncated axis is not a
mistake. Somebody chose it, for a reason, and the reason is usually
findable.

> [!tip]- Facilitation notes — for the teacher
> Build the gallery from genuine published charts — news outlets,
> annual reports, advertising, political leaflets, and social media —
> and print them large enough to read from a metre away. A mix of
> political leanings and a couple of *honest* charts among the twelve
> keeps the day from becoming a partisan exercise and rewards groups
> who check rather than assume.
>
> The area trick is the one that needs help: ask a group to measure
> the doubled pictogram with a ruler and report what actually doubled.
> The dual-axis chart is the hardest to fix honestly, and the argument
> about how to fix it is more valuable than the fix.
>
> Do not let the day end on "all charts lie". Insist that each group's
> honest redraw is as clear and as striking as their dishonest one —
> the finding to leave with is that good design and honesty are not
> opposites, and that the same data can be presented compellingly
> without a single distortion.
>
> The "who benefits" question connects straight to the task launched
> today. Keep the sticky notes; they are the catalogue students will
> want while writing it.

## What mathematics tends to surface

How graphical and numerical summaries misrepresent data while
remaining technically accurate — scale, baseline, aggregation, and
selection all doing quiet work. Area against length as a visual
encoding. Rates against counts, again. And the reader's habit this
whole unit is trying to build: check the axis before you read the
shape.
