Your group's job today is to be dishonest on purpose, in public, with
real data. Find two quantities that rise and fall together beautifully
— a correlation strong enough to impress a newspaper — and between
which no sane person believes there is any causal link at all.

Then write the headline a careless reporter would write about it.

## The task

Hunt in real data. Annual figures are the easiest hunting ground:
anything measured once a year for twenty years can be lined up against
anything else measured once a year for twenty years. Use the sources
from [[Where to Find Real Data]] and keep every citation as you go —
a spurious correlation you cannot source is just a rumour.

For each candidate pair your group produces:

- the scatter plot, and the correlation coefficient computed with
  technology;
- the headline a careless reporter would run;
- the **honest explanation** — and this is where the marks live. Is
  this an accident? Is there a third thing driving both, and can you
  name it? Is the relationship real but backwards from the headline?

Rank your finds by *how convincing the false story is*, not by how
high the correlation is. Then close with the hard question, which the
whole class answers together: **if a strong correlation cannot show
causation, what could?** Sketch a study that would actually settle a
causal claim, and say what it would cost.

> [!tip]- Facilitation notes — for the teacher
> Two ground rules. First, real data with real citations — inventing
> the numbers defeats the entire purpose, because the shock depends on
> the data being genuine. Second, students may look at Tyler Vigen's
> published *Spurious Correlations* collection for the flavour of the
> genre, but a copied example scores nothing; the hunting is the
> learning.
>
> The taxonomy usually arrives on its own, and it is worth pinning to
> the board as groups discover each type: pure coincidence; common
> cause (ice cream sales and drownings, both driven by summer);
> reverse causation (the headline has the arrow backwards); and the
> lurking third variable that is simply population size or inflation
> — the most common one in real annual data, and the reason careful
> analysts work in rates rather than totals.
>
> Push the strongest groups to the closing question early. Naming what
> *would* establish causation — a controlled experiment, randomized
> assignment, a plausible mechanism, replication — is what keeps the
> day from collapsing into cynicism about all statistics. The goal is
> a sharper reader, not a reader who believes nothing.

## What mathematics tends to surface

The correlation coefficient as a measure of *linear fit* and nothing
more. The four ways two correlated variables can be related — cause
and effect, common cause, reverse cause, accident — separated by
argument rather than by arithmetic. The rates-versus-totals trap. And
the beginning of an answer to the only question that matters after
this day: what kind of evidence would ever justify a causal claim?
