This class keeps a museum. Its exhibits are our best mistakes — and
in this course, "best" means the ones a careful, intelligent person
walks into on purpose. None of them are careless. Each is evidence
that a situation invited a reasonable person to a wrong conclusion,
and evidence is for studying, not shredding.

| Exhibit | What it looked like | Why a smart person made it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The swap | Treating $P(A \mid B)$ as $P(B \mid A)$ | Both are "the chance of a positive result", and English does not distinguish them |
| The independent aces | $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)$ on two cards drawn without replacement | The rule was learned before its condition was |
| The due coin | "It has landed heads six times, so tails is overdue" | Long-run balance is real; the coin's memory is not |
| The lonely average | Reporting a mean income from a wildly skewed set | The mean is what "average" means in every other room |
| The willing sample | "We surveyed the whole school" — via a form only some students ever saw | Everyone *could* have answered, so it felt like everyone |

Hence the standing rule at the whiteboards: first attempts are never
erased. Cross them out, leave them legible, and let the room read
what they have to say. Your first attempt is the only record of how
you were thinking *before* you knew — destroy it and you cannot study
the gap it was measuring.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Nominate an exhibit: the error that got you this month. What makes
   it *reasonable* — what were the words or the situation doing to
   invite it?
2. Four of the five exhibits above are mistakes about probability
   that ordinary English actively encourages. Is that a failure of
   students, of language, or of how the ideas are usually taught?
3. The swap is not only a student error — it has decided real court
   cases and real medical conversations. When an expert makes it, who
   is responsible for catching it, and what would have to be true of
   the room for someone to speak up?
4. A slip (a mis-keyed factorial) is not a misconception (believing
   two events are independent because nothing obviously connects
   them). Which deserves your study time, and how do you tell them
   apart in your own work?
5. Design a mistake worth making: what question would you have to
   attempt for the error to teach you the most?

Every practice set in this course carries a **find the error** item on
purpose — reading a stranger's slip trains the same eye that
[[Checking Your Own Work]] turns on your own page. The norm
protecting all of it lives in [[Our Classroom Norms]]: mistakes are
data, and the museum only works if everyone donates.
