If mathematics class only ever asked you to do things you already
knew how to do, it would be a photocopying service. The learning
happens in the stretch — standing at a whiteboard with your random
group, holding a counting problem where nobody can yet say whether
order matters.[^1] But not all struggle is equal.
*Stuck-and-thinking* means you still have a next move to try: shrink
the problem to three people instead of thirty and list every case by
hand, draw the tree, ask what would have to be true for the two
events to be independent. *Spinning* means repeating the last move
harder and hoping. The first builds statisticians; the second just
builds frustration — and telling them apart from the inside is a
skill this course teaches on purpose, with [[Getting Unstuck]] as its
field guide.

This course adds a second kind of struggle that the earlier ones did
not: the struggle of *not being able to be certain*. A counting
answer is either right or wrong. A conclusion drawn from a sample
never is. Learning to say "the data supports this, within these
limits, and here is what would change my mind" is uncomfortable in a
way that no factorial ever was — and it is the single most valuable
sentence you will take out of this room.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Recall a problem that finally gave in after real struggle. What
   did the moment of giving-in feel like — and would being handed the
   answer have produced anything like it?
2. Probability is strange struggle: you can often *feel* an answer
   before you can compute one, and the feeling is regularly wrong.
   The birthday problem and the rare-disease test both ambush
   confident intuitions. Does being ambushed make you more careful,
   or just less willing to guess? Which of those is the good outcome?
3. Why does this class put you in *random* groups at *vertical*
   whiteboards? What do standing up and a shared marker do to
   struggle that a worksheet at a desk does not?
4. [[The Culminating Investigation]] is deliberately not scheduled
   like an assignment — it is launched early and lived with for
   months, which means weeks where it is stuck and nothing visible
   happens. Is that a design flaw or the point?
5. Where is the line between productive struggle and genuinely being
   out of your depth? Is admitting the second a failure, or a move?

This is why every practice set here folds its answers away and why
nothing is ever timed: the callouts protect your struggle from your
own curiosity. Attempt first, peek second — the order is the whole
point.

[^1]: Tasks arrive before technique on purpose. Peter Liljedahl's
    research in *Building Thinking Classrooms in Mathematics* (2020)
    measured what actually happens in the minutes after a problem is
    posed, and found that groups standing at erasable vertical
    surfaces start sooner, discuss more, and persist longer than
    groups seated with paper.
