Most studying that feels good does not work. Re-reading notes feels
smooth because the material is *familiar* — but familiar and known
are different things, and tests examine known. The fix is one idea:
**retrieval practice**. Memory strengthens when you pull something
out of your head, not when you push it back in one more time.

## The nightly loop

Class already hands you the loop: every day ends with two or three
check-your-understanding questions. That night, ten minutes:

1. Try the questions cold — notes closed, no peeking.
2. Mark yourself honestly against the class page.
3. Whatever failed goes on tomorrow's ask-about list, or to
   [[Help Sessions]] — [[Getting Help]] explains how.

Ten honest minutes nightly beats two loyal hours the night before a
test, and it is not close.

## Space it out

Cramming stores things where next week cannot find them. Revisit
ideas after a day, then a few days, then a week — practice pages such
as [[Counting Practice]] and [[Probability Practice]] exist exactly
for the return visits, long after their units end. This course
punishes cramming more than most, because its ideas stack: counting
feeds probability, probability feeds distributions, distributions
feed inference. A shaky $\binom{n}{r}$ in the first unit resurfaces
as a wrong hypergeometric probability in the third, and by then it no
longer looks like a counting problem. Forgetting a little and
re-retrieving is not a failure of studying; it *is* the studying.

## The one thing you cannot cram

[[The Culminating Investigation]] is immune to all of the above. It
cannot be retrieved the night before because it was never in your
head — it is in your data, your decisions, and your journal.
Fifteen minutes a week from the moment it launches is genuinely
enough, and it is the only schedule that works. Students who leave it
alone until the final unit do not run out of *ability*; they run out
of the one resource the investigation actually consumes, which is
elapsed time for a dataset to disappoint you and be replaced.

## Notes to your future forgetful self

The notes you write after each thinking task have one reader: you, in
exam week, having forgotten today. Write for that person — one worked
example in your own words (a tree diagram with the *second* branch's
probabilities visibly different from the first, and the sentence
saying why; a two-way table with the conditional probability computed
off it and the words "given that" underlined), a sentence about when
the idea gets used, and the mistake you almost made. Then, when
studying, resist opening them: attempt a problem cold and consult the
notes only to settle the argument between you and your memory. A note
you had to reach for is remembered; a note you re-read is merely
visited.
