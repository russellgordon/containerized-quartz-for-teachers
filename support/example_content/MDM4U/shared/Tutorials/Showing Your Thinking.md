An answer with no visible reasoning is a rumour: it might be true,
but nobody — including you, three weeks later — can tell why. In this
course the reasoning *is* the work. A probability, a summary
statistic, and a conclusion drawn from a sample all live or die on
whether a reader can follow the chain, and showing it is a concrete
skill with named parts.

## The four-part habit on paper

1. **Restate** — one line saying what the problem actually asks, in
   your own words. "Find the chance that at least one of them is
   defective" is a different job from "find the chance that exactly
   one is" — half of all wrong answers are right answers to a misread
   question.
2. **Reason** — the steps in order, with a word about *why* each move
   is legal. "The draws are without replacement, so the second
   probability is conditional on the first" beats a bare arrow. In a
   counting problem the why *is* the product: the sentence saying
   whether order matters is worth more than the number it leads to,
   because the number is a consequence of it.
3. **Say what each number is a number *of*.** This course's version
   of units, and it is stricter. A percentage is a percentage of
   something — name the denominator. A count is a count of rows,
   people, or events — say which. A rate has a "per": per person, per
   year, per thousand. "23% of respondents" and "23% of the school"
   are wildly different claims, and the difference is where most bad
   statistics live.
4. **Answer sentence** — a full sentence a stranger could read on its
   own, carrying its own uncertainty: "Among the 412 students who
   responded, 23% reported walking to school — but the survey was
   given only in first-period classes, so students who arrive late
   are under-represented." A boxed $0.23$ answers nothing by itself.
   [[Writing About Data]] is that sentence, taught properly.

## At the whiteboards

One rule does most of the work: **the person holding the marker
writes only other people's ideas.** It forces reasoning out loud —
you cannot write what nobody has said — so every line on the board
has already survived one listener. And cross out rather than erase: a
corrected line is a record of thinking, which is what the board is
*for*, as [[Mistakes Are Data]] argues at length.

## The checklist

- [ ] Could someone follow this without me standing beside it?
- [ ] Is there a why beside every non-obvious step?
- [ ] Does every number say what it is a number *of*?
- [ ] Does it end in a sentence answering the actual question — and
      admitting what it cannot answer?
- [ ] Are my crossed-out attempts still visible?
