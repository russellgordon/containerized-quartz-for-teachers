Being stuck is not an emergency — in this course it is the expected
state at the start of every thinking task. But there are two kinds of
stuck. **Stuck-and-thinking** is still generating moves; it feels
uncomfortable and is quietly productive. **Stuck-and-spinning** is
rereading the same line while feeling worse; nothing is moving except
the feeling. [[Why Struggle Is the Point]] is the why; this page is
the how — five moves for turning spinning back into thinking.

## Five moves, in rough order

1. **Re-read the problem aloud.** Actually aloud, or at least moving
   your lips. A shocking share of stuckness is a misread question —
   "at least one" is not "exactly one", "and" is not "or", and the
   chance of a positive test given the illness is not the chance of
   the illness given a positive test. The ear catches what the eye
   skims.
2. **Shrink it until you can list it.** Cannot see how many ways
   eight people can sit around a table? Do three people, by hand, on
   paper, all of them. The general argument is nearly always visible
   in the small case, and [[Counting Without Counting]] surrenders to
   whoever shrinks it first. This is the single highest-yield move in
   the counting unit.
3. **Draw it.** A tree diagram for anything sequential, with the
   probabilities written *on the branches* so a changed denominator
   cannot hide. A Venn diagram for anything with overlap. A rough
   scatter plot before any correlation. A two-way table for anything
   conditional — the table does half the thinking, because the
   condition is simply a row you are now living inside.
4. **Ask what size the answer should be.** Every quantity here has
   legal limits: a probability lives between 0 and 1, a correlation
   coefficient between $-1$ and $1$, a standard deviation is never
   negative, and a mean always lies between the smallest and largest
   value. An answer outside its own fence has told you where to look
   before you have looked anywhere.
5. **Explain the stuck-point to someone.** Not "I don't get it" but
   "I got a probability of 1.4, which is impossible, so I think I
   added two outcomes that overlap instead of…". Half the time you
   hear the way forward as it leaves your mouth.

## When to step away

If all five moves are spent and you are spinning, stepping away is a
strategy, not a surrender — brains keep working on problems in the
background, which is why solutions famously arrive in showers. Leave
yourself a note about exactly where it broke, come back after a walk
or a sleep, and if the wall is still solid, that is what
[[Getting Help]] is for. Arriving with a well-described stuck-point
is not weakness; it is the most efficient question a student can ask.

> [!tip] Stuck on the investigation is a different animal
> When [[The Culminating Investigation]] stalls it is rarely a
> mathematical wall — it is usually a question too broad to answer or
> a dataset that cannot support the question you asked. Neither is
> fixed by trying harder. Narrow the question or change the data, and
> write down in your [[Math Journal]] which one you did and why.
> That note becomes a paragraph of your final report.
