The survey your class autopsied looked professional. Real letterhead,
a decent sample size, percentages to one decimal place. It took your
group about four minutes to find the sentence that sank it — a
question phrased so that agreeing was the polite answer — and another
ten to notice something worse: it had been distributed by email to
people who had already signed up for the organization's newsletter.
Neither flaw is arithmetic. Both are fatal.

## Four places bias enters

**Bias** is any systematic tendency for your data to differ from the
truth about the population. Systematic is the operative word. Random
error scatters around the right answer and shrinks as you collect
more; bias points one direction and grows more convincing as you
collect more.

- **Sampling bias** — your method could never have reached part of
  the population. A phone survey of landlines. A survey of "students"
  conducted in the cafeteria at lunch, when a third of the school is
  off-site.
- **Non-response bias** — you reached them and they did not answer,
  and the people who ignore surveys differ from the people who
  complete them. A 12% response rate is not a small sample of
  everyone; it is a full sample of the kind of person who responds.
- **Response bias** — they answered, but not truthfully. People
  under-report drinking and over-report voting, exercising, and
  reading. Anonymity helps. An interviewer standing there does not.
- **Measurement bias** — the instrument itself is off. A scale that
  reads 200 g heavy, a stopwatch started by hand, a rating scale
  running from "good" to "excellent" with nowhere to be unhappy.

> [!example]- One flawed question, four ways
> *"Most experts agree that our school needs a later start time. Do
> you agree?"*
>
> **Leading** — the first sentence tells the respondent what the
> correct opinion is. **Loaded** — "most experts agree" is an
> unsupported appeal to authority doing persuasive work inside a
> measuring instrument. **Forced choice** — agree or what? There is
> no scale, no "no opinion", no room for "only on Mondays".
> **Double-barrelled** in disguise — someone who wants a later start
> but not this proposal has no honest answer available.
>
> A repair: *"What time should the school day begin? 8:00 · 8:30 ·
> 9:00 · 9:30 · No preference."* Shorter, duller, and it measures
> something.

## Wording is data collection

That example is not a curiosity. The wording of a question is part of
the measuring instrument, in the same way a ruler's markings are, and
a question that nudges is a ruler with the numbers moved. Order
matters too: ask about school safety and then about school funding,
and the funding answers shift.

The professional habit is to pilot your survey on five or six people
first and ask them what each question meant to them. You will discover
that at least one question means something you did not intend. This is
the single highest-value hour in any student investigation, and it is
built into [[The Survey Autopsy]] deliberately.

## Ethics is not a separate section

Everyone in your data is a person who agreed — or did not — to be
counted. Before you collect, settle four things and write them down:
what you are asking, who will see it, how you will store it, and how
someone can decline without penalty. Do not collect identifying
information you do not need. Do not ask a question whose answer could
embarrass a respondent to someone who might see the spreadsheet.
Do not collect data on people who have not been told what it is for.

Cultural bias is the version of this that is easiest to miss and
hardest to repair, because it lives in what the question assumes: that
everyone celebrates the same holidays, eats the same meals, has the
same kind of household, reads English comfortably. A question that
does not fit a respondent's life gets an answer that does not fit
either, and that answer enters your data looking exactly like all the
others.

Bias is where a data set stops being neutral, which is why
[[Citing Your Sources]] insists you record *who* collected a secondary
data set and *why*. Being wrong about this is normal and fixable —
[[Mistakes Are Data]] makes the case — but only if you look. Take the
sharpened eye into [[Correlation and Causation]] and into
[[The Statistical Claim Report]].
