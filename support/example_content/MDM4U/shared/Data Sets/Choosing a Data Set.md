The launch class for [[The Culminating Investigation]] runs the same
way every year. Groups arrive with a question they care about, and
about half of them discover within twenty minutes that no data exists
which could possibly answer it. That is not a failure — it is the
first real piece of statistical work in the project, and doing it in
week six is infinitely cheaper than discovering it in week eighteen.
This page is how to do it deliberately.

## The question and the data choose each other

You cannot pick a question in isolation and then go shopping. Nor
should you pick a data set and then ask whatever it happens to answer,
which produces a report with no argument in it. Work both ends toward
the middle: draft a question, look for data, and let what you find
sharpen the question.

A workable question for this course has four properties.

- **It is answerable with numbers.** "Is our school fair?" is not.
  "Do students in different grades report different amounts of
  homework?" is.
- **It names its variables.** Say exactly what will be measured and in
  what units. Vague variables produce vague conclusions.
- **It names its population.** Students at this school, Ontario
  municipalities over 50 000 people, games played by one team this
  season. If you cannot say who the question is about, you cannot say
  who your answer is about either.
- **It leaves room to be wrong.** If you already know the answer, the
  investigation is a demonstration, not an investigation. Genuine
  uncertainty is the point of this course.

Two shapes work particularly well: a **comparison** (does group A
differ from group B?) and a **relationship** (do these two variables
move together, and how strongly?). The first leads to one-variable
summaries compared side by side; the second leads to scatter plots,
$r$, and regression. Both are well within what
[[One-Variable Statistics]] and [[Two-Variable Statistics]] have given
you.

## Primary or secondary

**Primary data** you collect yourself — a survey, an experiment, a
simulation, a set of measurements. You control the variables, you know
exactly how it was gathered, and you own every flaw. It is usually
small, and it takes real time.

**Secondary data** somebody else collected — a public catalogue, a
published study, a league's own statistics. It is usually much larger
and better collected than anything you could manage, but you inherit
decisions you did not make and must go find out what they were.

| | Primary | Secondary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Size | Small — tens, maybe hundreds | Often thousands or more |
| Control | You designed it | You inherit the design |
| Time cost | Collection | Understanding and cleaning |
| Main risk | Bias you introduced | Bias you did not notice |
| Documentation | You write it | You must find and read it |

Either is legitimate. A mixed approach — a public data set plus a
small survey of your own — often makes the strongest report, because
each one gives you something to check the other against.

## Six checks before you commit

Run these before you invest a single hour in analysis.

- [ ] **Enough rows.** Under about 30 observations, most of what this
      course teaches has nothing to grip. More is better, up to the
      point where cleaning becomes the whole project.
- [ ] **The right columns.** Both variables you need, in the same
      rows. Two data sets that "cover the same topic" but cannot be
      matched row by row will not do two-variable analysis.
- [ ] **Usable format.** A spreadsheet or a plain text table you can
      open. A PDF report full of prose is not a data set, and
      retyping 400 numbers by hand introduces its own errors.
- [ ] **Known origin.** You can say who collected it, when, from
      whom, and why. If you cannot, see [[Citing Your Sources]] —
      unknown origin is a reason to walk away.
- [ ] **Documentation exists.** Column names alone are not enough;
      you need the definitions. [[Reading a Data Dictionary]] shows
      what you are looking for.
- [ ] **Ethically clean.** No personal information about identifiable
      people, nothing collected without consent, nothing you would be
      uncomfortable showing the people it describes.

If a candidate fails two or more of these, drop it now. Sunk cost is
the reason weak investigations get finished instead of restarted.

## Then ask who is missing

The last question before you commit is the one this whole course keeps
returning to. Whose numbers are in this data set, and whose are not?
A survey of students answered during class period two misses everyone
with a spare. A municipal open data set covers only municipalities
that chose to publish. A league's statistics cover only players who
got on the field.

Nothing about a missing group announces itself in a spreadsheet. You
have to reason about the collection process — the skill
[[Sampling Techniques]] built — and then write the limitation into
your report while you still remember it. A conclusion that names its
own blind spot is stronger, not weaker, and markers of
[[The Culminating Investigation]] know it.
