A data management course without data is a course about nothing. That
is why this folder exists, and why it appears in the sidebar beside
Concepts and Exercises rather than tucked away as an appendix. Every
technique in this course — every mean, every $r$, every $z$-score —
was invented because somebody had a pile of real numbers and a real
question, and the numbers were messy.

Almost everything here serves one piece of work:
[[The Culminating Investigation]]. That project is not a task bolted
on at the end of the semester; it is a whole strand of this course,
launched in Unit 2 and fed by every unit after. It needs a question
worth asking and data capable of answering it, and finding both is
harder and more interesting than any calculation you will do with
them.

## Start here

[[Choosing a Data Set]] — the decision that determines whether the
rest of your investigation is possible.

[[Where to Find Real Data]] — the public catalogues that actually
exist, named honestly, with how to search them.

## Once you have data

[[Reading a Data Dictionary]] — what the columns mean, and why you
cannot analyse a spreadsheet you have not read the documentation for.

[[Cleaning Messy Data]] — the unglamorous hours nobody warns you
about, and the decisions you must record while you make them.

[[Citing Your Sources]] — where the numbers came from, who collected
them, and why that is a mathematical question and not a formatting
one.

## If you just want to practise

[[Sample Data Sets to Practise On]] — data you can generate or collect
in a single class, plus the kinds of tables the public catalogues
carry, for when you want to try a technique before you commit your
investigation to it.

One habit to carry through all six pages: every data set was collected
by someone, for some purpose, from someone. Ask who is in it, who is
missing, and what the collector wanted to find out.
[[Sampling Techniques]] and [[Bias]] give you the vocabulary; this
folder gives you the practice.
