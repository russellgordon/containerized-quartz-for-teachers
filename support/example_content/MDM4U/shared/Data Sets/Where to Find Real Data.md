Searching the open web for "data about X" gives you a thousand
articles that mention a number and roughly none that hand you a
table. Real data lives in **catalogues** — organized collections built
by agencies whose job is to publish them — and once you know the four
or five that matter, finding data stops being the hard part of an
investigation. This page names the ones that exist. It deliberately
gives you no addresses to type, because catalogue structures change
and a search from the organization's own front page always works.

## Canadian public catalogues

**Statistics Canada** is the national statistical agency and the first
place to look for anything about people in Canada — population,
income, employment, education, health, housing, crime, transport,
agriculture, trade. Its public data tables are searchable by topic and
by geography, and most can be downloaded in spreadsheet form at no
cost. The census is the flagship, but a great deal of the agency's
output comes from ongoing surveys published far more often than every
five years.

**The Government of Canada's open data portal** collects data sets
published by federal departments and agencies under an open licence —
a much wider net than the statistical agency alone, covering things
like environmental monitoring, transport, and government operations.
Search it when your question is about something the federal government
*does* rather than about the population.

**The Ontario Data Catalogue** is the province's equivalent, carrying
data published by Ontario ministries and agencies. Provincial
education, health, and transport data typically live here. Ontario
also publishes provincial assessment results at the school and board
level, which makes them usable secondary data for a school-focused
question.

**Municipal open data portals** exist for many Canadian cities,
including Toronto and Ottawa, and they are the best source for
anything local: transit, permits, service requests, parks, road
safety, recreation programs. Local questions make excellent
investigations precisely because you already understand the context.

**Census at School** is a classroom survey project in which students
answer a questionnaire about themselves and the anonymized results
become available for classroom analysis. It is the rare secondary
source where you understand the collection process completely, because
you can take the survey yourself.

## International sources

**Gapminder** is an independent foundation that assembles long-run
global indicators — income, life expectancy, population, child
mortality, education — from official statistical sources and publishes
them with tools for visualizing change over time. Because it
harmonizes data from many countries and many decades, it is superb for
questions about global patterns and terrible for questions needing
recent precision in one country. It documents its sources, which is
what makes it trustworthy; chase those citations when it matters.

**International organizations** — the World Bank, the OECD, and United
Nations agencies among them — publish large indicator collections
covering their member states. Their reach is enormous and their
coverage is uneven: countries with weaker statistical systems report
less, later, and less reliably, which is itself a fact about who is
missing from the data.

## Data you already have access to

Not everything worth analysing is in a catalogue. Sports leagues
publish official game and player statistics. Schools and boards post
enrolment and calendar information. Anything you can time, count, or
measure yourself is data you fully understand — and a small primary
data set you collected carefully often beats a large secondary one you
half understand. [[Sample Data Sets to Practise On]] is built on
exactly this idea.

## How to search a catalogue

The catalogues are not search engines and do not reward search-engine
habits.

- [ ] Start from the organization's own data or statistics section,
      not from a general web search.
- [ ] Search for the **subject**, not your question. "Commuting", not
      "do students in Ontario commute farther than students in
      Quebec".
- [ ] Filter by geography and by reference year before you read
      anything. Most disappointments are date or coverage
      disappointments.
- [ ] Open the documentation page *before* the download. If there is
      no documentation, treat that as a warning — see
      [[Reading a Data Dictionary]].
- [ ] Prefer a spreadsheet or CSV download over a formatted report.
      A PDF of tables is a picture of data, not data.
- [ ] Record the source the moment you download, while you still know
      what you clicked: [[Citing Your Sources]].

Two distinctions decide whether a file is usable at all.
**Aggregate** data reports totals and averages for groups — a
provincial median income, a city's monthly ridership. **Microdata**
gives one row per individual respondent, with identifying details
removed. Most public catalogue tables are aggregate, and that is fine
for many questions, but you cannot compute a standard deviation of
individuals from a table of averages, and you cannot build a scatter
plot of people from a table of provinces without quietly changing what
your conclusion is about.

Two habits will save you repeatedly. First, an article's chart is not
its data — find the source line, then go to the source. Second, an
encyclopedia table is secondary data about secondary data; follow its
citation to the original agency, because that is the version you can
actually defend in [[The Culminating Investigation]].
