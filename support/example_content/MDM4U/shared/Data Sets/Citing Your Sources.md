A group presented a graph with a striking trend and got a single
question from the floor: *where did the numbers come from?* They did
not know. Somebody had downloaded a file six weeks earlier, the file
was called `data(3).csv`, and the trend — which may well have been
real — became unusable in front of the whole room. Citation is not a
formatting chore attached to the end of a report. It is the part of
your work that makes the rest of it checkable.

## What a citation is actually for

In this course a source line does three jobs, and none of them is
about punctuation.

It lets a reader **verify**. A statistical claim that cannot be traced
to its data is a claim you are asking to be believed on trust, and
this is a course about not doing that.

It lets a reader **judge**. Who collected the data changes how much
weight it carries. A national statistical agency, an industry
association, and an advocacy group can all publish honest numbers, and
all three have different reasons for choosing which numbers to
publish. Naming the collector lets your reader apply the scepticism
that [[Bias]] taught them.

It **dates** the claim. Data has a reference period, and a conclusion
about "today" drawn from a five-year-old table is a different
conclusion than it appears to be.

## What to record, the moment you download

Do this before you analyse anything. Reconstructing it later is
tedious at best and impossible at worst.

- [ ] The **organization** that collected and published the data.
- [ ] The **title of the specific table or data set**, copied exactly
      as the catalogue writes it.
- [ ] The **reference period** — the year or range the data describes,
      which is often not the year it was published.
- [ ] The **date you retrieved it**, because catalogues update.
- [ ] The **address you retrieved it from**, complete.
- [ ] Where the **documentation** lives — see
      [[Reading a Data Dictionary]].
- [ ] Any **conditions of use** the catalogue states, such as an open
      licence requiring attribution.

A useful shape for the finished line, with your own details filled in:

> Organization. (Year of publication). *Title of the table or data
> set*, reference period. Name of the catalogue. Retrieved [date]
> from [address].

Your school may prefer APA, MLA, or a house style; follow it. The
information above is what every style is trying to capture, and
getting the information right matters more than the order of the
commas.

## Judging whether a source is worth citing

Four questions, and you should be able to answer all four before the
data enters your report.

**Authoritative** — does this organization actually collect data, or
is it repeating someone else's? Follow the chain until you reach
whoever measured something, and cite them.

**Reliable** — is the methodology published? A source that will not
tell you how it collected its numbers has told you something
important.

**Unbiased** — who benefits if these numbers are believed? This is not
an accusation, it is a question, and it applies to every source
including the reputable ones. An agency with a mandate publishes on
the topics of its mandate, and the gaps are as informative as the
tables.

**Current** — is the reference period appropriate to your question?
Older data is not worse data; it is data about an older situation, and
that is fine as long as your conclusion says so.

That quartet is the [[D3.2|source-validity expectation]] almost word
for word, and it is the same scan you will run on somebody else's
claim in [[The Statistical Claim Report]]. Running it on your own
sources first is the honest order.

## Citing inside your report

Cite at the point of use, not only in a list at the end. A table, a
graph, and a quoted statistic should each carry a short attribution
right there — reader-friendly, and it stops sources from drifting
away from the numbers they belong to during editing.

Two special cases come up constantly. If you **derived** a value —
computed a rate, combined two columns, converted units — say so, and
cite the underlying table rather than presenting your calculation as
though the agency published it. And if the data is **your own**, cite
it just as carefully: what you collected, from whom, when, and by what
method. Primary data with no methodology attached is exactly as
unverifiable as a mystery download.

Sources you consulted and did not use still belong in the report if
they shaped your thinking — including the data set you rejected, and
why. That paragraph is often the most convincing evidence that a
student did real statistical work, and both
[[The Culminating Investigation]] and [[Writing About Data]] expect to
see it.
