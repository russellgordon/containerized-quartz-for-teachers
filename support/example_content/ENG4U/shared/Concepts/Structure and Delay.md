Structure is the order a writer chose. In *Hamlet* the structure is
mostly delay, and the delay is the subject — which makes the play a
useful case for reading arrangement as argument.

## What to ask of any structure

- **Where does it start**, and what has been left out?
- **What is withheld, and until when?**
- **How is time distributed?** Where does the text slow down?
- **What interrupts?** In *Hamlet*: players, a graveyard, a pirate ship.
  Interruptions are structural claims about what a plot is for.
- **Where does it end** relative to the events it narrates? *The
  Handmaid's Tale* ends twice — once with Offred, once two centuries
  later.

## Delay as a formal device

The revenge plot arrives in Act 1. The revenge happens in Act 5. The
three acts in between are where the play actually lives, and every
account of *Hamlet* has to explain them.

Two readings worth testing against passages: the delay is
characterological (a mind that thinks instead of acting), or the delay is
epistemological (a man who reasonably wants evidence that the ghost is
telling the truth). The first is the traditional reading; the second is
better supported by the text's own language about proof.

## Frames

A frame changes what a text is. The Historical Notes at the end of
*The Handmaid's Tale* convert a testimony into an artefact and a
professor's joke into evidence about the future. Ask, of any frame: what
does it do to the authority of what it contains?
