Atwood has said repeatedly that she included nothing in
*The Handmaid's Tale* that had not already happened somewhere, at some
point, to somebody. Whether or not you find that claim entirely
persuasive, it is the right place to start reading the novel: as an
argument about the present made in the future tense.

## What dystopia is for

| Purpose | Looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **Extrapolation** | Take a trend and run it forward: what if this continues? |
| **Defamiliarisation** | Make the familiar strange enough to be seen |
| **Warning** | This is available; it is not far |
| **Consolation** | The bad thing is fictional, and therefore not here |

The last one is why dystopia is a risky form: a reader can enjoy the
horror precisely because it is not theirs. Ask what the novel does to
resist that.

## Gilead's method

Notice what the regime actually uses: existing scripture, existing law,
existing institutions, and an economy converted in a single afternoon by
freezing accounts. Atwood's argument is not that a new thing will be
built but that the materials are already here — which is why the novel's
most frightening chapters are the flashbacks.

## The Historical Notes

The novel ends with an academic conference two centuries later, where a
professor makes jokes about the material and cautions against judging
Gilead too harshly. Ask: what is that ending doing to the reader who has
just finished the testimony? See
[[Narration and Testimony]] and [[Structure and Delay]].

> [!question] Worth arguing about
> Is a dystopia that is entirely built from historical precedent still
> speculative fiction? Atwood's own answer has changed over the decades,
> which is worth knowing before you cite it.
