Some narrators tell a story. Some give testimony — an account produced
under conditions, for a purpose, with consequences for being believed.
Grade 12 is where the difference becomes the analysis.

## Questions for a narrator

- **When is this being told, and to whom?** Retrospect always adds a
  second voice.
- **Under what conditions?** Testimony given under threat is a different
  document from a memoir written at leisure.
- **What is the narrator's interest?** Not necessarily dishonesty —
  everybody arranges.
- **What does the text know that the narrator does not?**

## *The Handmaid's Tale* as testimony

Offred is not simply unreliable. She is producing an account, on tape,
under a regime that has criminalised her literacy — and the novel keeps
showing the seams: "I made that up. It didn't happen that way." Then the
Historical Notes hand the whole thing to academics who treat it as
material.

That frame is the novel's hardest argument: a testimony's survival
depends on people who may not deserve it.

## Free indirect style and interiority

Whose thoughts do we get, in whose words? A text that grants interiority
to one character and withholds it from another has made an argument about
whose inner life counts — which is a question [[Critical Approaches]]
gives you several ways to ask.

## In drama

A soliloquy is testimony to an audience: unopposed, unverifiable, and
addressed to us. *Hamlet* has seven of them. Ask what it means that the
play's most reliable-seeming voice is the one nobody on stage can check.
