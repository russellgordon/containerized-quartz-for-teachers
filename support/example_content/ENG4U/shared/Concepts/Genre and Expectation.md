Genre is a contract with a reader: certain things are promised, and the
promise can be kept, broken, or renegotiated. A text that breaks a
convention is arguing about it.

## Revenge tragedy, and what *Hamlet* does to it

The Elizabethan revenge play had a shape: a ghost, a wronged survivor, a
delay, a feigned madness, a play-within-a-play, a pile of bodies.
*Hamlet* has all six — and spends its length examining the man in the
middle rather than the machinery around him.

Ask, of any convention the play keeps: what does keeping it cost, and
what would be lost if it were cut?

## Dystopia, and what *The Handmaid's Tale* does to it

Dystopia promises a totalising system and a protagonist who resists it.
Atwood keeps the system and complicates the resistance: Offred is not a
revolutionary, she is a person trying to survive, and the novel refuses
to reward the reader who wants her to be braver.

See [[Dystopia and the Present Tense]] for the other half of that
argument — the claim Atwood makes about invention.

## The question worth asking of both

**What does the genre let this text assume, and what does it therefore
never have to argue?** That question produces better essays than a list
of conventions ever will.
