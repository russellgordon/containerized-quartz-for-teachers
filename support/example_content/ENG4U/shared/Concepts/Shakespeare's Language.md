The method is on [[Reading Shakespeare]]. This page is what to notice in
*Hamlet* specifically.

## Verse, prose, and Hamlet's switching

Hamlet moves between verse and prose more than any other Shakespearean
protagonist, and the switches track his audience: verse when he is alone
or with the court, prose with the players, with Rosencrantz and
Guildenstern, and in the graveyard.

Ask, each time: who is he talking to, and what is the prose doing that
verse could not?

## The soliloquies

Seven of them, and they do not repeat. Each begins with a different
problem and ends in a different place — one of them in a plan, one in
self-disgust, one in an argument he loses to himself. Track the last two
lines of each; the pattern they make is a whole essay.

## Rhetoric worth naming

| Device | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Antithesis** | "To be or not to be" — the play's basic shape |
| **Hendiadys** | Two nouns joined where one modified noun would do: "the book and volume of my brain". *Hamlet* is saturated with it, and the effect is a mind that cannot leave a phrase alone |
| **Stichomythia** | Rapid single-line exchange; used in the closet scene as combat |
| **Aposiopesis** | Breaking off mid-sentence; grief and evasion both |
| **Wordplay** | Hamlet's puns are weapons and evasions at once: "a little more than kin, and less than kind" |

## Imagery clusters

Disease and rot; ears and poison; acting and seeming; gardens
uncultivated. Track one across five acts, per [[Structure and Delay]],
and notice that the ear-poison image is both the murder method and the
play's account of how corruption spreads.
