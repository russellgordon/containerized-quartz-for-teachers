Criticism is not opinion with vocabulary. A critical approach is a set of
questions with a history, and knowing where the questions came from is
part of using them honestly.

## Six approaches, in one line each

| Approach | Central question | Where it came from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Formalist** | What does the text do, in itself? | Early twentieth-century reaction against biography-as-criticism |
| **Feminist** | How does the text construct gender and whose interiority does it grant? | 1960s onward, and continually revised since |
| **Postcolonial** | Who is centred, who is spoken for, whose land and language? | Mid-century decolonisation, and in Canada, ongoing |
| **Marxist / materialist** | Who works, who owns, and what does the text naturalise? | Nineteenth-century political economy, applied to culture |
| **Psychoanalytic** | What is repressed, displaced, repeated? | Freud, heavily revised; useful when a text will not say something |
| **Reader-response** | What does the text ask its reader to do? | 1970s; the insight that meaning happens in the reading |

## They are not equally useful on every text

*Hamlet* rewards psychoanalytic and formalist reading; it also rewards a
materialist question about who inherits a kingdom.
*The Handmaid's Tale* was written inside feminist criticism and argues
with it. A lens that produces nothing on a given text has told you
something about the text.

## Using them without becoming them

The method is on [[Using a Critical Lens]]. The short version: the lens
produces the question, the passage produces the answer, and the reading
has to survive the passage that resists it.

> [!question] Worth arguing about
> Is a reading through a lens *of* the text, or *about* the lens? Take a
> specific case — Ophelia's madness read three ways — and argue it. The
> best answers in this room have concluded "both, and the ratio is the
> difference between criticism and ventriloquism".
