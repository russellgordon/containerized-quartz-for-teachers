Characters are not people, and treating them as people is the fastest way
to a weak essay. They are constructions doing work in a text — and asking
what work is the analytical move.

## Four questions

1. **What does this character make visible** that could not be shown
   otherwise?
2. **What do they want, and what does the text think of that wanting?**
3. **Who are they placed beside?** A foil is an argument by comparison.
4. **What are they denied?** A character with no soliloquy, no name, or
   no last scene has been denied it on purpose.

## Worked example

Laertes is given the same situation as Hamlet — a murdered father, a
duty, a court — and acts immediately. That is not a personality note; it
is the play controlling a variable. Every account of Hamlet's delay has
to survive Laertes, who proves that the situation alone does not produce
paralysis.

Write that sentence and you have a thesis.

## In prose

In [[The Handmaid's Tale]], ask what Serena Joy makes visible that no
other character can: a woman who campaigned for the arrangement that has
now silenced her. The novel could have made her a victim or a monster;
what it does instead is the argument.

## Avoiding the two traps

- **Psychologising.** "Hamlet is depressed" may even be defensible, but
  it has to be argued from language on the page rather than diagnosed.
- **Moralising.** "Claudius is evil" ends the analysis at the point it
  should start.
