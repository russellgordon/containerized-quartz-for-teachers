An ambiguous text supports more than one reading. An indeterminate one
refuses to settle. Grade 12 is where the difference becomes worth
arguing about, because the strongest essays are usually about texts that
will not resolve.

## Testing an ambiguity

1. Can you build both readings out of quotations?
2. Does each account for the ending?
3. Is there a passage that only one reading can explain?

If the answer to the third is yes, you have a preferred reading and an
honest complication — which is exactly the shape a Grade 12 essay wants.

## Cases from this course

- **The ghost.** Spirit, demon, or hallucination? The play supplies
  evidence for all three and a scene in which four people see it.
- **Hamlet's madness.** Feigned, real, or feigned and then real. Track
  who is present each time.
- **Ophelia's songs.** Nonsense, testimony, or accusation.
- **Offred's ending.** Escape, capture, or the novel refusing to tell us
  because a testimony ends where the tape does.

## Writing about it without hedging

Weak: *There are many interpretations and it is up to the reader.*

Strong: *The play stages the ghost's reliability as a question and then
never answers it, which is why the Mousetrap scene is structurally
necessary: Hamlet builds an experiment because the text has made
certainty unavailable to him.*

Take a position. Acknowledge the other. Refusing to choose is not
sophistication.
