Every performance of a play is an adaptation, and every film of a novel
is an argument about it. Studying them is not a break from literary
analysis; it is the same analysis with more variables.

## What an adaptation decides

| Decision | The argument it makes |
| --- | --- |
| **What to cut** | What the text is *about*, in the adapter's reading |
| **Casting** | Who is allowed to be this character, and what the audience will assume |
| **Setting and period** | Whether the text's problem is historical or current |
| **Camera and framing** | Where the audience stands in relation to power |
| **What is shown that the text only reports** | Every offstage event brought on stage is a claim |
| **Score and silence** | The emotional instruction the words do not give |

## Reading two versions of one scene

The most productive exercise in this unit: take one scene, watch two
productions, and build a table of differences. Then ask, for each: what
reading of the play does this choice serve, and what does it cost?

*Hamlet* is unusually well served here — the soliloquies have been
staged to camera, to the audience, and to nobody, and each is a different
argument about what a soliloquy is.

## Media beyond adaptation

The same questions apply to any text made for an audience — a
documentary, a campaign, a trailer, a magazine profile:

1. Who made it, for whom, and with what interest?
2. What is the claim, stated as its makers would state it?
3. What techniques carry it?
4. What is left out, and who would have said it?

## The trap

Treating the film as evidence about the play. A production is evidence
about a *reading* of the play. Say "Branagh's Hamlet does X", not
"Hamlet does X", when the X is on screen and not on the page.
