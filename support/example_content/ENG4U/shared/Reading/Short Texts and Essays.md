The first unit reads short texts through different lenses, so that by the
time we reach a five-act play everybody has practised the method on
something that takes twenty minutes.

| Text | Writer | The lens it rewards first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "The Yellow Wallpaper" | Charlotte Perkins Gilman (1892) | Feminist, then psychoanalytic — and the argument between them |
| "The Boat" | Alistair MacLeod | Materialist: who works, who leaves, and what it costs |
| "A Short History of Indians in Canada" | Thomas King | Postcolonial, and the question of who the joke is aimed at |
| "Girl" | Jamaica Kincaid | Formalist: one sentence, two voices, a whole upbringing |
| An essay by Thomas King, from *The Truth About Stories* | Thomas King | Reader-response: what the text asks a listener to do |

> [!warning] Content
> "The Yellow Wallpaper" depicts a woman's mental collapse under
> medical control. "The Boat" ends in a death.

## How we read them

Read first without a lens, per [[Using a Critical Lens]]. Then take the
lens the text keeps handing you, and then a second one deliberately —
because the point of the unit is to see what each approach makes visible
and what it costs.

## What the unit establishes

That a reading is an argument, that a lens is a tool rather than a
verdict, and that the strongest essays in this course will be the ones
that name what their own approach cannot see.

## What it feeds

[[The Lens Essay]] — one text, two lenses, 1,000 words.
