A citation answers one question: *where would I go to check this?*

## In the sentence

Name the source where you use it — it reads better than a bracket and it
tells the reader how much weight the claim carries.

> A 2023 report from Statistics Canada found that…

For the texts we study, cite by act, scene, and line for drama and by
page for prose:

> Juliet calls her name "the enemy" of Romeo (2.2.38).
> Lisamarie hears the b'gwus story from her uncle (Robinson 8).

## In the Works Cited list

Alphabetical, at the end.

**A play in a single edition**
> Shakespeare, William. *Hamlet*. Edited by Roma Gill, Oxford
> University Press, 2001.

**A novel**
> Robinson, Eden. *Monkey Beach*. Alfred A. Knopf Canada, 2000.

**An article on a website**
> Nguyen, Thanh. "What the Storm Took." *The Globe and Mail*, 3 Feb.
> 2024, theglobeandmail.com/canada/storm-took.

**A work of non-fiction**
> King, Thomas. *The Inconvenient Indian: A Curious Account of Native
> People in North America*. Doubleday Canada, 2012.

**A secondary source in a journal**
> Fee, Margery. "Writing Orality." *Canadian Literature*, no. 224, 2015,
> pp. 12–29.

## Three habits that prevent the whole problem

1. Record the source the moment you use it, not at the end.
2. Write one sentence in your own words beside every source you keep.
3. If you cannot tell whose words they are, they are not yours yet —
   close the tab and write from memory.

> [!important] Academic honesty, plainly
> Using words or ideas as though you produced them. That includes
> pasting, rewording somebody's argument without naming them, and having
> a model write a passage. The response here is a rewrite, done properly,
> with me — because a mark for work that is not yours describes nobody.
