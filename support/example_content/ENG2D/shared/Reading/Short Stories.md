Five stories in the first unit, read closely and argued about. They are
here because each one does something different with the same small form.

| Story | Writer | Why this one |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "A Secret Lost in the Water" | Roch Carrier (Quebec) | A father, a son, and a skill that does not survive the move to the city |
| "The Leaving" | Budge Wilson (Nova Scotia) | A mother leaves for one night, and the whole family arrangement becomes visible |
| "Borders" | Thomas King | A border crossing where the entire conflict is one word, repeated |
| "The Sniper" | Liam O'Flaherty (Ireland) | Two pages, one civil war, one last sentence |
| "The Painted Door" | Sinclair Ross (Saskatchewan) | Isolation, weather as pressure, and an ending readers still argue about |

> [!warning] Content
> "The Sniper" and "The Painted Door" both end in a death. "The Leaving"
> deals with a family in which a mother has been treated as staff.

## How we read them

Three passes, per [[Close Reading]]. Then one of these questions each
time:

- Where does the story start, and what has been left out?
- Whose perspective organises it, and who is described rather than heard?
- What is repeated?
- What does the last sentence do to the first?

## What the five have in common

Very little, which is the point. By the end of the unit you should be
able to say what a short story *can* do that a novel cannot: compression,
a single turn, and an ending that reorganises everything before it.

## The task

They feed [[The Story Behind the Story]] — you write one, and then write
about the choices you made.
