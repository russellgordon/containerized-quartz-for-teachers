## The question

Can a reading of a text be wrong? If so, what makes it wrong — and who
gets to say?

## Start here

Three readings of the same moment in *Romeo and Juliet*, all offered in
this room in previous years:

1. The Friar is the villain: every disaster follows one of his
   concealments.
2. The Friar is the only adult who takes the children seriously, and the
   plague, not the plan, kills them.
3. The Friar is beside the point; the play is about a city that arms its
   teenagers.

## Positions to consider

| Position | The argument | The objection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The text decides | Evidence settles it; a reading contradicted by the text is wrong | Texts under-determine meaning constantly, which is why good critics disagree |
| The writer decides | Intention is the anchor | We usually cannot know it, and writers are not always right about their own work |
| The reader decides | Meaning happens in the reading, not on the page | Then no reading can be criticised, and criticism is what we are doing |
| The community of readers decides | Interpretations are tested by argument, and some survive | Which community? Communities have been wrong for centuries at a time |

## The Indigenous-writing complication

When we read [[Moon of the Crusted Snow]], "any reading is as good as any
other" collides with something real: a reading that ignores what the
novel says about Anishinaabe survival, or that treats the community as a
metaphor for something else, is not just different — it is doing the
thing the book is about. Bring that into the discussion rather than
around it.

## Ground rules for today

- Every claim comes with a passage.
- "That is just your opinion" is not an argument. Say what the evidence
  fails to support.

## Afterwards

One paragraph: the strongest reading you heard that was not yours, and
what it would take to convince you of it.
