Shakespeare is not hard because the ideas are hard. He is hard because
the syntax is inverted, the vocabulary has drifted, and it was written to
be heard rather than read. All three are fixable.

## Six moves that work

1. **Read it aloud.** Always. The verse tells you where to pause and the
   sense arrives with the sound.
2. **Follow the punctuation, not the line breaks.** Read to the full
   stop. Ending a thought at the end of a line is the commonest reason
   a speech becomes gibberish.
3. **Unscramble the syntax.** *"What light through yonder window
   breaks?"* → *what light breaks through that window over there?*
   Subject, verb, object: find them and reorder.
4. **Check the words that look familiar.** *Wherefore* means *why*, not
   *where*. *Nice* meant fussy. The false friends do more damage than the
   words you know you do not know.
5. **Paraphrase in your own words, line by line**, in the margin — for
   the speeches that matter. Twenty lines takes six minutes.
6. **Watch a performance of the scene after you read it**, never before.
   Two different productions is better still: the differences are
   interpretation made visible.

## The verse, briefly

Most of the play is **iambic pentameter**: ten syllables, five beats,
*ba-DUM* five times.

> But SOFT, what LIGHT through YON-der WIN-dow BREAKS?

Two things to do with that:

- **When the rhythm breaks, something is happening.** A short line is a
  silence; an extra syllable is a character overflowing.
- **Prose versus verse.** Shakespeare switches. Notice who speaks in
  which, and when a character changes — it is never decoration.

## Rhyme

A rhyming couplet usually closes a scene or a thought — a full stop you
can hear. And when Romeo and Juliet first meet, their shared lines form a
sonnet between them. Nobody in the audience is told that; it is the
scene's argument delivered as structure.

> [!tip] The two-column method for a hard speech
> Left column: the text, line by line. Right column: your own words. Do
> not summarise the whole speech — do it line by line, and where you
> cannot, mark it and bring it to class. That mark is a better use of a
> period than a summary you found online.
