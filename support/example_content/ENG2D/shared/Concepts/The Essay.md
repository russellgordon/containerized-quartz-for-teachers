An essay is an argument with evidence, arranged so a reader can follow
it. Everything else — the five-paragraph shape, the hook, the restated
thesis — is scaffolding you are now allowed to take down.

## What it needs

1. **A thesis that could be argued with**, per [[Theme and Thesis]].
2. **Body paragraphs that each prove one part of it**, in an order you
   chose and can defend.
3. **Evidence quoted and analysed**, not listed.
4. **The strongest counter-reading, addressed.** At Grade 10 this is what
   separates a level 3 from a level 4.
5. **A conclusion that goes somewhere** — the consequence of having been
   right, not a summary of the middle.

## Structuring the body

Three orders, each defensible:

| Order | Use when |
| --- | --- |
| Weakest to strongest | You want to build; the last point lands hardest |
| Chronological through the text | The argument is about development or change |
| By technique | The thesis is about how the writer works rather than what happens |

Say which you chose in your outline. An essay whose order is accidental
reads as accidental.

## The introduction, honestly

Two jobs: make the reader want to continue, and tell them what you are
arguing. It does not need a quotation about the human condition. A
concrete observation about the text is a better opening than any general
truth.

> Nobody in *Romeo and Juliet* is left alone for long. The play's few
> soliloquies belong almost entirely to its two teenagers — and the
> adults, who never get one, are the reason the tragedy happens.

## Before you hand it in

- [ ] Read it aloud, whole. The ear finds what the eye skips.
- [ ] Check every quotation against the text, character by character.
- [ ] Check every citation format against [[Citing Sources]].
- [ ] Cover the body and reread the thesis: does the essay you wrote
      prove the claim you made?
