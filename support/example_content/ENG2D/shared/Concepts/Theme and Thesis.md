A **theme** is what a text says about a topic. A **thesis** is what *you*
say about the text. They are related and they are not the same, and
confusing them is the commonest reason a Grade 10 essay stalls.

## From topic to theme

| | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Topic | Loyalty |
| Theme | *Romeo and Juliet* treats loyalty to family as indistinguishable from obedience, and every character who tries to separate them dies |
| Moral | "Family is important" — a lesson, not a reading, and the play is not offering it |

A theme statement must be arguable, drawn from the text, and survivable
against the ending.

## From theme to thesis

A thesis is a claim about how the text works. It usually names a
technique and an effect:

> Shakespeare gives Juliet the vocabulary of law and property in a scene
> everybody remembers as romantic, so that the audience hears the
> obstacle before the lovers do.

Notice: arguable, specific, and it tells the reader what the essay will
be doing. See [[The Essay]] for what follows from it.

## Testing a thesis

- **Could somebody intelligent disagree?** If not, it is a summary.
- **Can you prove it with three passages?** If not, it is a hunch.
- **Does the ending complicate it?** If it does, say so in the essay —
  handling the complication is what a level 4 looks like.

> [!tip] Where theses come from
> Not from the first idea. From a pattern in your annotations: a word
> that recurs, a scene that behaves differently from the others, a
> character who is never given a soliloquy. Read your marks, per
> [[Close Reading]], and the thesis is usually already there.
