The practical method for getting through the verse is on
[[Reading Shakespeare]]. This page is what to *notice* once you can read
it.

## Verse and prose

Most of the play is in blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter. Some of
it is in prose. The switch is meaningful:

- Nobles and lovers usually speak verse; servants usually speak prose.
- A character who switches has changed state. Watch Romeo before and
  after the balcony.
- Prose in a serious scene often carries the joke or the threat.

## When the rhythm breaks

Ten syllables, five beats, is the default. A departure is a signal:

- **A short line** is a silence — an actor's pause built into the text.
- **A shared line** (two characters completing one line of verse) shows
  people finishing each other's thoughts. Romeo and Juliet do this
  constantly; Romeo and his father never do.
- **A trochaic opening** — a stressed syllable where an unstressed one
  belongs — is an interruption in the middle of a calm speech.

## Rhyme

Rhyming couplets close scenes and speeches, like an audible full stop.
And the first exchange between Romeo and Juliet forms a shared sonnet:
fourteen lines, the rhyme scheme intact, split between two speakers who
have just met. Nobody announces it. The structure is the argument — they
are already writing the same poem.

## Wordplay

Puns are everywhere and they are not decoration: they are how characters
compete, flirt, and insult. Mercutio's punning is a way of refusing to be
serious, right up until the moment he cannot.

> [!tip] The habit that unlocks the play
> When a speech feels flat, ask what the actor would *do* while saying
> it. Language in a play is action — a threat, a plea, an evasion. Name
> the action and the meaning follows.
