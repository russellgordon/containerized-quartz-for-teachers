A play is not a novel with the descriptions removed. It is a script for
an event, and reading one means constantly asking what an audience would
see and hear.

## The parts

| Term | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Act / scene** | The structural divisions; a scene change is a change of place or time |
| **Dialogue** | What characters say to each other |
| **Soliloquy** | A character alone, thinking aloud — the audience gets the inside |
| **Aside** | A line to the audience while others are on stage |
| **Monologue** | A long speech to other characters |
| **Stage direction** | An instruction; in Shakespeare, mostly implied by the dialogue |
| **Dramatic irony** | The audience knows what a character does not — see [[Irony]] |
| **Comic relief** | A funny scene inside a serious play, usually raising the tension rather than releasing it |

## Tragedy, as this play uses it

A tragedy follows a protagonist to a catastrophe that their own choices
help produce. Two arguments worth having about *Romeo and Juliet*:

- **Is it a tragedy of character or of circumstance?** How much is haste
  and how much is a plague quarantine and an undelivered letter?
- **Whose tragedy is it?** The play is named for two teenagers; the
  Prologue blames the parents.

## Reading a scene as a director

Ask, for every scene: who is on stage, who wants what, where is the
audience's attention, and what is the last image before the lights go
down. Those four questions produce better essays than any amount of
theme-hunting, and they are exactly what [[The Scene, Staged]] assesses.

## Performance is interpretation

Two productions of the same scene are two arguments about the play. When
we watch, we watch for choices: what was cut, who was cast, where it was
set, what the director did with silence. Nobody stages a neutral
*Romeo and Juliet*, and the pretence that there is one is worth
attacking.
