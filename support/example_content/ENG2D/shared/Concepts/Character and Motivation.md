At this level, "character analysis" means motivation: not what somebody
is like, but why they do the specific thing they do at the specific
moment they do it.

## The four questions

1. **What do they want in this scene?** Scene by scene, wants change.
2. **What is in the way?**
3. **What do they do about it** — and what does that cost?
4. **What do they refuse to do?** The refusals define a character faster
   than the actions.

## Characterisation, direct and indirect

Direct: the text tells you. Indirect: you infer it from speech, action,
appearance, thought, and the reactions of others. Strong analysis lives
almost entirely in the indirect, because that is where a reader can be
shown to have read.

## Foils

A foil is a character placed beside another to make something visible.
Mercutio is Romeo's foil: same age, same city, opposite relationship to
language and to love. Every trait of Romeo's that matters is easier to
name once Mercutio is standing next to him.

Ask of any pair: **what does this one make me see about that one?**

## Motivation in a play versus a novel

A novel can tell you what a character thinks. A play, mostly, cannot —
so motivation has to be built out of what is said, in front of whom, and
what is done immediately afterwards. Soliloquies are the exception, which
is exactly why they matter: they are the only time the audience is
trusted with the inside.

> [!example] Motivation, written well
> Weak: *Tybalt is angry and violent.*
> Strong: *Tybalt reaches for a sword before he reaches for a sentence.
> Every one of his scenes begins with somebody else speaking and ends
> with him refusing to answer in words — which is why the play can kill
> him off in a single line and lose nothing.*
