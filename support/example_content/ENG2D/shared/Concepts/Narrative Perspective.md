Who is telling this, how much can they see, and how much should we
believe them? Three questions, and the third is where the analysis is.

## The options

| Perspective | Access | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First person | One mind, from inside | Intimacy, and doubt: we only get their version |
| Third limited | One mind, from outside | Closeness with room for irony |
| Third omniscient | All minds | Scope, and the risk of distance |
| Third objective | No minds | The reader does all the inferring |
| Second person | Puts you in it | Rare, unsettling, hard to sustain |

## Reliability

Ask: does the narrator's account match what other characters do? Do they
insist on something too often? Would somebody else in the story tell it
the same way?

An unreliable narrator turns reading into judging, which is the most
advanced thing a reader does.

## Focalisation, briefly

Even in third person, a text usually *sees* through somebody. In
[[Moon of the Crusted Snow]] the story stays close to Evan; what he does
not know, we do not know. That choice is why the collapse arrives as
rumour and silence rather than as explanation — and it is a choice, not a
limitation.

## Perspective in drama

A play has no narrator, so perspective is built out of who is on stage,
who is absent, and who gets a soliloquy. Ask which characters never get
to speak alone. In *Romeo and Juliet*, the answer is worth a paragraph.
