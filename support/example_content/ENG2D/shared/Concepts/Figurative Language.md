Figurative language says one thing in terms of another. Naming a device
is worth nothing on its own; saying what it does is the whole game.

| Device | What it is | What to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simile | Comparison with *like* or *as* | What does the comparison import? |
| Metaphor | One thing *is* another | What is claimed by making it identity rather than likeness? |
| Personification | Human qualities to the non-human | What does the text want us to treat as having intent? |
| Hyperbole | Deliberate exaggeration | Whose exaggeration — the writer's or the character's? |
| Understatement | Deliberate flattening | Almost always ironic; what is being held back? |
| Oxymoron | Two contradictory words joined | Where a character's mind is in two places at once |
| Paradox | A statement that contradicts itself and is true | Usually the text's central problem, compressed |
| Allusion | A reference to another text or event | What does it borrow, and does the borrowing hold? |
| Imagery | Language aimed at the senses | Which sense, and what pattern do the images make? |

## The oxymoron test case

Romeo's early speeches are stuffed with them: "brawling love", "loving
hate", "heavy lightness". A weak paragraph names them. A strong one
notices that they cluster while he is in love with Rosaline and largely
vanish afterwards — the device is characterising a *pose*, not a passion.

## Motifs

A **motif** is a repeated image or idea that accumulates meaning: light
and dark, poison and medicine, hands. See [[Symbol and Motif]] for how to
track one properly.

> [!warning] The device hunt
> A paragraph listing five devices explains none of them. Take one, quote
> four words, and spend three sentences on what it does. That is the
> paragraph that earns the mark.
