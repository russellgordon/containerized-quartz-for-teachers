Irony is a gap: between what is said and what is meant, between what is
expected and what happens, or between what a character knows and what we
know. Naming which kind is the first move; explaining what the gap does
is the analysis.

## The three kinds

| Kind | The gap | Example shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Verbal** | Between what is said and what is meant | "A plague o' both your houses" — a curse that the play then grants |
| **Situational** | Between what is expected and what happens | A plan designed to save two lives that kills them |
| **Dramatic** | Between what a character knows and what the audience knows | We know Juliet is alive; Romeo does not |

## Dramatic irony is structural

A play that opens by telling you the ending — as *Romeo and Juliet* does
— has made dramatic irony its whole engine. Every hopeful line in Act 2
is heard by an audience that already knows, and that gap is the source of
the discomfort the play is built to produce.

Ask, at any moment: **what do I know that they do not, and what is the
play doing with that?**

## Irony versus coincidence

Not every unlucky event is ironic. A messenger delayed by an outbreak of
plague is bad luck; a sleeping potion designed to imitate death that
causes a real one is irony, because the outcome is the opposite of the
intention that produced it. Keep the distinction — it is exactly what a
marker is checking.

## Satire, briefly

Satire uses irony to criticise. It says the opposite of what it means, or
takes a position to an absurd conclusion, in order to attack it. When we
read advertising and opinion writing in the media unit, the question
"is this satire or is it sincere?" turns out to be genuinely hard, which
is its own lesson.
