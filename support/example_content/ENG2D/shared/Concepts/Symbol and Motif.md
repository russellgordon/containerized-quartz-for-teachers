A **symbol** is an object or image that carries meaning beyond itself. A
**motif** is a symbol that recurs, accumulating meaning as it goes.

## How to tell a symbol from a thing

Three tests:

1. **Repetition.** Once is a prop; four times is a pattern.
2. **Placement.** It turns up at moments the text has marked as
   important.
3. **Weight.** The language around it gets more careful.

If a candle appears in one scene, it is light. If characters keep
carrying candles, and every one of them goes out at a moment of decision,
the play is doing something.

## Tracking a motif properly

Take light and dark in *Romeo and Juliet*. Do not collect quotations —
build a table:

| Where | What form | Who says it | What it means there |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.1 | Romeo's "day's sight" | Romeo | Misery, self-dramatised |
| 1.5 | "she doth teach the torches" | Romeo | Juliet as the source of light |
| 2.2 | Juliet as the sun | Romeo | Light as truth, not decoration |
| 3.5 | Dawn as the enemy | Both | The motif reverses |

The reversal in the fourth row is the paragraph. A motif that changes
meaning is the text arguing.

## In prose

The same method: in [[Moon of the Crusted Snow]], track the generator,
the store shelves, and the language of "the end of the world" — who says
it, and who declines to.

> [!tip] The trap
> Do not decide what something symbolises and then hunt for evidence.
> Track it first, then say what the pattern shows. The direction matters:
> the second produces readings that survive an argument.
