Every text is made by somebody from somewhere. **Perspective** is that
position. **Bias** is when the position distorts the account. Confusing
the two produces two bad habits: dismissing everything as biased, or
believing anything that sounds neutral.

## Where perspective shows up

- **Selection.** What is included and what is left out.
- **Emphasis.** What comes first, what is repeated, what gets the
  headline.
- **Language.** *Protester* or *rioter*. *Migrant* or *expat*. Both are
  choices, and both are arguments.
- **Framing.** Which question is treated as the question.
- **Sources.** Who is quoted, who is described, and who is not asked.

## Reading for it, without cynicism

1. Identify the position: who made this and where do they stand?
2. Ask what the account gets right despite its position — most biased
   accounts are not fabrications.
3. Find the account from another position and compare *selection*, not
   just tone.
4. Decide what you can conclude, and say what you cannot.

## In literature

Bias is not only a media word. A novel narrated by one character is one
account; a play that gives soliloquies to two people and to nobody else
has taken a position on whose interior matters. Ask, of any text: **whose
perspective organises this, and who is described rather than heard?**

## Your own

The hardest application. A thesis you want to be true gets less scrutiny
from you than one you resist, which is exactly backwards. The fix is
mechanical: write the strongest objection to your own claim before you
finish the draft, per [[The Essay]].
