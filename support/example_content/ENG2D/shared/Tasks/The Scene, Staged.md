> [!abstract] At a glance
> Groups of three to five · four class periods, then performed ·
> a staged scene of 60–100 lines plus a one-page director's note ·
> assessed on Communication, Thinking, and Application

## The task

Take one scene of *Romeo and Juliet*, decide what it means, and stage it
so the audience can see your decision. Then defend every choice in
writing.

## What "staging" means here

You are not required to act well. You are required to make choices and
execute them:

- **Cuts.** You may cut lines. Every cut is an argument about what the
  scene is for.
- **Blocking.** Where people stand, who moves toward whom, who is left
  alone on stage.
- **Delivery.** Which words carry the meaning — see
  [[Shakespeare's Language]] for how the verse tells you.
- **Setting and props**, if you want them. A scene set now is a claim
  about the play's relevance; a scene set in 1595 is also a claim.
- **Silence.** The most under-used tool available to you.

## The director's note

One page, and it is where most of the marks are:

1. **Your reading of the scene** in one sentence.
2. **Three choices you made** and what each one shows.
3. **One line you cut** and the argument for cutting it.
4. **What you would do differently with another two weeks.**

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Communication | The audience follows the scene; the verse is spoken so the sense arrives |
| Thinking | The staging is an interpretation, defended in the note with evidence from the text |
| Application | The choices use what we learned about dramatic form, not just enthusiasm |

## If performing frightens you

There is a role in every group that is not on stage: director, dramaturg
(the person who works out what the lines mean), or designer. All three
are assessed on the same note. Come and talk to me in the first period of
the unit.
