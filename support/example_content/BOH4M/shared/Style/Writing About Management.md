Every piece of writing in this course is judged as management writing:
could a busy decision-maker act on it?

## The four moves

**1. Lead with the decision.** Your reader wants to know what you want
before they want to know why. This is the opposite of an English essay
and it takes practice to stop building to a conclusion.

**2. Separate what you saw from what you concluded.** "Nine of
twenty-two staff left last year" is an observation. "Morale is poor" is a
conclusion. Both belong in the writing; confusing them is the most common
weakness in submitted work.

**3. Name the cost.** Every recommendation costs something. Writing that
admits the cost reads as trustworthy; writing that does not reads as
sales.

**4. Say how you would know.** What would you measure, when, and what
result would make you change course?

## Sentence-level habits

| Instead of | Write | Because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "It could be argued that" | "I recommend" or "The evidence suggests" | Own the claim or drop it |
| "Very significant" | The number | Adjectives are cheap; figures are not |
| "The company should try to improve communication" | "Weekly stand-ups, run by the team lead, from 6 January" | The first cannot be done on Monday |
| "Employees were unhappy" | "Eleven of nineteen rated workload 2 or below" | One is a mood, one is evidence |

## Vocabulary

Use the field's words precisely: *span of control*, *retrenchment*,
*stakeholder*, *psychological contract*, *progressive discipline*. Each
means one thing. Using them loosely is worse than not using them, because
your reader will assume the technical meaning.

And keep the rest plain. Business writing is full of language that exists
to sound serious; it does not, and it makes work harder to check.

> [!tip] The test before you hand anything in
> Give it to someone who has not taken this course and ask them what you
> want the organization to do. If they cannot tell you in one sentence,
> the writing is not finished — see [[Writing for Business]].
