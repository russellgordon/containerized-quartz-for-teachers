Slides are for the audience, not for you. Every rule below follows from
that.

## Before you open the software

Write the **one sentence** you want remembered, then the three points
that support it. That is your deck. Software first is how thirty-slide
decks happen.

## The rules that matter

| Rule | Why |
| --- | --- |
| One idea per slide | The audience reads the whole slide the moment it appears; give them one thing |
| Six words in the title, and make it a claim | "Turnover, 2023–2025" is a label; "Turnover doubled after the schedule change" is a finding |
| No paragraph you intend to read out | They read faster than you speak, then stop listening |
| A chart needs a sentence | Say what it shows and why it matters; a chart alone is homework for the audience |
| Cite the source on the slide | It is the first question, every time |
| Dark text on a light background, large | Rooms have windows |

## Structure for a management briefing

1. **The recommendation**, on the first content slide. Managers listen
   for it and cannot hear anything else until they have it.
2. **The evidence**, two or three slides.
3. **The cost and the risks**, one slide, honest.
4. **What happens next**, one slide with dates and names.
5. Stop. Take questions.

## Rehearsing

Twice, out loud, standing, with a timer. Once alone and once with someone
who will interrupt. Almost all timing disasters are decks that were only
ever read silently.

> [!warning] Do not put your notes on the slides
> If the deck has to work without you, it is a document — write a
> document. A deck that works alone and a deck that supports a speaker
> are different objects, and trying to be both produces neither.
