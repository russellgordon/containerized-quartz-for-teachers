A manager's week runs on a small set of tools, and most of the difference
between a smooth week and a chaotic one is whether the right work is in
the right tool.

## What each kind of tool is for

| Tool | It is good at | It is bad at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A document | Thinking something through properly | Anything urgent |
| A spreadsheet | Numbers you will change and re-check | Being a database |
| Email | A record of what was agreed with someone outside the room | A conversation |
| Chat | A conversation | A record of what was agreed |
| A shared calendar | Other people's time | Your own priorities |
| A slide deck | Standing in front of a room | Being read alone |

Half the management complaints you will hear this term are a tool
mismatch. "Nobody read my email" is often "that should have been a
meeting"; "we were in meetings all day" is often "that should have been a
document".

> [!tip] Search is a management skill
> When you research an organization this term, keep a note of *where* you
> found each fact. A claim you cannot re-find is a claim you cannot
> defend, and the first question about any recommendation you make is
> "how do you know?"

## Using them in this course

You will use these for real, not as an exercise: a shared document for
your team charter, a spreadsheet for the evidence behind
[[The Strategic Review]], and a deck for the hand-over at the end of
[[The Management Review]]. We name the tool on the class page each time,
and [[Writing for Business]] covers the writing itself.
