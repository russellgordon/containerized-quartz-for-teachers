A job is designed, whether or not anyone designed it. Someone decided
which tasks go together, how much variety there is, and how much of the
decision belongs to the person doing the work.

## Four approaches

| Approach | The move | Best when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simplification | Fewer, narrower tasks, easily learned | The work is genuinely repetitive and volume is everything |
| Rotation | Move people between jobs at the same level | Boredom and single points of failure |
| Enlargement | Add more tasks at the same level | The job is too narrow to see a whole outcome |
| Enrichment | Add planning, checking, and decisions — depth, not width | People are ready for more responsibility and want it |

Only enrichment changes how much control a person has. The other three
change what they touch.

## What makes a job worth doing

Five properties keep showing up in the research: **variety** in the
skills used, a whole and identifiable **task**, work that visibly matters
to someone (**significance**), **autonomy** over how it is done, and
**feedback** that arrives from the work itself rather than only from a
manager.

> [!warning] Enlargement is not enrichment
> Adding three more of the same tasks is more work, not better work, and
> people can tell the difference immediately. If the redesign does not
> hand over a decision, it is enlargement wearing enrichment's name.

Job design is also where accessibility and accommodation live in
practice: which tasks are essential to a job and which are simply how it
has always been arranged is a design question with legal weight — see
[[The Law at Work]].

You will redesign a real job, with the person's own account of it, as
part of [[The Management Review]].
