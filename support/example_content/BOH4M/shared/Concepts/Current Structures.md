The traditional four assume the organization is a container with people
inside it. Newer forms loosen that assumption in different directions.

| Structure | The idea | Where it wins | Where it hurts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Team | Permanent cross-functional teams own outcomes end to end | Speed; nobody can hand a problem sideways | Needs teams that can genuinely self-manage |
| Network | Keep a small core, contract out the rest | Low fixed cost, quick to reconfigure | Capability you rent can be rented by competitors too |
| Boundaryless | Deliberately weak internal walls; work forms around problems | Information moves; talent moves to where it is needed | Accountability is hard to locate when something fails |

## What they are buying

Two things: **productivity** — fewer hand-offs, less waiting for another
department — and **competitive advantage**, which usually means being
able to change what you do faster than a rival can.

Both claims should be interrogated rather than accepted. A network
structure has lower fixed costs and less control over quality. A team
structure removes the hand-off and needs people who can handle
disagreement without a manager to appeal to. These are trades, and a
company that adopts one because the term is fashionable typically gets
the cost without the benefit.

> [!warning] "We have no hierarchy" is almost never true
> Flat organizations still have people whose opinions end discussions.
> The difference is that the authority is undocumented, which makes it
> harder to appeal, harder to inherit, and much harder for a newcomer to
> read. That is worth saying out loud when someone presents flatness as
> pure gain.

The case [[The Flat Structure]] gives you a company that made exactly
this move, three years ago, and now has a problem.
