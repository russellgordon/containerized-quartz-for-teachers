Planning tools do one of two jobs: they help you decide, or they help you
keep going. The mistake is using a long-range tool for this week's work,
or a scheduling tool for a decision about the next three years.

## Tools for deciding

| Tool | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Participatory planning | Brings the people who do the work into the planning of it — better plans, and buy-in you do not have to manufacture later |
| Benchmarking | Compares your performance against another organization's, so "good" stops being an opinion |
| Scenario planning | Builds two or three futures and asks what you would do in each — not prediction, preparation |
| Forecasting | Projects a trend forward, with its assumptions stated |

## Short term and long term

| Horizon | Tools | Question they answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short — days to a year | Policies, operational plans, budgets for the period, schedules | How does the work run? |
| Long — one to five years | Strategic plans, multi-year budgets, simulations, forecasts | What are we becoming? |

A **policy** is a standing decision: made once, applied many times, so
nobody has to re-decide it. An **operational plan** says who does what,
with what, by when. A **budget** is a plan expressed in money — which is
why finance departments end up holding the real plan.

> [!warning] A long-term plan with short-term numbers is a wish
> If the five-year plan does not change this year's budget, it will not
> happen. Check that link in any strategic plan you analyse — the gap
> between stated strategy and funded strategy is where most of the
> interesting analysis lives.

Simulations sit between the two: run the plan against invented conditions
and see what breaks before it costs anything. You will do exactly that in
[[The Crisis Hour]], and use these tools for real in
[[The Strategic Review]].
