The method an organization chooses shapes what gets noticed, so it is a
management decision rather than a form to fill in.

| Method | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Comparison (ranking, forced distribution) | People are ranked against each other | Simple; forces a decision | Destroys teamwork; assumes performance is distributed the way the form says |
| Graphic rating scale | Rate traits or behaviours 1–5 | Fast, comparable across a department | "3 out of 5" for *initiative* means whatever the rater thought |
| Behaviourally anchored rating scale (BARS) | Each point on the scale is anchored to a described behaviour | Much more consistent between raters; defensible | Expensive to build, one per job family |
| 360° feedback | Input from manager, peers, reports, sometimes customers | Sees what a manager cannot; excellent for development | Damaging if attached to pay; needs real anonymity |
| Management by objectives | Agreed goals, reviewed against results | Clear, and focuses on outcomes | Only as good as the goals; undervalues what was not a goal |

## Choosing between them

Ask what the appraisal is for. If it is development, 360° and MBO earn
their cost. If it is a defensible record for promotion or discipline,
BARS is the one that stands up. If it is speed across two hundred
employees, a rating scale is honest about being a rough instrument.

> [!warning] Forced distribution has a body count
> Requiring that a fixed percentage be rated lowest guarantees the label
> exists even in a strong team, and it prices cooperation out of the
> workplace — helping a colleague now costs you your ranking. Several
> large firms adopted it publicly and abandoned it publicly, which is
> itself worth studying.

You will compare two methods on the same employee, and defend one, in
[[The Appraisal Meeting]].
