Control systems differ by *when* they act, and the difference decides
what they can and cannot save you from.

| System | When | Example | Catches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Feedforward | Before the work | Hiring standards, supplier inspection, training, a checklist before the shift | Problems in the inputs |
| Concurrent | During the work | Supervision, a dashboard, a stage gate, a pilot check | Problems while they can still be fixed |
| Feedback | After the work | Results reviews, customer complaints, financial statements, appraisals | Problems in time to prevent the next one |

## Using all three

Most organizations over-invest in feedback, because it is the easiest to
build: the numbers already exist. But feedback control can only ever tell
you about work that has already been done — the batch is made, the
customer has left, the term is over.

The cheapest defects to fix are the ones prevented, which is what
feedforward buys. A restaurant checks deliveries at the door
(feedforward), a chef tastes while cooking (concurrent), and the manager
reads the reviews (feedback). Skip the first two and you are a manager
who finds out from strangers.

> [!success]- Sort these three (click to expand)
> A pre-flight checklist; a monthly sales report; a supervisor watching a
> new employee's first solo shift. Feedforward, feedback, concurrent —
> and notice that the third one costs the most in a manager's time, which
> is exactly why it is the one that gets dropped.

The three appear together in [[The Crisis Hour]], where you have to
decide, live, which one you are missing.
