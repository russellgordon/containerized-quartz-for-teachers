Every idea about how to run an organization was invented by someone with
a problem. Read the schools in that order — problem first, theory second
— and they stop being a list to memorise.

## Four answers to "how should this be run?"

| School | The problem it was answering | What it says to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classical | Factories where nobody knew the best way to do anything | Study the work, standardise it, train to the standard, structure the authority |
| Behavioural | Workers who did not behave the way the classical school predicted | Attend to people: groups, motivation, satisfaction |
| Quantitative | Wartime problems too big to solve by judgement alone | Model it, measure it, optimise it |
| Systems | Organizations that failed even when each department did well | Treat the parts as connected; nothing is local |

**Total quality management** belongs beside these rather than inside
them. Its claim is that quality is not an inspection at the end but
everyone's job all the way through, improved continuously and measured
honestly — an idea that borrows the quantitative school's measurement and
the behavioural school's belief that the people doing the work know the
most about it.

> [!tip] Nobody is only one of these
> A manager who times a process (classical), asks the team what is
> getting in the way (behavioural), builds a forecast (quantitative), and
> worries about the effect on the warehouse (systems) has used four
> schools before lunch. The schools are lenses, not tribes.

## Reading them critically

The classical school gave us the assembly line and the time-and-motion
study; it also gave us workplaces that treated people as machine parts,
which is exactly what the behavioural school was reacting against. When
you evaluate a theory in this course, evaluate it the way its critics
did: what does it get right, what does it cost, and who pays that cost?

That question comes back in Unit 3, when
[[Analysing a Strategy|strategy tools]] turn out to have the same
property — powerful, and blind in particular directions.
