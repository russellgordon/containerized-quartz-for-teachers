Job satisfaction is not a mood. It is an attitude — a settled evaluation
a person holds about their work — and it is built out of specific things:
what the work is, who they do it with, whether effort is noticed, and
whether the place is fair.

## Where satisfaction comes from

| Source | The question the employee is answering |
| --- | --- |
| The work itself | Is this worth doing, and am I any good at it? |
| Recognition | Does anyone notice when I do it well? |
| Relationships | Do I want to spend a day with these people? |
| Fairness | Am I treated the way others here are treated? |
| Fit | Does this job suit the person I actually am? |

Personality shapes what a person needs from that list. Perception decides
what they see: two people can receive the same feedback and one hears
encouragement while the other hears a warning.

## From attitude to behaviour

An attitude shows itself in what people do. Commitment shows up as
discretionary effort — the thing done on Friday that nobody asked for.
Its absence shows up as absenteeism, as work done to the letter, and as
the quiet withdrawal of ideas.

> [!warning] Some attitudes are not a management style question
> Behaviour that reflects racist, sexist, or homophobic attitudes is not
> a matter of fit or preference, and it is not managed by adjusting your
> approach. It is a conduct issue and, under Ontario law, a duty on the
> employer to act. Naming that clearly is part of managing well; treating
> it as a personality clash is how organizations end up in front of a
> tribunal.

## Why a manager should care about a feeling

Because attitude is upstream of everything measurable. Quality,
turnover, safety, and the willingness to raise a problem before it
becomes expensive all move with it — which is why satisfaction shows up
later in [[Motivation in Practice]] and again in
[[The Individual and the Organization]].

We argue about the honest limits of this in
[[Bosses We Have Known]].
