Conflict at work is not a sign that something has gone wrong. It is a
sign that two people want different things and both care — which is
better than the alternative. What matters is the style used to handle it.

## Five styles, and when each is right

| Style | You get | Right when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collaboration | Both sets of needs met, slowly | The issue matters and the relationship continues |
| Compromise | Both sides give something up | Time is short and the positions are close |
| Accommodation | The other side's outcome | You were wrong, or the issue matters far more to them |
| Avoidance | Nothing, for now | The issue is trivial, or tempers need a day |
| Competition | Your outcome | Safety, law, or ethics leave no room to negotiate |

Avoidance has a bad reputation it half deserves. Postponing a decision
until people can think is a legitimate move; hoping a problem will
dissolve is not, and the difference is whether you named a time to come
back to it.

## The move that changes most conflicts

Separate **positions** from **interests**. A position is what someone
says they want ("I need the Thursday slot"). An interest is why ("I can't
get childcare on Friday"). Positions collide; interests often do not, and
a solution nobody had thought of usually lives in that gap.

> [!tip] Two questions that unlock a stuck conversation
> "What would you need for this to work?" and "What am I missing?" Both
> hand the other person something to do besides defend their position.

## What it costs the organization

Unmanaged conflict is expensive in ways that never appear on a budget
line: information stops moving between two teams, meetings grow to
include people who are only there to watch each other, and the best
person leaves. Managing conflict is not conflict avoidance — it is
choosing the style deliberately instead of falling into your habitual one.

You will find out what your habitual one is in [[The Negotiation]].
