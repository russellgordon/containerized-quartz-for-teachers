Work arrangements are no longer a single default with exceptions. Each
one is a trade, and the trade lands differently on different people.

| Arrangement | Gains | Costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job sharing | Keeps experienced people who cannot work full weeks | Hand-over overhead; needs two people who trust each other |
| Self-managed teams | Speed, ownership, fewer hand-offs | Needs skill and maturity; conflict has no easy referee |
| Compressed or flexible schedules | Autonomy; often better retention | Coverage gaps; long days do not suit every body or household |
| Off-site work | No commute, deep focus, a wider hiring pool | Weak informal information; proximity bias in who gets noticed |
| Contract work | Flexibility for the organization; freedom for some workers | No security, no benefits, and no reason to invest in the future here |

## Individuals and groups experience these differently

An arrangement that suits an individual can hurt the group and the
reverse. Off-site work suits the person who needs quiet and disadvantages
the newcomer who learned everything by overhearing it. Self-managed teams
reward the confident and can leave a quieter member doing invisible work.

The manager's job is to notice the asymmetry and design against it —
scheduled overlap for teams that are partly remote, deliberate rotation
of who represents the team, and promotion decisions checked against who
is actually in the room.

> [!tip] Ask who is not choosing freely
> "Flexible" is a benefit when you have a choice and a euphemism when you
> do not. Contract work and unpredictable schedules are chosen by some
> workers and imposed on others; a fair analysis says which is which.

The seminar [[The Four-Day Week]] takes the most argued-about of these
and asks for evidence, not preference.
