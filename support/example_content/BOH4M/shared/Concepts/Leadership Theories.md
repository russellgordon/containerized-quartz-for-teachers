Leadership theory is a hundred-year argument about one question: is
leadership something you *are*, something you *do*, or something the
*situation* calls for?

## The argument, in order

| Theory | Its claim | Its blind spot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leadership Grid | Leaders vary in concern for people and concern for production; high on both is best | Assumes one best style everywhere |
| Contingency | The right style depends on the situation — the task, the team, the leader's power | Harder to act on; "it depends" is not advice |
| Transactional | Leadership is an exchange: clear expectations, rewards, and corrections | Buys compliance, rarely commitment |
| Transformational | Leaders raise what followers want — purpose, identity, standards | Can shade into manipulation; hard to sustain |
| Servant | The leader's job is to serve the people doing the work | Slow, and vulnerable to being exploited |

## How to compare them without flattening them

Do not ask which is correct. Ask **what each one predicts** in a
situation you can describe, then test it:

- A crisis with a deadline in six hours
- A team of experts who know more about the work than you do
- A group that has just been through a badly handled restructure
- A volunteer organization where nobody can be fired

Transactional leadership does well in the first and poorly in the last.
Servant leadership is the reverse. That is contingency's whole point, and
it is why the theories are more useful together than ranked.

> [!warning] Watch for theory used as a compliment
> "Transformational" has become a word people apply to leaders they like.
> The test is not whether people admired them; it is whether followers'
> goals and standards actually changed, and whether that change survived
> their departure.

You choose one of these to defend, with evidence from a real leader, in
[[The Leadership Profile]].
