Structures have been moving in the same broad direction for thirty years,
and a manager's job inside that movement is specific.

## The direction of travel

| Trend | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| Shorter chain of command | Fewer layers between the work and the decision |
| Wider spans of control | More people per manager, so less supervision each |
| Decentralization | Decisions made closer to the customer |
| Increased empowerment | Authority pushed to the person doing the work |
| More organic structures | Roles that shift with the problem rather than fixed job boxes |

## What that asks of a manager

Every trend above moves work from *supervising* to *enabling*, and the
manager's role changes with it:

- **Set the boundaries of a decision before delegating it.** Empowerment
  without a stated boundary is a trap, and people learn quickly not to
  step into it twice.
- **Make information available.** A decentralised decision made without
  the numbers is just a guess made further down.
- **Build the capability first.** Delegating to a team that has not been
  trained for the decision is abandonment with a nicer name.
- **Hold the standard.** A wider span means less watching, which means
  the standard has to be explicit enough to work unwatched.

> [!warning] A wider span is a real cost, paid by someone
> Twelve direct reports means a fraction of the coaching each person
> would get from a manager with five. That trade may be right — but it is
> a trade, and the people who pay it are usually the newest employees.

None of this is free of structure: what changes is where authority sits,
not whether it exists — which is where [[Design and Culture]] picks up.
