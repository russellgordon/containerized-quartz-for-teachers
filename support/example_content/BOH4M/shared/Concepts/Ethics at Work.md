Most workplace ethics is not a dramatic choice between right and wrong.
It is a small decision, made quickly, by someone who is busy — and the
reason it matters is that the small ones set what the next person thinks
is normal.

## The situations that actually come up

| The situation | Why it is hard |
| --- | --- |
| A supplier's gift at the wrong moment | The line between hospitality and influence is drawn by timing |
| Harassment reported to you, in confidence | Two duties — to the person, and to the organization — that pull apart |
| A cost that is cheaper to externalise | The person who pays it is not in the room |
| Theft that everyone knows about | Doing nothing is a decision, and it is contagious |
| A land claim against a resource the company wants | The law and the obligation are not the same size |

## Three questions that do more work than a code of conduct

1. **Who is affected, including people not in the room?**
2. **Would I be comfortable if this were reported accurately?**
3. **What does this decision teach the person watching me make it?**

> [!warning] "It's legal" is where the question starts
> Compliance is the floor, not the ceiling. Much of what organizations
> later apologise for was legal at the time, and the people who did it
> were not villains — they were busy, and nobody asked question 3.

## A dilemma is not a puzzle

A puzzle has an answer you can find. A dilemma has two defensible
answers, and management means choosing one and living with the cost. In
this course you will be marked on how you reason, what you weigh, and
whether you name the cost you accepted — not on landing where the teacher
would have landed.

That is exactly what [[The Ethics Brief]] asks for, and
[[The Ethics Memo]] is where we practise it first.
