Announcing a change is not leading one. The announcement is the easiest
part and the one most organizations get wrong.

## Strategies for winning acceptance

| Strategy | What it is | Best when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Education and communication | Explaining the why, repeatedly, in person | Resistance comes from missing information |
| Participation | Bringing affected people into the design | They know things you do not, and will own the result |
| Shared power | Giving groups real authority over part of it | Trust is low and needs rebuilding |
| Negotiation | Trading — protections, timing, compensation | Someone will genuinely lose something |
| Facilitation and support | Training, time, help through the transition | The change is real and the fear is competence |
| Coercion | Requiring it | Safety or law; and it spends trust you will want later |

Most changes need several of these at once, aimed at different groups.

## What the announcement itself has to do

1. **Say what is changing, plainly, in the first thirty seconds.**
2. **Say why, with the evidence** — including what will happen if
   nothing changes.
3. **Say what is not changing.** People fill silence with the worst
   version.
4. **Say who is affected and how**, honestly, including bad news.
5. **Say what happens next, and when they will hear more** — and then
   actually do that, or nothing else you said will be believed.

> [!tip] The rumour is already ahead of you
> By the time a change is announced, most people have heard a version of
> it. Your announcement is not the first word — it is a correction, and
> it should answer the rumour explicitly rather than pretend it does not
> exist.

You will deliver one of these to a room that is allowed to push back, in
[[The Change Announcement]], and plan a real one in [[The Change Plan]].
