Theories describe. A manager has to choose what to actually do on Monday,
usually without control over pay.

## What organizations try

| Strategy | What it assumes | Where it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Performance bonuses | Effort responds to money | Rewards what is measured, which is never the whole job |
| Recognition programs | Being seen matters | Becomes routine, or is seen as favouritism |
| Job enrichment | People want more of the work, not less | Reads as more work for the same pay if handled badly |
| Autonomy | Adults do better untangled from supervision | Needs the person to be ready, and the manager to let go |
| Career development | People stay for a future | Only credible if someone has actually been promoted |
| Team-based rewards | Cooperation matters more than individual output | Invites social loafing unless contributions stay visible |

## Reward, motivation, and performance

The chain is looser than it looks. A reward affects motivation only if
the person values it, believes effort will actually produce it, and
believes the process is fair. Break any of those three links and the
reward becomes an expense with no effect — or worse, a signal that the
organization thinks it can buy something it cannot.

And performance does not come from motivation alone. A motivated person
with no training, no tools, or an impossible workload still performs
badly, and treating that as a motivation problem is one of the most
common management errors you will see this term.

> [!success]- The diagnostic question (click to expand)
> Before reaching for an incentive, ask: *is this a can't or a won't?* A
> can't is training, tools, workload, or process. A won't is motivation.
> Incentives applied to a can't make people feel worse and change
> nothing.

Job satisfaction sits underneath all of it — see
[[Attitudes and Satisfaction]] — and you will diagnose a real case of
this in [[The People Problem]].
