Time management is a management skill, not a study skill. A manager's day
is mostly other people's requests, and the whole discipline is deciding
which of them get your attention.

## Sorting by urgency and importance

| | Urgent | Not urgent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Important** | Do it now — crises, hard deadlines | **Schedule it** — planning, coaching, prevention |
| **Not important** | Delegate or decline — most interruptions | Drop it |

The top-right box is where management actually happens, and it is the box
that disappears first, because nothing in it will shout at you.

## Techniques that survive contact with a real week

- **Block the important work first**, in the calendar, before the week
  fills with meetings.
- **Batch the small things.** Twenty small tasks scattered through a day
  cost more than the same twenty done in two blocks.
- **Delegate with the decision attached.** Handing over a task while
  keeping the decision means you are still doing it, badly, in the
  background.
- **Say no with an alternative.** "Not this week; Thursday next?" is a
  refusal a colleague can plan around.
- **Leave slack.** A fully booked calendar cannot absorb a single
  surprise, and every week has one.

> [!tip] Track a week before you change anything
> Where the hours went, in thirty-minute blocks, honestly. Almost
> everyone is wrong about their own week, and the plan you would have
> made on Monday is not the plan the data suggests.

That tracking is exactly what [[A Week of Your Time]] asks for, and you
will feel the cost of poor prioritising directly in [[The In-Basket]].
