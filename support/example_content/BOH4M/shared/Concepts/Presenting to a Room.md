Presenting is not a personality trait. It is a small set of decisions,
most of which are made before you stand up.

## Before you stand up

1. **Decide the one sentence.** If the room remembers one thing, what is
   it? Write it down. If you cannot, the presentation is not ready.
2. **Decide the order.** Managers listen for the recommendation first and
   the reasoning second — the opposite of an essay.
3. **Decide what is on the slide.** A slide is a signpost for the
   audience, not a script for you. If you are reading it aloud, they can
   read it faster than you can say it.

## While you are up there

| Do | Because |
| --- | --- |
| Look at people, in turn, for a whole sentence each | Sweeping the room reads as nerves; a held look reads as address |
| Say the number, then what it means | A figure with no interpretation is homework you handed to the audience |
| Stand still, deliberately | Movement should mean something — a step forward is emphasis |
| Stop when you are done | The last thirty seconds of most presentations are an apology |

> [!success]- What to do with a question you cannot answer (click to expand)
> Say so, say what you would need to answer it, and offer to follow up.
> "I don't have that figure — I'd need last year's returns, and I can
> send it tonight" is a strong answer. Guessing in front of a room is the
> only wrong one, because everything else you said now needs checking.

## Where you will use it

Three times, in front of an audience that is not only your teacher: the
