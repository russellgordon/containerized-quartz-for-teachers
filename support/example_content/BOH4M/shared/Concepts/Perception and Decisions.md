Two managers read the same report and reach opposite conclusions. Neither
is lying. They perceived different things — and perception, not
information, is what decisions are actually made from.

## How perception bends a decision

| Pattern | What it does | Where you will see it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Selective attention | You notice what you already expected | Reading a performance file |
| First impressions | The first fact anchors everything after it | Interviews, and the first week of a project |
| Attribution | Their failure is character; yours was circumstance | Any post-mortem |
| Stereotype | A group membership stands in for a person | Hiring, promotion, and who gets the interesting work |
| Confirmation | You look hardest for evidence you are right | Every strategy analysis you will do |

## What to do about it

You cannot switch perception off. You can build habits that make it
visible:

- **Write the decision down before the discussion**, then see what
  changed and why.
- **Ask what would change your mind.** If nothing would, it is not a
  judgement, it is a position.
- **Get the disagreement early.** A team that only speaks after the
  decision is giving you agreement you did not earn.
- **Separate what you observed from what you concluded.** "She missed
  three deadlines" is an observation. "She isn't committed" is a
  conclusion, and it may be wrong.

> [!success]- The two-column test (click to expand)
> Draw a line down a page. Left: what you actually saw or read. Right:
> what you concluded. Most disputes turn out to be entirely in the right
> column, which is where they can be argued with — and where a
> stereotype, if there is one, will be sitting in plain sight.

Perception is also why [[Performance Appraisal|appraisal]] is so hard,
and it is the fault line under the case
[[The Motivation Problem]].
