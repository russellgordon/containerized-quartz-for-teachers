Three tools do most of the work when you are asked whether a strategy is
any good. Each looks in a different direction, and each is blind
somewhere — which is why you use more than one.

## SWOT

Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats. The first two are inside
the organization; the last two are outside it. That distinction is the
whole discipline, and it is the one students lose first.

| | Helpful | Harmful |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inside** | Strengths — what you have and rivals do not | Weaknesses — what you lack that the work needs |
| **Outside** | Opportunities — a change in the world you could use | Threats — a change in the world that could hurt |

A SWOT is worthless unless each entry is specific and evidenced. "Good
team" is not a strength. "Three of the four senior installers in the
region work here" is.

## PESTLE

The outside world, sorted: political, economic, social, technological,
legal, environmental. Its job is to stop you from analysing an industry
as though it floated free of the country it operates in — minimum wage
changes, an interest rate, an aging population, and a new accessibility
requirement are all strategy, whether or not the strategy mentions them.

## Porter's five forces

How much profit is available in this industry at all: rivalry among
existing competitors, the threat of new entrants, the threat of
substitutes, the bargaining power of buyers, and the bargaining power of
suppliers. When every force is strong, even a well-run firm struggles —
which is a finding, and often the most useful one.

> [!warning] The tool is not the analysis
> A completed SWOT grid is not an answer; it is raw material. The
> analysis is what you say next: which two of these matter most, what
> follows for the strategy, and what you would do about it. Marks in this
> course go to that sentence, not to the grid.
