Between a person and their employer there is a written agreement about
hours and pay, and a second, unwritten one about everything else. The
second one causes most of the trouble.

## The psychological contract

Each side believes the other has promised things nobody wrote down.

| The employee expects | The organization expects |
| --- | --- |
| Interesting work, eventually | Effort beyond the job description |
| To be told the truth about the future | Discretion about what is said outside |
| Fair treatment relative to others here | Flexibility when things are busy |
| That loyalty will be reciprocated | Loyalty |

Nobody negotiates these. They are assumed — which is why a breach feels
like betrayal rather than a variation of terms. The classic version: a
person works two hard years expecting a promotion nobody ever promised,
and leaves feeling cheated by an organization that believes it did
nothing wrong.

## Meaning, quality of work life, and satisfaction

**Meaning of work** — what the job is for, beyond pay. **Quality of work
life** — whether the day is humane: safety, autonomy, respect, a
manageable load, a life outside it. **Job satisfaction** — the settled
attitude that results, which you met in
[[Attitudes and Satisfaction]].

They are related and not identical. Meaningful work in a badly run place
burns people out faster than dull work in a decent one, precisely because
they care.

> [!tip] Make the contract explicit early
> The most useful conversation a new manager can have with each person:
> what do you want from this job in the next year, and what does this job
> need from you? Twenty minutes, and it prevents most of the
> disappointment above.

Job design is where an organization acts on this, which is the next page.
