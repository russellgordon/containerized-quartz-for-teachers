Style is what a leader's decisions look like from underneath: who is
consulted, who decides, and how quickly.

## Four styles, and what each costs

| Style | The decision is made by | Fast? | Buy-in? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Autocratic | The leader | Yes | Low |
| Democratic | The group, after discussion | No | High |
| Collaborative | The group and the leader together, working the problem | No | High, and durable |
| Laissez-faire | Whoever picks it up | Depends | Depends who picked it up |

Every style is right somewhere. Autocratic is the correct style for a
fire alarm. Laissez-faire is the correct style for a team of experienced
professionals who need you to stop interrupting them. The failure is not
using a style — it is using one style everywhere.

> [!tip] Style is judged by what it produces on a bad day
> Anyone can be collaborative when the project is going well. Which style
> a leader falls back to under pressure is the one their team actually
> experiences, and the one they will describe to you years later.

## Reading style in real leaders

When you study a leader, resist the biography's summary and look for
decisions: what did they do when they were wrong, when money was short,
when someone senior disagreed? Study leaders unlike each other — women
and men, from a range of ethnocultural communities, including Indigenous
leaders in business and governance, where leadership is often exercised
through consensus and obligation to community rather than through
position alone. A course that only examines one kind of leader teaches
that only one kind exists.

Your own working style — the one you would claim, with evidence — is what
[[Your Leadership Statement]] asks you to write, and it is the argument
under [[The Leadership Profile]].
