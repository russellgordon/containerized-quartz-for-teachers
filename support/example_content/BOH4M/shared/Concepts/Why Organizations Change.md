Organizations change because something made staying the same worse than
moving. Naming that something is the first step in managing it.

## What sets change off

| Source | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Technology | A system that changes who does the work, and how many are needed |
| A growth plan | New locations, new roles, a structure that no longer fits |
| Culture, evolving | What the organization tolerated five years ago it now does not |
| The outside world | Regulation, a competitor, a supply chain that stopped working |
| People | A new leader, a retirement that takes twenty years of knowledge with it |

## Why people resist — and why that is rational

Resistance to change is usually treated as an obstacle. It is better
treated as information.

| The fear | What it is really saying |
| --- | --- |
| "I'll be worse at my job" | Competence took years to build and is about to be reset |
| "I'll lose something" | Status, a good shift, a team, a commute that worked |
| "This will not work" | Sometimes the person closest to the work is right |
| "I don't believe you" | The last change was announced the same way and went badly |
| "Nobody asked me" | The people affected were told, not consulted |

Enthusiasm is worth understanding too: people embrace change when they
expect to gain from it, when the current situation is genuinely bad, or
when they trust whoever is leading it.

> [!warning] "They're resistant to change" is a diagnosis that stops thinking
> It attributes the problem to character. Every row above is a specific,
> addressable cause — and the last two are about how the change was
> introduced, which is entirely the manager's responsibility.

[[Leading Change]] is what you do with all of this, and
[[The Change Announcement]] is where you try it.
