Structure is one of the few levers a manager can actually pull on
culture. You cannot instruct people to collaborate; you can build an
organization in which collaboration is the path of least resistance.

## Design choices that teach

| Design choice | What it teaches people |
| --- | --- |
| Who reports to whom | Whose approval matters |
| Which meetings exist, and who is in them | Whose voice counts |
| Communication channels — open or routed | Whether it is safe to raise something early |
| Team structures | Whether success is individual or shared |
| Alternative work schedules | Whether the organization trusts adults with their own time |
| Where information lives | Whether knowledge is a resource or a currency |

## The alignment test

Take a value the organization claims and ask what the design does with
it:

- *"We value collaboration"* — and pay individual bonuses on individual
  targets. The design wins.
- *"We want people to raise problems early"* — and every escalation goes
  through the person whose project it is. The design wins.
- *"We are a learning organization"* — and nobody has time booked to
  learn. The design wins.

The design always wins. Culture is not what an organization says it
values; it is what its structure makes easy.

> [!tip] Change one thing and watch
> The cheapest culture experiment available to a manager is to change who
> is in a meeting, or where a decision is made, and see what happens over
> a month. It is smaller than a restructure and tells you more than a
> survey.

This is the last piece of the argument that started in
[[Corporate Culture]] — and it is the lens you will bring to a real
organization in [[The Management Review]].
