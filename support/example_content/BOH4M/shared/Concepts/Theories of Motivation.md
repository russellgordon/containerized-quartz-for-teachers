Motivation theory tries to answer a question managers cannot avoid: why
does a person put in effort they were not required to?

## The main answers

| Theory | The claim | The useful consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maslow's hierarchy | Needs run from physiological to self-actualisation; lower ones dominate until met | Do not offer purpose to someone worried about rent |
| Herzberg's two factors | Pay and conditions can only make people dissatisfied; achievement and recognition motivate | Fixing complaints ≠ creating motivation |
| Alderfer's ERG | Existence, relatedness, growth — pursued at once, not in order | People can regress to a lower need under frustration |
| McClelland | People differ in need for achievement, affiliation, and power | Match the job to the need, not the person to the average |
| Goal-setting | Specific, difficult, accepted goals raise performance | The most directly usable finding in the whole list |

## How to compare theories honestly

Maslow's hierarchy is the most famous and the least supported by
evidence; goal-setting is the least famous and the best supported. That
is worth saying plainly, because a course that presents all five as
equally established teaches something false about how knowledge works.

Compare them the way you compared leadership theories: not "which is
right", but **what does each predict, and where do they disagree?**
Herzberg says a raise will not motivate; goal-setting says a clear,
accepted, difficult target will. Those are testable claims about a real
workplace, and the ones you will test.

> [!warning] Needs are not a ranking of people
> None of these theories says that someone motivated by security is less
> serious than someone motivated by growth. They say motivation differs,
> and that managing everyone with one incentive will reach some people
> and insult others.

The practical half of this is in [[Motivation in Practice]]; the argument
about money is in [[Does Money Motivate?]].
