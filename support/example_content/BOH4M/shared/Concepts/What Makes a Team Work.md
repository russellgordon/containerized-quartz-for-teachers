A team is not a group of talented people. It is a group whose way of
working together produces more than the same people would produce alone —
and plenty of teams manage the opposite.

## What the evidence keeps pointing at

| Factor | When it is present | When it is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shared norms | Everyone knows what "done" and "on time" mean here | Every deadline is renegotiated |
| Cohesiveness | People will spend social capital to help each other | Work stops at the edge of each person's task |
| Clear roles | You know what you are accountable for | Two people do it; one thing goes undone |
| Psychological safety | Someone says "I don't understand" out loud | Problems surface at the deadline |
| Cultural awareness | Different expectations about disagreement are worked out | The quietest people are read as agreeing |

## Social loafing, and how to design it out

In a group with a single shared mark, some effort goes missing — reliably
enough that it has a name. It is rarely laziness; it is what happens when
nobody's contribution is visible.

Design it out rather than complaining about it: make each person's piece
identifiable, keep groups small enough that a missing person is obvious,
and make the group agree its own standards rather than inheriting yours.

> [!success]- The three questions a team charter must answer (click to expand)
> How do we decide when we disagree? What does "finished" mean here?
> What happens the first time someone misses a commitment? A charter that
> does not answer these is a list of promises, not an agreement.

Cohesiveness has a failure mode worth naming: a group can become so
comfortable that disagreement feels like betrayal, and it stops testing
its own ideas. Watch for a team that never argues. It is not a good sign.

Your own charter is the first deliverable of [[The Team Project]], and it
is written in the period after [[The Team Build]].
