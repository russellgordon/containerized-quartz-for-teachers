Communication improves through technique, not through effort. Trying
harder to be clear is not a plan; the techniques below are.

## Active listening

Not nodding. Three specific moves:

1. **Say back what you heard**, in your own words, before you reply.
2. **Ask the question that would change your mind**, not the one that
   proves your point.
3. **Let the pause happen.** The sentence after a silence is usually the
   one that mattered.

## Feedback that can be acted on

| Weak | Better | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Great job" | "The summary at the top saved me ten minutes" | Names the thing to repeat |
| "This isn't working" | "The client asked twice for the cost — it isn't in here" | Names the gap, not the person |
| "You're always late" | "Both of the last two check-ins started without you" | A fact can be discussed; a character claim cannot |

The pattern: **behaviour, effect, request**. What happened, what it
caused, what you want instead — in that order, and soon enough that
everyone still remembers the event.

## Choosing the channel

Match the channel to the message: anything sensitive, ambiguous, or bad
happens face to face; anything that must be remembered exactly gets
written down; anything urgent gets a channel the person actually watches.
Technological tools help with reach and with the record, and they help
not at all with the two hardest parts — saying the difficult thing, and
checking that it landed.

> [!tip] End meetings with "who does what by when"
> Thirty seconds, out loud, before anyone leaves. It is the single most
> reliable communication improvement available to a manager, and it costs
> nothing.
