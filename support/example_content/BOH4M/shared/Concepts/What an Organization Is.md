Three people selling lemonade on a corner are not an organization. Add a
rota, someone who decides the price, and an agreement about splitting the
money, and they are. The difference is not size — it is **structure**: a
shared purpose, work divided between people, and some understanding of
who decides what.

## The three things to look for

| What to look for | The question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | What is this group for, and who says so? |
| Division of labour | Who does which part, and why that split? |
| Hierarchy of authority | Who can decide, and who must be told? |

Every organization you will study this term has all three, including the
ones that insist they do not. A start-up with no titles still has someone
whose "let's do it" ends the conversation — that is a hierarchy, just an
undocumented one.

> [!tip] Purpose is not the same as the mission statement
> The mission statement is what an organization says it is for. Its
> purpose is what it actually spends money and hours on. When the two
> disagree, the second one is the answer, and the gap between them is
> usually the most interesting thing on the page.

## Why this comes first

You cannot say anything useful about a manager without saying what they
are managing. A decision that is obviously right in a two-person
non-profit is obviously wrong in a hospital, and the reason is structure:
different purposes, different divisions of labour, different rules about
who may decide.

We come back to this on the last day of the course, when
[[Traditional Structures|structure]] stops being background and becomes
the thing you are asked to redesign.
