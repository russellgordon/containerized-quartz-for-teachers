Management problems arrive badly stated. "Morale is low" is not a
problem; it is a symptom, a complaint, and a mood, and the first job is
to turn it into something that can be solved.

## Strategies worth having

| Strategy | Use it when | How it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Define the gap | Always, first | State where you are, where you should be, and the size of the difference |
| Five whys | The cause is hidden behind a symptom | Ask why until the answer is something you can change |
| Fishbone | Many possible causes at once | Sort candidate causes by category — people, process, equipment, materials |
| Decision matrix | Several options, several criteria | Score each option against weighted criteria you set before you look |
| Cost–benefit | The choice is mostly about money | Compare over the same period, including what you give up |
| Pilot it | The stakes are high and reversible | Test small, measure, then decide |

## Where problem-solving goes wrong

- **Solving the first cause you find.** It is usually the most visible
  one, not the most important.
- **Choosing criteria after you see the options.** That is not deciding;
  it is justifying.
- **Confusing a decision with a plan.** Deciding to reduce turnover is
  not a plan for reducing turnover.
- **Ignoring who has to live with it.** The people closest to the work
  usually know which options are fantasies.

> [!tip] Write the problem as a question with a verb
> "How might we cut the time between order and shipment by two days?" can
> be answered, argued with, and checked. "The warehouse is a mess" cannot.
