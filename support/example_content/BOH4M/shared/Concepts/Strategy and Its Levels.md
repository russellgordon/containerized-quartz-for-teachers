Strategy is the set of choices that decide what an organization will be
good at, and what it will therefore not do. The second half of that
sentence is the part organizations find difficult.

## Three levels, three different questions

| Level | The question | Decided by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Corporate | What businesses should we be in at all? | The board and top management |
| Business | How do we compete in this particular market? | The head of that business unit |
| Functional | How does marketing, or operations, or HR support that? | Functional managers |

A functional plan that does not serve the business strategy is busywork,
however well executed — and a business strategy that no functional plan
supports is a slide.

## Why the process matters as much as the plan

The strategic planning process forces an organization to say out loud
what it believes about its customers, its costs, and its future. Much of
the value is in the argument: the finance team and the operations team
discover they have been assuming different things, and it is much cheaper
to find that out in a planning meeting than in a bad quarter.

> [!warning] Strategy is what you fund
> The stated strategy is on the website. The real one is visible in the
> budget, the hiring plan, and what got cancelled. When you analyse an
> organization, compare the three — the gap is the finding.

Once a direction is chosen, someone has to organize people to deliver it
and control whether it is being delivered, which is why this strand ends
in [[The Control Process]] and the next one begins with structure.
