Structure is the answer to one question asked over and over: **who
groups with whom?** Four traditional answers cover most organizations you
will meet.

| Structure | Grouped by | Strength | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Functional | Kind of work — marketing, operations, finance | Deep expertise; clear career paths | Departments optimise themselves, not the customer |
| Divisional | Product, region, or customer | Each division can move fast and be judged whole | Duplication; four HR teams doing one job |
| Hybrid | Some functions central, divisions elsewhere | Keeps scale where it pays and speed where it matters | The boundary is argued about forever |
| Matrix | Two at once — function *and* project | Expertise aimed at real problems | Two bosses, and conflicting priorities are the employee's problem |

## Reading a structure

An org chart tells you three things immediately: the **span of control**
(how many report to one manager), the **chain of command** (how far a
decision travels), and where the organization has decided expertise
matters more than speed, or the reverse.

Nothing about a structure is neutral. A functional structure makes
cross-department work slow, which is a choice about what the organization
wants to be good at. A matrix makes conflict routine and pushes it down
to individuals, which is why it needs unusually good managers to work at
all.

> [!tip] Structure follows strategy — usually late
> Most organizations restructure after the strategy changed, not with it,
> which is why so many carry a structure built for a strategy they
> abandoned. When you find one, you have found the beginning of a real
> analysis.

[[Current Structures]] takes up what happened when these four stopped
being enough.
