Some control is exercised by the organization, and some by the person.
Good management uses both, and knows which one it is relying on.

## Internal and external

| Internal control | External control |
| --- | --- |
| Self-discipline, professional standards | Supervision and approval steps |
| Goals a person set and owns | Compensation systems and targets |
| Pride in the work | Progressive discipline |
| Habits and routines | Audits, checks, and reporting |

Internal control is cheaper, faster, and more durable — and it only works
when people know what good looks like, are trusted, and are not set up to
fail. External control is necessary where the stakes are high, the work
is invisible, or the standard is legal rather than professional.

**Lean too far external** and you get people who do exactly what is
measured and nothing else, plus a supervision cost that grows forever.
**Lean too far internal** and problems go unnoticed until they are
expensive.

## Progressive discipline

The standard sequence: an informal conversation, a documented verbal
warning, a written warning, suspension, then termination — with the
purpose, at every step, being *correction* rather than punishment.

For the individual, the effect depends almost entirely on how the first
step is handled. Done well — private, specific, with a clear standard and
a genuine chance to meet it — it is often the moment a career is saved.
Done badly, it is the moment someone starts looking for another job and
stops telling you anything.

> [!warning] Consistency is the whole thing
> The same conduct must draw the same response regardless of who did it.
> Inconsistent discipline is unfair, destroys the credibility of every
> other control the organization has, and in Ontario is exactly what an
> arbitrator or tribunal will examine.

Where this tips into surveillance is a real argument, and we have it in
[[When Control Becomes Surveillance]].
