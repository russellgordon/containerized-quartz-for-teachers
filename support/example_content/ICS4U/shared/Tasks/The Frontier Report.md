> [!abstract] At a glance
> Solo · launched in Unit 4 and due seven classes later · four working
> periods · one written report, one twelve-minute seminar, and questions
> you have to answer without notes

## What you are making

A report and a seminar on **one topic at the edge of computer science**,
chosen from three kinds — and you must engage with all three in the
report, even though one of them leads:

1. **An emerging technology** and its effects on people: who gains, who
   loses, which communities are affected differently, and what is
   already being decided about it.
2. **A collaborative field** where computer science works with another
   discipline: bioinformatics, computational linguistics, health
   informatics, computational archaeology, climate modelling,
   computational social science, digital humanities.
3. **A topic in theoretical computer science**: computability, P versus
   NP, formal verification, information theory, cryptographic hardness,
   automata, or another you can source.

The lead choice is yours. The other two appear as connections: what
theory underlies the technology you chose, and which other discipline it
touches. Real work sits at those intersections, which is the point of
making you cross them.

## What must be in it

- **The question**, in one sentence a classmate understands.
- **What is actually established**, distinguished from what is claimed.
  A result with a paper behind it and a result in a company's
  announcement are different kinds of evidence, and your report must
  treat them differently.
- **The theory underneath**: what problem class this sits in, what is
  known to be hard, and what that means for what it can promise.
- **The other discipline**: what it contributes, and why computing alone
  could not answer the question.
- **The human consequences**: who is affected, unevenly, and what is
  being done about it. [[Ethics, Security, and the Profession]] is the
  frame; "some people worry" is not an analysis.
- **What is unsolved**, and why it is hard rather than merely unfinished.
- **Sources**: at least five, at least two of which are primary —
  a paper, a standard, a specification, a government or professional
  body. Cited where used, not heaped at the end.

## The seminar

Twelve minutes, then questions from the room for five, answered without
notes. The audience's job is to ask the question your report avoided —
and everybody prepares one question for each seminar in advance.

## The working periods

| Day | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Topic registered; the first two sources found and judged together |
| 2 | The theory section — the hardest part, drafted with me available |
| 3 | The consequences section, and the connection to another discipline |
| 4 | Seminar rehearsed on one other student, and cut to twelve minutes |

## How this is assessed

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Established separated from claimed | Says which is which, every time |
| Theory is real | Names the problem class and what follows from it |
| Crosses disciplines | The other field is doing genuine work, not decoration |
| Consequences are specific | Named groups, named effects, named responses |
| Sources hold up | Five or more, two primary, cited in place |
| Seminar survives questions | Answers the hard one honestly, including "I do not know" |

> [!question]- Choosing a topic nobody else will
> The overlooked ones make the best reports, because the general-audience
> writing does not exist and you are forced to the real sources:
> computing and hydrology, formal methods in medical devices, language
> revitalisation, error-correcting codes in storage, provenance in
> archives. Bring one to me early and I will tell you whether it has
> enough behind it.

## Reflect

A [[Code Journal]] entry: what did you believe about this topic before
you read the primary sources, and which one changed your mind? Then:
which of the three kinds — technology, collaboration, theory — do you
want to spend more time in, and what would that require of you?
