Two entries about the same afternoon in [[Boiling Points and Structure]].
One of them tells me nothing I did not already know.

## The weak version

> [!example]- The entry that took two minutes and was worth about that
> Today we found the boiling points of four liquids. Water was highest
> and hexane was lowest. This is because of intermolecular forces —
> water has hydrogen bonding which is the strongest. The lab worked.
> Next time I will be more careful with the thermometer.

Everything in it is true, and none of it is information. There is not a
single measurement in it. "This is because of intermolecular forces" is
the name of an explanation rather than an explanation. And "be more
careful" is a resolution nobody has ever kept, because it does not name
a thing to do differently.

Look hardest at the second sentence. It gives the right answer to a
question the data was never asked. Four liquids that differ in molar
mass *and* in bonding cannot, by themselves, tell you which of those two
did the work — and noticing that is the entire skill this page is about.

## The strong version

> **Prediction.** Boiling point measures how much energy is needed to
> separate molecules from each other, so it should rank by the strength
> of the intermolecular forces. **Model:** hydrogen bonding beats
> dipole–dipole beats dispersion for molecules of comparable size, and
> dispersion itself grows with the number of electrons, so with molar
> mass. I predicted the order water, then propan-1-ol, then ethanol,
> then hexane lowest. I was least confident about hexane, whose molar
> mass (86.18 g/mol) is nearly double ethanol's (46.07 g/mol), so its
> dispersion forces should be substantially larger.
>
> **Method note.** Heated in a water bath on a hot plate, not over a
> flame — three of these are flammable and it is the vapour that
> ignites. Thermometer bulb in the vapour just above the liquid, read to
> 0.1 °C. Barometric pressure not measured, which matters below.
>
> | Liquid | Measured (°C) | Booklet value (°C) |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Hexane | 67.9 | 68.7 |
> | Ethanol | 77.2 | 78.4 |
> | Propan-1-ol | 96.1 | 97.2 |
> | Water | 99.1 | 100.0 |
>
> **What the numbers say.** The order came out as predicted. But every
> one of my values is low, by between 0.8 and 1.2 °C, and a thermometer
> I can read to 0.1 °C does not explain a gap that size. A systematic
> shift in one direction across four different liquids is not random
> scatter — it is one cause acting on all four. The likeliest candidate
> is that the room was below standard atmospheric pressure, which lowers
> every boiling point together. That is checkable and I did not check
> it, which is the real limitation of this afternoon.
>
> **Where I was wrong.** Hexane has the largest molar mass and the
> lowest boiling point of the four, by nearly ten degrees against
> ethanol. So molar mass is not the variable in charge here, and my
> hesitation about hexane was hesitation about the right thing for the
> wrong reason — I thought dispersion might *win*, when the real
> question was how much ground hydrogen bonding can make up.
>
> **What this data cannot decide.** Comparing hexane with ethanol
> changes two things at once: the molar mass falls and the capacity to
> hydrogen bond appears. My result is consistent with hydrogen bonding
> being decisive, and it is equally consistent with some other property
> of alcohols that I have not thought of. To separate them I need a pair
> with the *same* molar mass and different bonding. Ethanol and dimethyl
> ether are both 46.07 g/mol, and the booklet gives 78.4 °C and about
> −24 °C. That is a hundred-degree difference at identical molar mass,
> and it settles it.
>
> But I did not measure that, and I should say so plainly: the strongest
> evidence in this entry is a pair of numbers I looked up, taken under
> conditions I did not control, in an argument I did not design.
> Dimethyl ether is a gas at room temperature, so this room could not
> have produced that comparison. That is a limit of the equipment, not
> of my procedure.
>
> **What I actually learned.** I came in thinking the job was to rank
> four liquids and give the reason. The job is to work out which
> comparison my measurements are entitled to make — and it turned out to
> be a comparison I had to go and find rather than one I ran.

## Why the second one earns what it earns

| Move | Where it shows up |
| --- | --- |
| Prediction stated with a model, by name | "hydrogen bonding beats dipole–dipole beats dispersion" |
| The model's own assumption stated | "for molecules of comparable size" |
| Real numbers with units, against a reference | the four-row table |
| A systematic offset spotted, not averaged away | every value low by roughly the same amount |
| A cause proposed for it, and a way to check | pressure, unmeasured, and admitted |
| A prediction admitted wrong, specifically | hexane, and *why* the reasoning was off |
| What the evidence cannot decide | two variables changing together |
| The experiment that would decide it | equal molar mass, different bonding |
| Honesty about where the strongest evidence came from | "numbers I looked up" |
| A limitation owned as equipment, not carelessness | dimethyl ether is a gas |

Ten moves, under 500 words. Length is not the thing.

## The move that is new this year

Look at the row about what the evidence cannot decide.

Last year a strong entry checked whether a difference was bigger than
the uncertainty in the measurement. You should still do that, and it is
now assumed rather than praised. This year the extra move is one level
up: **check whether your design could have distinguished the
explanations at all.** Two variables that move together give you data
that supports both accounts equally well, however precisely you measured
it, and no amount of extra decimal places rescues it.

The habit to build is a single question, asked before you start:
*what would I see if my explanation were wrong?* If the answer is "the
same thing", go and change the design, not the thermometer.

> [!tip] The test to apply before you close the notebook
> Could someone who was not in the room reconstruct what you did, what
> you found, why you believe it, and which model you were reading it
> through? If the answer is no, one more sentence usually fixes it — and
> it is almost always the sentence naming the model or the sentence
> giving the number.
