Write this in the first week, before the course has had a chance to
change your mind about anything. Roughly 300 words, answering five
things.

1. **Where you are starting.** You have already done a year of
   chemistry, so this one is not a first impression — it is a second.
   What did last year leave you with, honestly? Name the actual thing: a
   lab that went well, an idea that never landed, a mark that annoyed
   you, a moment something clicked.
2. **What you think a scientific model is.** One sentence, the one you
   actually hold rather than the textbook one. You spent last year using
   models without being asked to notice them. This year you will be
   asked to say where each one stops being true, so it is worth writing
   down now what you currently think one *is* — a description of
   reality, a useful fiction, a set of rules that happens to work.
3. **What you want to be able to explain** by the end of the course.
   Something specific. "Why some reactions stop before they finish" is
   specific. "Why the pool test kit gives a number and what that number
   is measuring" is specific and better.
4. **Your worry.** One thing you expect to find hard. Naming it now is
   what lets you notice, later, the week it stops being hard.
5. **Where you stand with the mathematics.** Be blunt. This course uses
   logarithms in Unit 4, powers and roots in equilibrium calculations,
   and negative numbers that carry meaning in Unit 5. If any of those
   are shaky, I would far rather know in the first week than in the
   fourteenth — see [[Working with Logarithms in Chemistry]] for what is
   actually involved.

> [!note] Honesty is the only requirement
> "I am taking this because I need it for the program I want" is a
> perfectly good first entry, and it is reliably the one that is most
> interesting to re-read in the last week of the semester — which is
> exactly what [[Showing Growth]] asks you to do.
>
> Question 5 is not a diagnostic test and nothing you write there is
> held against you. It exists so that if the arithmetic is what is in
> your way in Unit 4, we both already know it is the arithmetic and not
> the chemistry, and we can fix the right thing. Every year several
> people discover that what they thought was trouble with equilibrium
> was trouble with a calculator key.

Question 2 is the one worth taking most seriously, because this course
is going to argue with it in every unit. Come back at the end and see
whether you would still write the same sentence.
