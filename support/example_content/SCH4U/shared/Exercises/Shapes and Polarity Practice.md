Two rules run this whole page and everything else is arithmetic on top
of them.

**Count electron groups on the central atom.** A single bond, a double
bond, a triple bond, and a lone pair each count as one group. Groups
push each other as far apart as they can get, and that fixes the
**arrangement**.

**Name the shape after the atoms, not the groups.** Lone pairs occupy
space and appear in no shape name. A molecule with four groups, one of
which is a lone pair, is not tetrahedral — it is trigonal pyramidal, and
saying so correctly is worth more marks over a semester than anything
else on this page.

| Groups | Arrangement | Ideal angle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Linear | 180° |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | 120° |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° |

Electronegativity values come from your data booklet. Different tables
differ in the last digit, so quote differences to two decimal places at
most and never argue from the third.

**1.** $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{NH}_3$, and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ all have
four electron groups on the central atom. Give the arrangement, the
molecular shape, and the bond angle for each, and explain the pattern in
the angles.

> [!success]- Answer 1
> All three have four groups, so all three have a **tetrahedral
> arrangement** of groups. What differs is how many of those groups are
> lone pairs.
>
> | Molecule | Bonding pairs | Lone pairs | Molecular shape | Bond angle |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $\text{CH}_4$ | 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° |
> | $\text{NH}_3$ | 3 | 1 | Trigonal pyramidal | about 107° |
> | $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | 2 | 2 | Bent | about 104.5° |
>
> **The pattern: each lone pair closes the angle by roughly two and a
> half degrees.** A lone pair is held by one nucleus instead of two, so
> it spreads out closer to the central atom and takes up more angular
> room than a bonding pair does. It pushes the bonding pairs together.
>
> Two things to notice about how that answer is phrased. First,
> "tetrahedral" appears once as an *arrangement* for all three and once
> as a *shape* for only one — those are different claims and the
> distinction is the whole point of the question. Second, 109.5° gets a
> decimal and the other two get "about", because 109.5° is what the
> geometry of a regular tetrahedron requires while 107° and 104.5° are
> measured. The model predicts the first exactly and only predicts the
> *direction* of the other two.

**2.** Predict the shape and bond angle of $\text{SO}_3$ and of
$\text{SO}_3^{2-}$. They contain the same elements in the same ratio.
Why are they not the same shape?

> [!success]- Answer 2
> **$\text{SO}_3$**: three electron groups on the sulfur, no lone pair.
> Three groups spread as far apart as possible lie in a plane at 120°
> to each other, so the molecule is **trigonal planar**, angle **120°**.
>
> **$\text{SO}_3^{2-}$**: the two extra electrons that give the ion its
> charge sit on the sulfur as a **lone pair**. That makes four groups —
> three bonds and one lone pair — so the arrangement is tetrahedral and
> the shape is **trigonal pyramidal**, angle **about 107°**.
>
> **Why they differ:** the formula tells you what atoms are present, and
> the shape is decided by *electrons*, not atoms. Adding two electrons
> added a group without adding an atom, and one extra group is enough to
> lift the sulfur out of the plane.
>
> This is the cleanest demonstration on the page that a molecular
> formula is not a structure. If you can see why $\text{SO}_3$ is flat
> and $\text{SO}_3^{2-}$ is not, you have understood the counting rule
> rather than memorised a list.

**3.** Predict the shape of $\text{NH}_4^{+}$. Then say what the
counting rule predicts for $\text{O}_2$.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> **$\text{NH}_4^{+}$**: nitrogen has five valence electrons and forms
> four bonds here, and the positive charge means one electron has gone.
> Count what is left on the central atom: four bonding pairs, **no lone
> pair**. Four groups, no lone pairs, so the shape is **tetrahedral**,
> angle **109.5°**.
>
> Compare it with $\text{NH}_3$ in question 1, which has the same
> nitrogen and the same tetrahedral arrangement but a lone pair, and so
> comes in at about 107°. **Adding a proton to ammonia opens the bond
> angle out**, because the lone pair that was squeezing the hydrogens
> together has become a bond. That is a real, measurable consequence of
> a rule you can apply in ten seconds.
>
> **$\text{O}_2$**: the counting rule predicts **nothing**, because
> there is no central atom to count around. Two atoms joined by a bond
> can only be arranged one way, which is in a straight line, and that
> is true of every diatomic molecule without any model being needed.
>
> Say that plainly rather than forcing an answer. A model that is
> **silent** on a case is not the same as a model that gets the case
> wrong, and being able to tell those apart is what separates using a
> model from reciting one.

**4.** Which of these are polar molecules?
$\text{CO}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CCl}_4$,
$\text{CHCl}_3$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{BF}_3$.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> Polarity takes two steps and skipping the second one is the classic
> error. **Step one: are the bonds polar?** **Step two: does the shape
> let the bond dipoles cancel?**
>
> | Molecule | Shape | Bonds polar? | Do they cancel? | Molecule |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $\text{CO}_2$ | Linear | Yes | Yes, exactly opposite | **Non-polar** |
> | $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | Bent | Yes | No | **Polar** |
> | $\text{CCl}_4$ | Tetrahedral | Yes | Yes, by symmetry | **Non-polar** |
> | $\text{CHCl}_3$ | Tetrahedral | Yes | No — one corner differs | **Polar** |
> | $\text{NH}_3$ | Trigonal pyramidal | Yes | No | **Polar** |
> | $\text{BF}_3$ | Trigonal planar | Yes | Yes, by symmetry | **Non-polar** |
>
> Every one of these has polar bonds. Three of them are non-polar
> molecules anyway, which tells you that **the shape is doing more work
> than the electronegativity difference is**.
>
> The pair worth staring at is $\text{CCl}_4$ against
> $\text{CHCl}_3$. Replacing one chlorine with a hydrogen does not
> change any of the other three bonds at all, and it turns a non-polar
> molecule into a polar one, purely because the four corners are no
> longer identical and the cancellation stops being exact.
>
> A quick test that works for most of what you will meet: **if every
> outer atom is the same and there are no lone pairs on the central
> atom, the dipoles cancel.** Break either condition and they usually do
> not.

**5.** $\text{BF}_3$ and $\text{NF}_3$ have the same number of atoms and
both contain fluorine, the most electronegative element. Using the
values in your data booklet, the electronegativity difference in a B–F
bond is about 1.94 and in an N–F bond about 0.94. Predict the polarity
of each molecule and explain the outcome.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> **$\text{BF}_3$**: boron here has three bonding pairs and no lone
> pair, so three groups, **trigonal planar**, 120°. Three identical
> highly polar bonds arranged symmetrically in a plane cancel exactly.
> The molecule is **non-polar**.
>
> **$\text{NF}_3$**: nitrogen has three bonding pairs and one lone pair,
> so four groups, **trigonal pyramidal**, about 107°. The three bond
> dipoles no longer lie in a plane and cannot cancel. The molecule is
> **polar**.
>
> **The result is the wrong way round from the bond data**, and that is
> the point of the question. $\text{BF}_3$ has by far the more polar
> bonds — a difference of about 1.94 against about 0.94 — and it is the
> one with no dipole at all. Bond polarity is necessary for a polar
> molecule and it is nowhere near sufficient.
>
> **One honest extra.** Predicting "$\text{NF}_3$ is polar" is right.
> Predicting "$\text{NF}_3$ is *strongly* polar" is not: its measured
> dipole is very much smaller than ammonia's, because the lone pair on
> the nitrogen contributes a dipole that points roughly *against* the
> three N–F bond dipoles rather than with them. In $\text{NH}_3$ the two
> contributions reinforce. So the counting model gets the yes-or-no
> right and gets the size badly wrong, which is exactly the kind of
> boundary you are expected to be able to name this year.

**6.** Your data booklet gives these boiling points: propanone 56 °C,
hexane 69 °C, propan-1-ol 97 °C, water 100 °C. Propanone is polar and
hexane is not. Explain the order.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> Take them in the order the booklet gives them and account for each
> step.
>
> | Substance | Molar mass (g/mol) | Boiling point | What holds the molecules together |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Propanone | 58.08 | 56 °C | Dipole–dipole, plus dispersion |
> | Hexane | 86.18 | 69 °C | Dispersion only, over a large molecule |
> | Propan-1-ol | 60.10 | 97 °C | Hydrogen bonding through $-\text{OH}$ |
> | Water | 18.02 | 100 °C | Hydrogen bonding, two per molecule |
>
> **Hexane beats propanone even though hexane is non-polar.** Dispersion
> forces are individually weak and they scale with how many electrons
> there are and how easily the electron cloud distorts. Hexane is a big
> floppy molecule with far more surface for its neighbours to touch,
> and enough weak interactions add up to more than propanone's smaller
> number of stronger ones. **"Polar therefore higher boiling" is not a
> rule**, and this pair is the counterexample to keep in your head.
>
> **Propan-1-ol beats hexane** despite being the smaller molecule,
> because its $-\text{OH}$ group hydrogen bonds — a hydrogen attached
> directly to oxygen, and a lone pair on the next molecule's oxygen to
> attach to.
>
> **Water beats everything here on a molar mass of 18.02 g/mol**, which
> is a fifth of hexane's. Each water molecule has two hydrogens to
> donate and two lone pairs to accept, so it can form up to four
> hydrogen bonds, where propan-1-ol's single $-\text{OH}$ can manage
> far fewer per molecule. Water is the anomaly on every graph of this
> kind, and it is why the first investigation of the course could not
> be explained by molar mass alone.
>
> The general shape of the argument: **structure sets the forces, the
> forces set the boiling point.** Never argue directly from molar mass
> to boiling point without saying what the forces are — it works often
> enough to be dangerous and fails exactly where the interesting
> chemistry is.

**7.** Four claims from a study group. Correct each one.
*(a) "$\text{CCl}_4$ has four polar bonds, so it must be a polar
molecule."*
*(b) "$\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is bent because oxygen is bigger than
hydrogen."*
*(c) "$\text{SO}_3$ and $\text{SO}_3^{2-}$ have the same shape, because
they contain the same atoms."*
*(d) "$\text{NH}_3$ has four electron groups, so its bond angle is
109.5°."*

> [!success]- Answer 7
> **(a) Bonds are not molecules.** Every C–Cl bond in $\text{CCl}_4$ is
> polar, and the four of them point at the corners of a tetrahedron from
> a common centre. Four equal pulls, symmetrically arranged, sum to
> zero. The molecule has **no net dipole**.
>
> The student has done step one and stopped. Compare $\text{CHCl}_3$
> from question 4: same kind of bonds, one corner different, and the
> cancellation fails. If bond polarity alone decided the matter, those
> two would behave the same way, and they do not — $\text{CHCl}_3$
> dissolves things $\text{CCl}_4$ will not touch.
>
> **(b) Size has nothing to do with it.** The bend comes from the two
> **lone pairs** on the oxygen. Four groups take a tetrahedral
> arrangement; two of them are invisible in the shape name; the two
> hydrogens are left at an angle of about 104.5°.
>
> Test the student's reasoning against a case: in $\text{CO}_2$ the
> central carbon is *smaller* than the oxygens on either side, and the
> molecule is perfectly linear. Size does not predict either result. An
> explanation that cannot handle a second example was never an
> explanation.
>
> **(c) Same atoms, different electrons, different shape.** The
> $2-$ charge is two extra electrons, and they sit on the sulfur as a
> lone pair. That is a fourth electron group, so the sulfite ion is
> **trigonal pyramidal at about 107°** while $\text{SO}_3$ is
> **trigonal planar at 120°**. The shape follows the electron count, and
> the electron count is not visible in a list of elements.
>
> **(d) Right count, wrong step.** Four groups do give a tetrahedral
> *arrangement*, and 109.5° is the angle for four **identical** groups.
> Ammonia's four are not identical: one is a lone pair, it is held by
> only one nucleus, it spreads wider near the nitrogen, and it squeezes
> the three N–H bonds to **about 107°**.
>
> This is the model's boundary and it is worth stating precisely rather
> than treating as an exception to be memorised. **Electron counting
> predicts arrangements exactly and predicts angles exactly only when
> there are no lone pairs.** With lone pairs it predicts the
> *direction* of the deviation — smaller than ideal — and not its size.
> A measured angle is the only source for the size, which is why
> $\text{NH}_3$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ come with "about" in front of
> them everywhere on this page.
