The lookup sheet for Unit 2. $\text{R}$ and $\text{R}'$ stand for the
rest of the molecule — a chain of carbons whose length rarely changes
the answer. The reasoning behind all of it is in [[Functional Groups]];
the naming rules are in [[Naming Organic Compounds]].

## The groups

| Class | Group | Suffix | Prefix form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alkane | C–C only | `-ane` | `alkyl-` | propane |
| Alkene | $\ce{C=C}$ | `-ene` | — | propene |
| Alkyne | $\ce{C#C}$ | `-yne` | — | propyne |
| Aromatic | benzene ring | — | `phenyl-` | methylbenzene |
| Haloalkane | $\text{R}-\text{X}$ | — | `chloro-`, `bromo-` | 2-chloropropane |
| Alcohol | $\text{R}-\ce{OH}$ | `-ol` | `hydroxy-` | propan-1-ol |
| Ether | $\text{R}-\ce{O}-\text{R}'$ | — | `alkoxy-` | methoxyethane |
| Aldehyde | $\text{R}-\ce{CHO}$ | `-al` | `oxo-` | propanal |
| Ketone | $\text{R}-\ce{CO}-\text{R}'$ | `-one` | `oxo-` | propanone |
| Carboxylic acid | $\text{R}-\ce{COOH}$ | `-oic acid` | `carboxy-` | propanoic acid |
| Ester | $\text{R}-\ce{COO}-\text{R}'$ | `-oate` | rarely needed | ethyl ethanoate |
| Amine | $\text{R}-\ce{NH2}$ | `-amine` | `amino-` | propan-1-amine |
| Amide | $\text{R}-\ce{CONH2}$ | `-amide` | rarely needed | propanamide |

**Priority when two groups are present**, highest first — the winner
takes the suffix and everything else becomes a prefix:

$$\text{acid} > \text{ester} > \text{amide} > \text{aldehyde} > \text{ketone} > \text{alcohol} > \text{amine}$$

Multiple bonds sit below all of these and are carried in the root
(`-en-`, `-yn-`) rather than competing for the suffix.

## The three that are named in two parts

| Class | How the name is built | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ester | the alcohol's group first as `alkyl`, then the acid as `-oate` | ethyl ethanoate, from ethanol and ethanoic acid |
| Ether | the smaller side becomes an `alkoxy-` prefix on the larger chain | methoxyethane |
| Amine | count the carbon chains on the nitrogen: one is primary, two secondary, three tertiary | N-methylethanamine |

## Physical properties, ranked

For compounds of **roughly equal molar mass**:

| Property | Order, lowest first | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Boiling point | alkane, ether, aldehyde and ketone, alcohol, carboxylic acid, amide | dispersion only, then a dipole, then hydrogen bonding, then two hydrogen bonds per pair |
| Solubility in water | falls as the carbon chain lengthens, in every class | the non-polar tail eventually outweighs the group |
| Acid or base | acids are weakly acidic, amines weakly basic, amides neither | see [[Acids and Bases\|conjugate pairs]] |

Two comparisons worth remembering because they look wrong:

- A **ketone boils lower than an alcohol** of similar mass, even though
  its $\ce{C=O}$ is more polar than an $\ce{O}-\ce{H}$ bond. The
  ketone has no hydrogen attached to oxygen, so it cannot donate a
  hydrogen bond to a neighbour, and dipole–dipole attraction alone is
  weaker.
- An **amide boils higher than a carboxylic acid** of similar mass,
  despite being far less acidic. Acidity and boiling point are
  answering different questions.

## Tests that distinguish them

| Test | Positive result | Tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bromine water | orange-brown colour disappears | an alkene or alkyne — an unsaturated compound |
| Acidified dichromate, warmed | orange turns green | a primary or secondary alcohol, or an aldehyde |
| Same test, no change | stays orange | a tertiary alcohol, or a ketone |
| Tollens' reagent, warmed | a silver mirror forms | an aldehyde, not a ketone |
| Sodium hydrogencarbonate solution | fizzing, gas released | a carboxylic acid |
| Damp red litmus | turns blue | an amine |

The first two rows together separate the three kinds of alcohol, and
rows three and four separate an aldehyde from a ketone — which is the
distinction the classical tests were invented for, because those two
classes are otherwise very similar.

> [!danger] Two reagents on that list are not ordinary
> **Dichromate** solutions are toxic and are treated as a serious
> hazard. They are dispensed by your teacher, used at the stated dilute
> concentration, and go into the labelled waste container, never down
> the sink.
>
> **Tollens' reagent** is prepared immediately before use and destroyed
> immediately after. On standing it can form a solid that is dangerously
> unstable. You will not be storing it, keeping it for the next class,
> or taking it back to a stock bottle.
>
> Everything in this unit is also flammable. Every flame and hot plate
> is off before an organic bottle is opened, and warming is done in a
> water bath rather than over a burner. Full rules in
> [[Lab Safety and WHMIS]].

## The reactions that connect them

| From | To | Reaction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alkene | alkane, haloalkane, or alcohol | addition |
| Alkane | haloalkane | substitution, needs ultraviolet light |
| Alcohol | alkene | elimination — dehydration |
| Primary alcohol | aldehyde, then carboxylic acid | oxidation |
| Secondary alcohol | ketone | oxidation |
| Acid plus alcohol | ester and water | esterification — a condensation |
| Ester plus water | acid and alcohol | hydrolysis |

Each one is explained in [[Organic Reactions]], and the last two run in
both directions, which is the whole basis of [[Polymers]].
