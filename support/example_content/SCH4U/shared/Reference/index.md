These are the pages you keep open **during** a problem, not pages you
read once and file away.

Everything else on this site is written to be understood. A
[[Concepts/index|concept page]] wants you to follow an argument; an
investigation wants you to make a decision. These five want nothing at
all. They are lookup sheets, you will open them dozens of times this
semester, and you are not expected to work through any of them from top
to bottom even once.

| Page | Open it when |
| --- | --- |
| [[VSEPR Shapes]] | you have counted the electron domains and need the shape and the angle |
| [[Functional Groups at a Glance]] | you have a structure and need the class, the suffix, or the property that follows |
| [[Significant Figures and Units]] | you have an answer and are deciding how to write it |
| [[Reading an Equilibrium Table]] | the booklet has given you a $K$ value and you are not sure what it entitles you to say |
| [[Reading a Reduction Potential Table]] | you need to know which half-reaction wins, and by how much |

## Three of them you should stop needing

[[VSEPR Shapes]] and [[Functional Groups at a Glance]] are the two to
work towards not opening. Six shapes and a dozen functional groups carry
almost every question this course will ask, and by Unit 2 the whole
method — structure, then property — assumes you can go from a drawing to
a class without stopping. Reaching for a sheet three times inside one
question does not just cost time; it breaks the thread of the problem
you were actually solving.

[[Significant Figures and Units]] is the third. It is a set of habits
rather than a set of facts, and habits are learned by doing the thing
correctly enough times that doing it incorrectly feels wrong.

## Two of them are about reading, not remembering

[[Reading an Equilibrium Table]] and
[[Reading a Reduction Potential Table]] are a different kind of page
entirely, and they are the ones worth reading properly at least once.

Neither reproduces the numbers. The values you will use for $K_a$,
$K_b$, $K_{sp}$, and $E^\circ$ come from the **data booklet you are
given**, which is the authority, and a table printed on a website is
not. What these two pages teach is what those numbers mean, which
direction the table is sorted in, what you are permitted to conclude
from a comparison, and — in both cases — a specific operation that looks
obvious and is wrong.

> [!tip] Compare against your booklet early
> Data tables differ between sources in small ways: the exact value of a
> constant, the temperature the table was compiled at, whether
> potentials are printed as reductions or oxidations, how many
> significant figures are given. None of these pages can be
> authoritative over the booklet handed to you for an assessment.
>
> Put the two side by side in the first week of a unit rather than the
> night before a test. If you find a difference, raise it — noticing one
> is a good sign, not a nuisance, and it is exactly the habit
> [[Reading a Data Table]] is trying to build.
