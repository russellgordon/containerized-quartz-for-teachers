There are six labelled bottles on the bench. Every one of them is a
clear, colourless liquid. Every one of them looks exactly like water and
none of them behaves like water, and the differences are not small —
some of these will be gone off your skin before you have put the lid
back on, and one of them will still be sitting there in a minute.

Nobody has told you why yet. That is deliberate. Today you measure the
difference, and the account of it arrives afterwards.

## What you are trying to find out

A liquid becomes a gas when a molecule at the surface gets enough energy
to break away from its neighbours. So the question **how hard is it to
boil this liquid** is really the question **how strongly do its
molecules hold on to each other** — and that is a question about
structure, which is something you can draw.

You will not boil anything today. Boiling six organic liquids in a
school laboratory means six open containers of flammable vapour, and
there is a better measurement available anyway.

Instead you will measure **evaporative cooling**. Wrap a temperature
probe in a small piece of filter paper, wet it with a liquid, and watch
the temperature. Evaporation takes energy, and the energy comes out of
the probe, so the temperature falls. A liquid whose molecules hold on
tightly evaporates slowly and cools the probe a little; a liquid whose
molecules barely hold on evaporates fast and cools it a lot.

The number you take from each trial is $\Delta T$ — the **largest drop**
below the starting temperature, in degrees Celsius, as a positive
magnitude.

Two things you are really testing:

1. **Is there a pattern at all**, or are these six liquids just six
   liquids? Rank them by $\Delta T$ and see whether the ranking makes
   any structural sense.
2. **Does the pattern have exceptions?** If one liquid sits badly out of
   line with the rest, that liquid is the most interesting thing on the
   bench, and you should not smooth it away.

> [!question] The question worth carrying into the consolidation
> Two of the liquids you are given have very nearly the **same molar
> mass** and behave completely differently. Two others have the same
> functional group and differ only in the length of the carbon chain.
>
> If molar mass alone decided the answer, the first pair would match.
> They will not. So what else is there? You are not expected to know the
> answer before the bench — you are expected to notice, precisely, that
> the simple explanation has just failed, and to be able to say which
> pair broke it.

## What you have to work with

- **Six labelled liquids**, from a set that will include water, ethanol,
  propan-1-ol, butan-1-ol, hexane, and propanone. Their structural
  formulas are on the board.
- **A temperature probe or a thermometer** you can wrap. A probe with a
  data logger is far better here, because the minimum happens quickly
  and a human reading a glass thermometer will miss it.
- Small squares of filter paper, an elastic band or a small piece of
  tape, a stand and clamp.
- A stopwatch, and the room thermometer.
- **The fume hood**, which is where every liquid other than water is
  opened, used, and closed.

Your design decisions, written down and justified **before** you touch a
bottle:

- **What you will hold constant.** There are at least five candidates
  and you should name all of them: the volume of liquid applied, the
  size and type of the filter paper, the position of the probe in the
  air, the starting temperature of the probe, and the air movement
  around it. Say for each one **which way** a failure to control it
  would push $\Delta T$.
- **How you will apply the same amount each time.** "A few drops" is not
  a controlled variable. A dropping pipette held vertically, counting
  drops, is better; state your count.
- **How long you will wait between trials**, and how you will know the
  probe has recovered. A probe still cold from the last liquid gives a
  smaller $\Delta T$ for the next one, and the error runs downhill
  through the whole set.
- **How many trials per liquid**, and what agreement between repeats you
  will accept before moving on.
- **The order you will test them in.** If you always test in the same
  order and the room warms up over the period, order and temperature are
  confounded. Say what you will do about that.

> [!danger] Flammable liquids — the vapour is the hazard, not the liquid
> Five of the six liquids on this bench are flammable, and one of them
> is not water.
>
> - **No flames anywhere in the room, and no hot plates on.** Not at
>   your bench, not at somebody else's, not "just for a minute". Solvent
>   vapour is heavier than air, it flows along the bench and down onto
>   the floor, and it will reach an ignition source you had not thought
>   was nearby. The liquid in the bottle is not what catches — the
>   invisible layer above it is.
> - **Everything except the water is opened, used, and closed inside the
>   fume hood**, with the sash at the marked height. Keep every bottle
>   closed between uses. An open bottle is a vapour source even when
>   nobody is using it.
> - **Waft, do not sniff.** These have strong odours and you do not need
>   to smell them to do this lab. Repeatedly inhaling solvent vapour is
>   a real exposure, not a joke.
> - **Skin contact defats your skin.** These solvents dissolve the oils
>   that keep your skin intact, which is why hexane and propanone leave
>   your hands feeling stripped. Gloves if they are provided, and wash
>   with soap and water at the end regardless.
> - **Eye protection on for the whole period**, including while you are
>   waiting for a probe to recover and including during cleanup.
> - **Nothing goes down the sink and nothing goes back into stock.**
>   Used filter paper goes in the labelled solvent waste container in the
>   hood, not the regular bin, where a pile of solvent-soaked paper is a
>   fire waiting for a source.
> - **Report every spill immediately**, however small, and do not mop it
>   up with paper towel and drop it in the bin.

## The prediction you write first

Before you open anything, in your notebook, with today's class heading
on it:

1. **Your ranking of all six liquids** from largest expected $\Delta T$
   to smallest. Commit to an order. A tie is allowed only if you say
   which two and why you cannot separate them.
2. **The reason for your top and your bottom.** One sentence each, about
   the structures on the board rather than about the labels.
3. **The pair you expect to be closest together**, and the pair you
   expect to be furthest apart.
4. **A number.** For the liquid you put first, guess $\Delta T$ in
   degrees Celsius, to the nearest whole degree. You will almost
   certainly be wrong and it costs you nothing, but a guessed magnitude
   is what turns "it cooled" into a measurement you can be surprised by.

A prediction written after the data is not a prediction and it shows,
every time.

Repeat each liquid at least twice and record every trial, not the one
you liked. Temperatures to the resolution your instrument actually has
— a probe reading to 0.1 °C gives you 0.1 °C, and writing 21.43 °C from
it is a claim you cannot support.

Then two things that are not measurements:

- **Look up the boiling point of each liquid** in the data booklet, and
  add it as a column. You now have a measured quantity and a published
  one that ought to be related.
- **Plot $\Delta T$ against boiling point** for all six. Not a bar chart
  of $\Delta T$ — a scatter plot with one point per liquid. If the two
  quantities are measuring the same underlying thing, the points fall on
  a trend. If one liquid sits off the trend, circle it.

## What to bring to the consolidation

- Your prediction, dated before the data, with the parts you got wrong
  left visible.
- Your completed table and your scatter plot.
- **The pair with similar molar masses**, side by side, with the
  difference in $\Delta T$ stated as a number. This is the pair that
  kills the simplest explanation and it is where the discussion starts.
- **The homologous series** — ethanol, propan-1-ol, butan-1-ol — in
  order, with the trend stated as a direction and a rough size per
  carbon added.
- **One structural feature** you think is doing the work, and the
  evidence from your own table that points at it. You are not expected
  to have the vocabulary yet. You are expected to point at the right
  part of the molecule.
- **The class spread.** Put every group's ranking on the board. A
  ranking everybody agrees on is evidence about the liquids; a ranking
  that varies group by group is evidence about the method.[^1]

[^1]: Watch what happens if one group worked at a bench near the door.
    Moving air carries evaporated molecules away from the surface, which
    stops them returning to the liquid, which speeds up net evaporation
    and **increases** $\Delta T$ for every liquid that group tested.
    That group's ranking may still be right even though every one of its
    numbers is too large — which is a useful thing to notice early in
    the year. A systematic error can leave an ordering intact while
    making every value untrustworthy, and an ordering is often all you
    needed.

## What you should not claim

- **You did not measure boiling points.** You measured how fast a small
  amount of liquid evaporated at room temperature. The two are related
  and they are not the same quantity, and the write-up has to keep them
  apart.
- **You cannot claim a cause from a ranking.** A correlation between
  $\Delta T$ and molecular size is a pattern. Saying that size *causes*
  the difference requires a mechanism, and the mechanism is what the
  next few classes are for.
- **Draughts push every reading the same way**, and they push it
  **up**. Air moving past the probe removes vapour and increases the
  rate of evaporation, so $\Delta T$ comes out larger than it should. If
  your bench was near a door, a fume hood, or a fan, say so and say that
  your values are overestimates.
- **A probe that had not recovered pushes the next reading down.**
  Starting a trial from a temperature below room temperature gives you
  less room to fall, and $\Delta T$ comes out too small. If you did not
  wait, the error runs through the rest of your set in one direction.
- **Two trials are not a spread.** They tell you whether you can repeat
  yourself once. They do not tell you the uncertainty in the mean, and
  quoting a mean of two values to three decimals is a claim the data
  cannot support.
- **The liquid that sat off the trend is not a mistake.** Report it as
  measured. An anomaly you kept is worth more than a smooth graph you
  produced by dropping a point, and this particular anomaly is the
  reason the next class exists.
