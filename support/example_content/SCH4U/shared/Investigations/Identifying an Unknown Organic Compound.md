Your group gets one coded bottle. The code means nothing. What you do
know is that the compound inside is one of six, that all six are written
on the board with their structures, and that nobody is going to tell you
which.

You have a bench of tests and a period. The interesting question is not
"which one is it" — you will get there. The interesting question is
**how few tests it takes**, and whether you can say, before you run
each one, exactly what each possible outcome would rule out.

## What you are trying to find out

A functional group is a claim that a particular arrangement of atoms
behaves in a particular way **wherever it appears**. If that claim is
true, then a test that responds to the group should respond the same way
in any compound carrying it, regardless of what the rest of the molecule
looks like.

That is what makes identification possible at all, and it is what you
are really testing today.

Two things you are after:

1. **Can a handful of cheap tests name a compound you have never met?**
2. **What does a negative result buy you?** A test that does nothing is
   not a wasted test — it eliminates candidates, and a well-chosen
   negative can eliminate more than a positive does. Your design should
   exploit that.

## The six candidates

All six are on the board with structures. They are:

| Candidate | Class | What is on it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethanoic acid, dilute | Carboxylic acid | $-\ce{COOH}$ |
| Propan-1-ol | Alcohol | $-\ce{OH}$ |
| Propanone | Ketone | $\ce{C}=\ce{O}$ inside the chain |
| Propanal | Aldehyde | $\ce{C}=\ce{O}$ at the end of the chain |
| Ethyl ethanoate | Ester | $-\ce{COO}-$ |
| Cyclohexene | Alkene | $\ce{C}=\ce{C}$ |

Two of those differ only in **where** the carbonyl group sits, and they
have the same molecular formula. If your scheme cannot separate
propanal from propanone, it has not finished.

## What you have to work with

**Tests you may run yourself:**

- **Solubility in water.** Equal small volumes, shaken, allowed to
  stand. Record whether it mixes completely, partly, or not at all.
- **pH.** Universal indicator paper or a probe, on the aqueous mixture.
- **The hydrogen carbonate test.** A few drops of the unknown into
  sodium hydrogen carbonate solution. Steady effervescence is a
  positive:
  $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + NaHCO3(aq) -> CH3COONa(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}$
- **Odour, by wafting only**, in the fume hood, once, briefly. This is a
  real piece of evidence and it is also the weakest one you have. Treat
  it as a tiebreaker, never as a decider.

**Tests I run as demonstrations, in the fume hood, at the front:**

- **Bromine water.** An alkene decolourises it rapidly by addition
  across the double bond,
  $\ce{C6H10 + Br2 -> C6H10Br2}$,
  and the others do not. Bromine water is corrosive and its vapour is
  toxic, so this one is mine.
- **Benedict's solution, in a thermostatic water bath.** An aldehyde is
  oxidised and reduces the copper(II) in the reagent to a brick-red
  copper(I) oxide precipitate. A ketone does not, because a ketone's
  carbonyl has no hydrogen on it to lose. This one needs a hot bath in
  the same room as flammable liquids, so it is also mine.

You may request either demonstration for your unknown, **but you have to
say first what you expect and what the result would eliminate.** A
request without a prediction is a request to be told the answer.

Your design decisions, written before you start:

- **Your decision tree**, drawn, with a test at every branch point and a
  candidate list at every leaf. The shape from
  [[Building and Testing Models]] is the right shape for this.
- **Your test order, and why that order.** A first test that splits six
  candidates into three and three is worth more than one that splits
  them into one and five, unless the one it isolates is the one you
  suspect. Argue your case.
- **What each possible outcome eliminates** — for every test, both ways.
  Write it down before you run anything, because the temptation to
  reinterpret a test after seeing its result is enormous and it is
  exactly what this exercise is designed to expose.
- **Your stopping rule.** How many tests must agree before you name a
  compound? Decide now.

> [!danger] Flammable organics, a corrosive demonstration, and dirty tubes
> Every candidate except the dilute ethanoic acid is flammable, and the
> vapour is the hazard rather than the liquid.
>
> - **No flames anywhere in the room, and no hot plate at any bench.**
>   Solvent vapour is heavier than air, travels along the bench, and
>   finds an ignition source you did not know was there. The only heat
>   in this room today is the thermostatic water bath at the front, and
>   it is at the front for that reason.
> - **All work with the unknowns happens in the fume hood**, with the
>   sash at the marked height, and bottles are closed between uses. An
>   open bottle is a vapour source whether or not anybody is using it.
> - **Bromine water is corrosive and its vapour attacks your airways.**
>   You do not handle it. Watch from the marked line, and if you can
>   smell it at the line, tell me and step back — that is information
>   about the hood, not about your nose.
> - **Waft, once, briefly, and only in the hood.** Odour is a legitimate
>   test here and it is also the one students most often overdo. Cup a
>   hand, draw the air toward you, and stop. Never put your face over a
>   container.
> - **Ethanoic acid is an acid even when it is dilute**, and it stings on
>   broken skin and in eyes. Eye protection stays on from the first move
>   to the last of the cleanup.
> - **A dirty test tube is a safety problem and a data problem at once.**
>   Acid carried over from a previous group's tube produces
>   effervescence with hydrogen carbonate that has nothing to do with
>   your unknown, and it will send you confidently to the wrong answer.
>   Rinse, rinse again, and rinse with distilled water last.
> - **Nothing is tasted, ever**, including the one that smells like pear
>   drops and the one that smells like nail varnish. Both of those
>   smells are on this bench and neither of them is food.
> - **Nothing goes down the sink and nothing returns to stock.** Organic
>   waste to the labelled container in the hood. Wash your hands before
>   you leave the room.

## The prediction you write first

Before you open your coded bottle:

1. **Your decision tree**, complete, with all six candidates placed.
2. **The number of tests your tree needs in the worst case**, and in the
   best case. State both numbers.
3. **A guess at your unknown**, from the bottle's appearance and nothing
   else, with a confidence in words. You are allowed to be wrong; you
   are not allowed to write this afterwards.
4. **The test you expect to be least useful**, and why. Then run it
   anyway, and see whether you were right about your own design.

## Bench checklist

Work down this list in order — it is in this order for a reason.

- [ ] Decision tree drawn and checked by me before any bottle is opened
- [ ] Eye protection on, fume hood sash at the marked height
- [ ] Bottle code recorded in the notebook, exactly as written
- [ ] Every tube rinsed and rinsed again with distilled water
- [ ] Solubility test run with **equal** volumes, both recorded
- [ ] pH recorded as a value, not as "acidic"
- [ ] Hydrogen carbonate test run alongside a **known** ethanoic acid
      control in an identical tube
- [ ] Every observation written at the bench, not reconstructed after
- [ ] Prediction stated to me **before** either demonstration is
      requested
- [ ] All waste in the labelled organic container
- [ ] Bench wiped, glassware washed, hands washed

That control tube in the middle of the list is not padding. Running a
known positive next to your unknown, in the same glassware, at the same
time, is what turns "it fizzed a bit, I think" into a comparison you can
defend — and it is the single technique that most improves the quality
of a qualitative result.

## What to collect

| Test | What I expected | What I observed | What this eliminates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solubility in water | | | |
| pH | | | |
| Sodium hydrogen carbonate | | | |
| Odour (wafted, once) | | | |
| Bromine water (demonstration) | | | |
| Benedict's (demonstration) | | | |

The fourth column is the one that matters and it is the one people leave
until the end. Fill it in **as you go**, from the tree you drew, not
afterwards from the answer you settled on.

Then a second table, which is your actual conclusion:

| Candidate | Still possible after all tests? | The single result that ruled it out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethanoic acid | | |
| Propan-1-ol | | |
| Propanone | | |
| Propanal | | |
| Ethyl ethanoate | | |
| Cyclohexene | | |

If more than one row still says "possible", you have not finished, and
saying so is a better outcome than picking the one that feels right.

## What to bring to the consolidation

- Your decision tree as drawn beforehand, with any branch you had to
  repair marked and dated.
- Both tables, complete.
- **Your identification, with the evidence chain**: which results, in
  which order, closed which doors. A conclusion is the chain, not the
  name at the end of it.
- **The number of tests you actually used**, against the number your
  tree predicted. If you used more, say what went wrong with the tree.
- **The class table.** Every group's code and identification on the
  board. Two groups with the same code and different answers is the most
  productive thing that can happen in this lab, and at least one pair of
  groups usually manages it.
- **One test you would add** if the candidate list were larger, and what
  it would separate.

## What you should not claim

- **You did not prove an identity — you eliminated alternatives.** Your
  evidence supports one candidate **from a list of six that I gave you**.
  Hand the same bottle to a chemist with no list and these tests would
  narrow it to a class, not to a compound. Say "consistent with", and
  say what the list was.
- **A negative result is weaker than it feels.** A test can fail because
  the group is absent, and it can also fail because the concentration
  was too low, the tube was contaminated, the reagent was old, or the
  bath was not hot enough. A negative eliminates a candidate only if you
  are confident the test would have worked. Your control tube is the
  evidence for that confidence, which is why it is on the checklist.
- **Odour is evidence and it is weak evidence.** People differ enormously
  in what they can detect, description is subjective, and two esters can
  smell almost identical. If your identification rests on a smell, say
  so plainly, and treat it as the weakest link in the chain.
- **Carry-over pushes you toward acids.** Residue in an imperfectly
  rinsed tube adds a false positive far more often than it removes a
  true one, and the most common contaminant on a bench like this is
  acid. So the systematic error here has a direction: it makes you
  **over-report carboxylic acids**. If your identification is ethanoic
  acid on the strength of one fizz, rinse and repeat before you commit.
- **You cannot claim purity.** Every test you ran responds to a
  functional group and none of them counts molecules. A bottle
  containing 95% of one compound and 5% of another would behave, on
  this bench, exactly like the pure compound.
- **Two compounds with the same formula are not the same substance.**
  Propanal and propanone are both $\ce{C3H6O}$. If
  your chain never distinguished them, your answer is
  "$\ce{C3H6O}$, carbonyl present, position not
  established" — which is a real result, honestly stated.
