In [[Identifying an Unknown Organic Compound]] you were handed a
labelled-off bottle and a short list of tests, and asked to say what
class of compound was in it. Nobody expected you to name the exact
substance. The tests do not distinguish butan-1-ol from pentan-1-ol —
and they do not need to, because for most purposes those two behave the
same way.

That is the idea this page is about. The long hydrocarbon part of an
organic molecule is fairly inert. Almost everything interesting happens
at one small site, and knowing which site is there tells you most of
what the substance will do.

## The group is the reactive site

A **functional group** is a specific arrangement of atoms that gives a
molecule a characteristic set of chemical and physical properties. The
rest of the molecule — written $\text{R}$ when we do not care about
it — is scenery.

There is a reason it works this way, and you already have it. A chain of
carbons and hydrogens has almost no electronegativity difference across
any of its bonds, so it is non-polar, unreactive, and holds itself
together with dispersion forces alone. Introduce an oxygen or a nitrogen
and you introduce a $\delta^+$ and a $\delta^-$ — a place where the
electrons are unevenly shared, which is exactly where another reagent
can attack. [[Polarity]] said reactivity would come back. This is it.

So organic compounds are sorted into **classes** by their group, and a
class is a genuine prediction rather than a filing convenience. Given
the class you can state, before touching the bottle, roughly how it will
boil, whether it will dissolve, whether it is acidic, and what it will
react with.

## The classes

| Class | Group | Suffix | Named on | Distinguishing property |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alkane | C–C only | `-ane` | [[Naming Organic Compounds\|the root]] | non-polar, unreactive, insoluble |
| Alkene | $\ce{C=C}$ | `-ene` | [[Isomers\|geometric isomers possible]] | decolourises bromine water |
| Alkyne | $\ce{C#C}$ | `-yne` | the root | as alkene, more so |
| Aromatic | benzene ring | — | the ring keeps its name | unusually stable for its unsaturation |
| Alcohol | $-\ce{OH}$ | `-ol` | [[Naming Organic Compounds\|position numbered]] | hydrogen bonds; oxidises |
| Aldehyde | $-\ce{CHO}$ at a chain end | `-al` | always carbon 1 | oxidises easily to an acid |
| Ketone | $\ce{C=O}$ within the chain | `-one` | position numbered | resists further oxidation |
| Carboxylic acid | $-\ce{COOH}$ | `-oic acid` | always carbon 1 | weakly acidic; sharp odour |
| Ester | $-\ce{COO}-$ | `-oate` | two parts, two names | fruity odour; hydrolyses |
| Ether | $\text{R}-\ce{O}-\text{R}'$ | `alkoxy-` prefix | named as a substituent | low boiling; very flammable |
| Amine | $-\ce{NH2}$ | `-amine` | position numbered | weakly basic; fishy odour |
| Amide | $-\ce{CONH2}$ | `-amide` | always carbon 1 | very high boiling; **not** basic |

The compact version, with structures drawn out, is
[[Functional Groups at a Glance]] — keep it open while you work rather
than reading it.

Two rows in that table repay a second look. The **ketone** resists
oxidation while the aldehyde does not, and the reason is structural: an
aldehyde's carbonyl carbon still carries a hydrogen that can be removed,
and a ketone's, buried mid-chain, does not. And the **amide** is not
basic although it contains nitrogen, because the nitrogen's lone pair is
pulled into the neighbouring $\ce{C=O}$ and is no longer available to
accept a proton. In both cases the property is a consequence of the
arrangement, not an extra fact.

## Boiling points follow the group

Take a set of compounds of roughly the same molar mass, one from each
class, and their boiling points come out in this order every time:

$$\text{alkane} < \text{ether} < \text{aldehyde} \approx \text{ketone} < \text{alcohol} < \text{carboxylic acid} < \text{amide}$$

Every step in that sequence is [[Intermolecular Forces]] applied
directly:

- The **alkane** has dispersion forces only.
- The **ether** has a bent, polar $\ce{C}-\ce{O}-\ce{C}$ and so
  has dipole–dipole attraction — but no hydrogen attached to its oxygen,
  so it cannot hydrogen bond to another ether molecule.
- The **aldehyde and ketone** have a strongly polar $\ce{C=O}$,
  giving a bigger dipole than an ether's, and still no $\ce{O}-\ce{H}$.
- The **alcohol** has $\ce{O}-\ce{H}$ and hydrogen bonds properly,
  which is a large jump rather than a small one.
- The **carboxylic acid** has both an $\ce{O}-\ce{H}$ to donate and
  two oxygens to accept, so a pair of molecules can lock together
  through two hydrogen bonds at once. It behaves in part like a
  molecule of twice the size.
- The **amide** has $\ce{N}-\ce{H}$ hydrogen bonding along with the
  strongest carbonyl of the set, and boils highest of all. That the
  amide link makes such tenacious solids is precisely why nylon is
  worth making — see [[Polymers]].

Within a class, boiling point rises with chain length, for the ordinary
dispersion reason. So there are two variables and you have to say which
one you are holding still.

## Solubility, odour, and telling them apart

**Solubility in water** needs the same argument. A short-chain alcohol,
aldehyde, ketone, acid, or amine dissolves readily, because the group
can hydrogen bond with water — donating, accepting, or both. As the
hydrocarbon tail grows, the non-polar part comes to dominate and the
solubility collapses; the changeover for alcohols sits at around four or
five carbons. Methanol mixes with water in any proportion. Octan-1-ol
essentially does not, and it has the same functional group.

**Odour** is a real diagnostic and this is the point at which safety
stops being a formality. Esters smell of fruit; carboxylic acids smell
sharp and rancid; amines smell of decay. In the laboratory you
==waft rather than sniff==, you work in a fume hood where the procedure
says so, and you never put your face over a container to find out what
is in it.
