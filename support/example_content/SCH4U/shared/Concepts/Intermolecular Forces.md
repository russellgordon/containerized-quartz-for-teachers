The boiling points you collected in [[Boiling Points and Structure]] did
not sort by molar mass, and that was the point of collecting them. Two
liquids of nearly the same mass boiled more than a hundred degrees
apart. Something other than how heavy a molecule is decides how hard it
is to pull one away from its neighbours.

This page names that something. It is the last floor of Unit 1: shape
gave you [[Polarity]], and polarity gives you this.

## Boiling breaks nothing

The single most useful sentence in this topic:
==boiling a molecular substance breaks no chemical bonds at all.==

Steam is still $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Every O–H bond that existed in the
liquid exists in the vapour. What boiling does is separate whole
molecules from each other, and the attractions holding molecules
*together* — **intermolecular** forces — are far weaker than the bonds
holding a molecule *internally* — **intramolecular** bonds.

That is why the numbers are so different. Boiling water takes a modest
amount of energy per mole. Breaking the O–H bonds inside water molecules
takes far more, and does not happen in a kettle.

## Three forces, and one of them is everywhere

**London dispersion forces** exist between *every* pair of particles,
polar or not, atomic or molecular. Electrons are not static. At any
instant the electron cloud of a molecule is momentarily lopsided, giving
a fleeting dipole, which induces a matching lopsidedness in a
neighbour — and the two attract. The instant passes and another takes
its place.

Their strength grows with **polarisability**: how easily the cloud
distorts. More electrons, and electrons held further out, mean a squashier
cloud and a stronger force. That is why the halogens go from gas to
liquid to solid down the group with nothing else changing:
$\text{F}_2$ and $\text{Cl}_2$ are gases, $\text{Br}_2$ is a liquid, and
$\text{I}_2$ is a solid at room temperature, and every one of them is
non-polar.

Shape matters too, because dispersion needs contact. Pentane, a straight
chain, boils around 36 °C. Its branched isomer 2,2-dimethylpropane is a
compact ball with less surface to lie against a neighbour, and boils
around 10 °C — same formula, same mass, more than twenty-five degrees
apart.

**Dipole–dipole forces** act between molecules with permanent dipoles.
The $\delta^+$ end of one is attracted to the $\delta^-$ end of another,
and unlike dispersion this attraction does not flicker. It is additional
to dispersion, never instead of it.

**Hydrogen bonding** is the strong case, and it has a narrow, strict
condition: a hydrogen atom bonded **directly** to nitrogen, oxygen, or
fluorine, attracted to a lone pair on the nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine
of a neighbouring molecule. Nothing else qualifies. The C–H bonds in
hexane do not hydrogen bond and never will, no matter how many hydrogens
there are.

Why only those three elements? They are small and highly
electronegative, so the bonding pair is dragged far off the hydrogen —
and a hydrogen with its only electron pulled away is a bare proton with
nothing shielding it. That concentrated, exposed positive charge sits
very close to the neighbour's lone pair, and closeness is what makes it
strong.

> [!warning] Hydrogen bonding is not a bond
> The name is historical and it has misled generations of students. A
> hydrogen bond is an **attraction between molecules**, in the same
> family as the other two forces on this page, and it is a small
> fraction of the strength of the covalent bond inside the molecule.
> Writing "the hydrogen bonds break when water boils" is correct.
> Writing "the bonds in water break when water boils" is not, and the
> two sentences are one word apart.

## Ranking them from evidence

Within a set of similar molecules, the ordering is dispersion <
dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding. But ==size beats type== often enough
that the ranking is useless applied blindly.

| Comparison | Which is stronger, and why |
| --- | --- |
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ against $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ | water, hugely — hydrogen bonding against dipole–dipole |
| Hexane against methanol | almost a tie — hexane's dispersion nearly matches methanol's hydrogen bonding |
| $\text{I}_2$ against $\text{HCl}$ | iodine — dispersion in a big polarisable molecule beats a small permanent dipole |
| Pentane against 2,2-dimethylpropane | pentane — same force, more contact area |

The second row is the one that should unsettle you. Hexane is
non-polar and boils around 69 °C; methanol hydrogen bonds and boils
around 65 °C. Hexane is much larger, and its dispersion forces, summed
over a long chain, come out roughly equal to a hydrogen bond. Never
argue from the type of force alone.

The cleanest evidence for hydrogen bonding is a graph you can construct
from a data table. Boiling points of the group 16 hydrides climb steadily
with mass — $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, then $\text{H}_2\text{Se}$, then
$\text{H}_2\text{Te}$ — exactly as dispersion predicts. Extend that line
backwards and water should boil far below zero. It boils at 100 °C. The
gap between the line and the measurement is what hydrogen bonding is
worth, and the same gap appears for $\text{NH}_3$ and $\text{HF}$ and
for nothing else in those rows.

Water pays for that in ways that matter: a high heat capacity that makes
lakes slow to warm, a surface tension that holds insects up, and — because
hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice when it freezes — a
solid less dense than its liquid. Ice floats. Almost nothing else does.

## The four kinds of solid

Cool a substance far enough and what you get depends on what the
particles are and what holds them.

| Type of solid | Particles | Held together by | Melting point | Conducts electricity? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ionic | cations and anions | electrostatic attraction throughout the lattice | high | not when solid; yes when molten or dissolved |
| Molecular | whole molecules | intermolecular forces | low | no |
| Covalent network | atoms | covalent bonds running through the whole crystal | very high | usually not — graphite is the exception |
| Metallic | cations in a sea of delocalised electrons | metallic bonding | variable, often high | yes, solid or molten |

Two predictions fall straight out. Ionic solids are **brittle**: strike
one and a layer shifts by half a lattice spacing, like charges come face
to face, and the crystal splits along a plane. Metals are **malleable**
for the opposite reason — the layers slide and the electron sea simply
follows, with no arrangement to disrupt.

Sugar and sand look similarly granular and behave nothing alike, because
sugar is molecular and quartz is a covalent network. Predicting which
one a substance forms, and what follows from it, is the whole assignment
in [[The Property Prediction]].

> [!question] Why does a covalent network melt so high?
> Because there is no such thing as "a molecule of quartz". Melting a
> molecular solid overcomes attractions *between* molecules. Melting a
> covalent network means breaking actual covalent bonds, millions of
> them, all the way through the sample. The difference in melting point
> is the difference between those two jobs.
