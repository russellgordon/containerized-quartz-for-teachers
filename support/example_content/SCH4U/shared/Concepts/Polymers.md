A demonstration you will not forget: two clear liquids poured carefully
into a beaker so that they sit in layers, and a solid film forming at
the boundary where they touch. Catch the film with tweezers, pull, and a
continuous thread comes out of the beaker for as long as anyone keeps
pulling. Nothing was heated. Nothing was stirred. The thread is nylon,
and it was not there a minute ago.

That is a **polymerisation**, and it is the same chemistry as
[[Organic Reactions#Esterification and hydrolysis]] performed a few
thousand times in a row.

## A polymer is a repeat

A **monomer** is a small molecule that can join to others of its kind. A
**polymer** is the long chain that results, and the section that repeats
along it is the **repeating unit**. Polythene bags, protein, cotton,
DNA, and the resin in a hockey stick are all polymers, which tells you
the word describes an architecture rather than a substance.

The properties come from the architecture as much as from the chemistry.
A long chain has an enormous surface along which dispersion forces act,
so polymers are solids where their monomers are gases or thin liquids.
Chains that pack closely give a stiff, dense, opaque material; chains
with awkward branches cannot pack and give something softer and more
transparent. Same repeating unit, different processing, different
product.

There are exactly two ways to join monomers at this level, and the
difference between them is the whole of this page.

## Addition polymerisation

The monomer must contain a **carbon–carbon double bond**. Under heat,
pressure, or a catalyst, the second bond of the pair opens and the
monomers link end to end.

$$n\,\text{CH}_2\text{=CH}_2 \rightarrow [\text{--CH}_2\text{--CH}_2\text{--}]_n$$

The defining feature: ==nothing leaves.== Every atom that was in the
monomers is in the polymer. The repeating unit has exactly the same
formula as the monomer it came from, which is the test you can apply on
paper without knowing anything about the conditions.

Ethene gives polyethene, propene gives polypropene, chloroethene gives
PVC, and phenylethene gives polystyrene. All four have the same
backbone — a plain chain of carbon atoms — and differ only in what hangs
off it.

## Condensation polymerisation

Here each monomer needs **two reactive ends**, and the two ends react
with each other. Every time a link forms, a small molecule — almost
always water — is expelled from the join.

A diol and a dicarboxylic acid give a **polyester**, joined by ester
links. A diamine and a dicarboxylic acid give a **polyamide**, joined by
amide links — that is nylon, and it is why the thread came out of the
beaker at the boundary where the two layers met.

$$\text{HO--R--OH} + \text{HOOC--R}'\text{--COOH} \rightarrow \text{--O--R--O--CO--R}'\text{--CO--} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Nature does this constantly. Amino acids joined into proteins lose a
water molecule at every peptide link. Glucose units joined into starch
or cellulose lose one at every link too. Every one of those joins is a
condensation, and it is not a coincidence that digestion — running the
reaction backwards with water and an enzyme — is a hydrolysis.

> [!example] Telling them apart from the formulas alone
> You are given a monomer, $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$, and a repeating unit
> written $[\text{--CH}_2\text{--CH}_2\text{--}]$. Count the atoms: two
> carbons and four hydrogens on each side. Nothing is missing, so it was
> **addition**, and the monomer must therefore have contained a double
> bond.
>
> Now a monomer pair totalling, between them, some number of hydrogens
> and oxygens, and a repeating unit two hydrogens and one oxygen short.
> One water molecule has gone missing per link, so it was
> **condensation**, and the monomers must therefore have had two
> functional ends each.
>
> That is the entire distinction, and you can apply it without ever
> being told which reaction was run.

## Why the difference decides the waste

This is the part that reaches outside the classroom, and it follows
directly from the backbone.

An **addition polymer** has a backbone of nothing but carbon–carbon
bonds, with hydrogens or halogens along it. There is no polar site, no
carbonyl, nothing for water to attack — which is exactly the property
that makes polythene useful for a container and exactly the property
that makes it persist in the environment for a very long time. It is
inert by construction. Burning it is possible and heating it can be
made to break it down, but water and micro-organisms are slow to.

A **condensation polymer** has ester or amide links *in the chain
itself*. Those are precisely the bonds that hydrolyse. A polyester can
be broken back down to its monomers with the right conditions, which is
what chemical recycling of drinks bottles does, and natural
condensation polymers are digested by living things every day.

> [!question] Is the reactive one the better material?
> Not simply. The property that lets a polyester be recycled back to
> monomer is the same property that makes it less durable in hot, wet
> conditions. A material that never breaks down and a material that
> breaks down when you would rather it did not are two sides of one
> structural fact, and choosing between them is an engineering
> judgement rather than a chemical one. Bring that argument to
> [[Chemistry We Live With]].

Trace one polymer from monomer to product to disposal in
[[The Molecule Dossier]], and remember that both routes here started
from the small-molecule reactions in [[Organic Reactions]]. Nothing new
was needed to make a polymer — only the decision to give the monomer two
ends.
