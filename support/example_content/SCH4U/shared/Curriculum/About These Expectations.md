The expectations in this folder are reproduced from **The Ontario Curriculum,
Grades 11 and 12: Science, 2008 (revised)**, the university-preparation course
**SCH4U**, published by the Ontario Ministry of Education. SCH4U occupies
pages 104 to 117 of that document — the fourteen pages immediately after the
Grade 11 course you took last year.

> [!info] The official source
> [The Ontario Curriculum, Grades 11 and 12: Science, 2008 (revised)](https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf)
>
> The Ministry's current curriculum site lists the course here:
> [Chemistry, Grade 12, University Preparation (SCH4U)](https://www.dcp.edu.gov.on.ca/en/curriculum/secondary-science)
>
> © Queen's Printer for Ontario, 2008. Reproduced for educational use.

Each page keeps the expectation's official **code** — `C2.3`, `E3.5`, `F2.4`,
and so on — and every lesson, investigation, exercise set, and task in this
course links back to the codes it addresses. The codes are also tags, so every
page tagged `#E3` can be gathered in one place. They are the document's own.

## Grade 12 chemistry is still the 2008 curriculum

Ontario replaced the **Grade 9** science course in 2022 — the de-streamed
SNC1W — and left Grades 10 through 12 alone. The 2022 document says so itself:

> Educators should be aware that, with the exception of the Grade 9 Science
> course, 2022 (SNC1W), the 2008 Science curriculum for Grade 10 and the 2008
> Science curriculum for Grades 11–12 remain in effect.

Nothing here is out of date as a result. SCH4U's prerequisite is SCH3U, and
de-streaming did not touch it.

## What the abbreviations in square brackets mean

Most expectations in strands B through F end with something like `[PR, AI, C]`.
Those are printed, and they point back at Strand A:

| Code | The skill area |
| --- | --- |
| IP | Initiating and planning |
| PR | Performing and recording |
| AI | Analysing and interpreting |
| C | Communicating |

Strand A is never taught as a unit of its own — it runs through every
investigation, and the brackets show which part of it each piece of work
exercises. The "Understanding Basic Concepts" groups (`B3`, `C3`, `D3`, `E3`,
`F3`) carry no brackets, and neither do Strand A's own expectations, which also
open with *Throughout this course, students will* rather than *By the end of
this course, students will*.

## What the document prints, and what it leaves to us

**Sample issues and sample questions** sit beside ten expectations in the
printed document — planning support for teachers rather than expectation
wording — and are not reproduced here.

**There are almost no symbols.** This is worth knowing before you go looking.
The Grade 12 chemistry curriculum names equilibrium constants, rate laws, cell
notation, organic structures, and standard reduction potentials **in prose**,
and prints no chemical equations at all — not one reaction arrow in fourteen
pages. What it does typeset, these pages typeset: the shapes list in `C2.3`
($\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{SO3}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{H2O}$,
$\ce{NH4+}$), the equilibrium constants ($K_{eq}$, $K_{sp}$, $K_{w}$,
$K_{a}$, $K_{b}$, $K_{p}$), the ions in `E3.5`, and the two thermochemical
symbols.

> [!note] Two small oddities, reported rather than reproduced
> The document prints $\Delta H$ completely bare — no superscript circle, no
> subscript, no standard-state mark — so these pages do too, even though your
> textbook will very likely write $\Delta H^\circ$ or $\Delta H_f$. Do not read
> the absence as a claim about conditions.
>
> In `E3.5` the printed negative charge on hydroxide is set with an en dash
> rather than a minus sign, and the positive charge on hydronium is set in bold
> where nothing around it is. Both are typesetting slips in the original. They
> are set correctly here, because reproducing them would look like emphasis
> rather than fidelity.

## Why the codes matter more than they look

A code is a shared address. When you ask "why are we doing this?", the answer is
a link. When a department writes a course outline, or a teacher takes over a
class partway through the semester, the codes are what make one person's
planning legible to another.
