This is the reference page — how to read a label, where the information
lives, and the practices that prevent almost every accident in a school
lab. The agreement you sign before your first investigation is
[[Safety in the Lab]]; this page is what that agreement is built on.

You have read a version of this before. Read it again, because this
course is not last year's course with harder arithmetic. You will use
flammable organic solvents, concentrated bases, and cells that make
hydrogen while you watch, and each of those brings a hazard that Grade
11 could avoid entirely.

## WHMIS in one paragraph

WHMIS — the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System — is
Canada's law-backed system for telling anyone who handles a hazardous
product what it can do to them and what to do about it. It is harmonised
with the international system used in most of the world, so a label
printed in Germany and one printed in Ontario carry the same symbols. It
has three parts: **labels** on containers, a **safety data sheet** for
each product, and **training** for the people using it. That third part
is this page, and the class it comes from.

## The nine pictograms, by what they are warning you about

The picture is a summary, not the instruction. It tells you what kind of
harm is possible so you know which precautions to read first.

| Pictogram | What is drawn | What it signals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flame | A flame | Flammable and combustible materials, including ones that ignite in air, heat themselves, or release flammable gas on contact with water |
| Flame over circle | A flame above a circle | Oxidizing materials — they feed a fire, and can start one in things that would not otherwise burn |
| Gas cylinder | A gas cylinder | Gases under pressure, including compressed, liquefied, refrigerated, and dissolved gases |
| Corrosion | Liquid dropping onto a hand and onto a surface | Corrosive to skin and to eyes; some are corrosive to metals as well |
| Exploding bomb | A bursting sphere | Explosives, and the self-reactive materials and organic peroxides that can detonate |
| Skull and crossbones | A skull above crossed bones | Acute toxicity — serious harm or death from a small amount, quickly |
| Health hazard | A starburst inside a human torso | Serious, often delayed effects — cancer, reproductive and genetic harm, respiratory sensitisation, organ damage, aspiration hazard |
| Exclamation mark | An exclamation mark | Less severe effects — skin and eye irritation, skin sensitisation, drowsiness or dizziness |
| Biohazardous infectious materials | Three crescents arranged around a central circle | Micro-organisms and their toxins that cause disease in people or animals |

You will also meet a **dead fish and dead tree** symbol on many labels,
marking hazards to the aquatic environment. It comes from the same
international system, though it is not one that WHMIS itself requires in
Canada. Treat it as real information: it is telling you the product must
not go down the sink.

> [!important] The two you will see most this year, and the one you will underrate
> The organic unit puts the **flame** on almost every bottle you touch,
> and the acid–base and electrochemistry units put **corrosion** on most
> of the rest. Those two you will take seriously because they look
> alarming.
>
> The one people skim past is the **health hazard** starburst. It means
> the damage may take years to appear and may never be traceable to
> today, so nothing about handling the product will feel dangerous at
> the time. Several common organic solvents carry it. That is precisely
> why it is printed on the front of the container: your senses are not
> going to warn you.

## Two kinds of label, and the word between them

A **supplier label** — printed by whoever manufactured or imported the
product — carries the product identifier, the supplier's name and
contact information, the pictograms, a **signal word**, hazard
statements saying what the product does, and precautionary statements
saying what to do about it.

The signal word is doing real work and is easy to skim past. **Danger**
marks the more severe hazards; **Warning** marks the less severe. Two
bottles carrying the same pictogram and different signal words are not
equally dangerous, and that word is the only place the difference
appears on the front of the container.

When a chemical is decanted into a smaller container for a class, it
needs a **workplace label**: the product name exactly as it appears on
the safety data sheet, the information you need to handle it safely, and
a pointer to that sheet. Anything else is an unlabelled container.

An unlabelled beaker is not a puzzle to solve by smelling it or testing
it. It comes to me, and it gets disposed of. This has never once been
the wrong call.

## The safety data sheet

Every hazardous product has an SDS, written in a standard order of
**sixteen sections**, so the same information sits in the same place on
any product from any supplier. You will never read all sixteen. These
are the ones you will actually open:

| Section | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| 2 — Hazard identification | What can this do to me? |
| 4 — First-aid measures | What do we do in the first two minutes? |
| 6 — Accidental release measures | It is on the bench. Now what? |
| 7 — Handling and storage | How is it kept, and what must it not sit beside? |
| 8 — Exposure controls and personal protection | What do I need to wear, and does this need a fume hood? |
| 9 — Physical and chemical properties | At what temperature does this become a vapour problem? |
| 10 — Stability and reactivity | What must it never be mixed with? |
| 13 — Disposal considerations | Where does what is left over actually go? |

Sections 9 and 13 are the Grade 12 additions, and section 9 is the one
that will change how you work. It carries the **flash point** — the
temperature at which a liquid gives off enough vapour to be ignitable —
and for several solvents in this course that temperature is below the
temperature of this room. A liquid you are treating as inert because it
is sitting still in a beaker is, on paper, already producing a burnable
atmosphere above itself.

Section 10 lists **incompatible materials**, which is why acids and
bases are not stored on the same shelf, why oxidising agents are kept
away from anything flammable, and why the answer to "can we put these
two in the same waste beaker" is always *check first*.

Sheets for everything in this room are kept where I will show you on the
first lab day. Read section 4 **before** the investigation, not during
the accident.

## The practices that prevent most of it

- **Eye protection goes on when you enter and comes off when the last
  person has cleaned up** — not when your own work is done. Somebody
  else's splash is still your eyes. Tell me if you wear contact lenses,
  so we can make sure your eye protection is right for them.
- **Long hair tied back, loose sleeves secured, closed-toe shoes.**
  Spills and broken glass go down.
- **Never pipette by mouth.** Ever, with anything, including water. Use
  a bulb or a pump — [[Measuring Well]] shows how.
- **Add acid to water, never water to acid.** Diluting a concentrated
  acid releases a great deal of heat. Pour acid slowly into a larger
  volume of water and the water carries that heat away. Pour water onto
  concentrated acid and the heat is dumped into the small amount of
  water sitting on the surface, which can boil instantly and throw acid
  out of the container.
- **Hot glassware looks exactly like cold glassware.** No colour change,
  no glow, no warning. Assume anything near a heat source is hot, and
  use tongs or a clamp.
- **Never heat a sealed container, and never seal a reaction that makes
  a gas.** A gas that cannot expand raises the pressure until something
  gives, and what gives is glass.
- **Point the open end of a heated tube away** from yourself and from
  everyone else, and heat it along its length rather than at one spot.
- **Waft**, do not sniff. Cup a hand over the container and draw the air
  toward your face.
- **Never return unused chemical to the stock bottle.** Take what you
  need; the excess is disposed of as directed. One contaminated stock
  bottle spoils every class after yours.
- **Know the disposal route before you start.** It is not your judgement
  call — it is written in the investigation, or I tell you.
- **Read the label twice** — reaching for it, and again before pouring.
  The two bottles that look alike are always beside each other.
- **Nothing enters your mouth.** No eating, no drinking, no gum, no
  tasting, and wash your hands before you leave whether or not you think
  you touched anything.

## The three hazards this course adds

None of these appeared in Grade 11 chemistry. All three appear in this
one, and each behaves in a way that ordinary caution does not cover.

### Flammable organic solvents: the vapour is the hazard

The liquid does not burn. **The vapour burns**, and the liquid's job is
to keep producing it. That single sentence explains every rule that
follows.

- **No open flame, anywhere in the room**, while a solvent bottle is
  open. Not at your bench, not at the far bench. The vapour of many
  common solvents is denser than air, so it flows off the bench, along
  the floor, and reaches an ignition source you were nowhere near.
- **No hot plate with an exposed element, and never a direct flame.**
  Heat with a water bath. If the procedure needs more than that, it
  needs equipment I will set up.
- **Work in the fume hood**, with the sash at the marked height, and
  check that it is actually running before you open anything.
- **Keep the container closed** except while you are pouring, and keep
  the volume small. An open beaker of solvent left on a bench is
  evaporating the whole time you are doing something else.
- **Section 9 of the SDS tells you how bad this is** for the particular
  solvent in your hand. Look it up once and you will never treat these
  bottles casually again.

### Sodium hydroxide: it hurts less and does more

Concentrated bases behave differently from acids on skin, and the
difference works against you.

A dilute acid splash announces itself — it stings immediately, so you
go to the tap immediately. A sodium hydroxide solution often feels like
almost nothing at first, and then **feels slippery**. That slipperiness
is not a sign that the solution is mild. It is your skin's own fats
being turned into soap, which is the reaction happening, in real time,
on you. Alkali damage penetrates more deeply than an equivalent acid
splash and it keeps going while it does not hurt.

So: **wash for a full fifteen minutes**, longer than feels necessary,
and tell me even if it seems fine. Two more points on the same reagent —
dissolving sodium hydroxide in water releases a lot of heat, so it is
made up slowly, in water, never in a stoppered vessel; and the solid
pellets are handled with a spatula, never fingers.

### Electrolysis: you are manufacturing hydrogen

An electrolytic cell is not a passive piece of glassware. It is a device
that makes gas continuously for as long as it is connected — hydrogen
where water is reduced, oxygen where water is oxidised, and with some
chloride solutions, chlorine instead.

- **No flames in the room while a cell is running.** Hydrogen and oxygen
  produced side by side, in the proportions electrolysis produces them,
  is the most easily ignited mixture you will ever have on a bench.
- **The cell is never sealed.** Gas that cannot leave is pressure.
- **Ventilate, and stop if you smell anything sharp.** Chlorine is not a
  smell to investigate. Step back, tell me, and we deal with it.
- **Disconnect the supply before touching or moving anything**, and use
  only the low-voltage DC supply provided.

> [!danger] Two things that are demonstrations, not activities
> **Combustion calorimetry** is run by me, at the front, unless a
> procedure has been explicitly designed for student hands and says so
> in writing. Burning a measured sample in a sealed or semi-sealed
> vessel combines flammable material, heat, and pressure, and there is
> no version of it that is improved by more people participating.
>
> **Concentrated acids and bases** stay where they are. Every solution
> you work with is at the dilute concentration printed in the
> investigation. If a procedure genuinely needs something stronger, I
> handle it and hand you the diluted result.

## Concentration is part of the hazard

Grade 10 could treat a substance as safe or unsafe. Chemistry cannot,
because the same substance is a different problem at a different
concentration, and this course works with solutions constantly.

**Everything in this room is a dilute school reagent** unless I hand it
to you and say otherwise. That is deliberate, and it is what makes the
procedures here reasonable. It also means two things you must not do:

- Do not scale a procedure up because it seems to be working. Volume,
  concentration, and rate of addition are all safety variables, and a
  reaction that is gentle at 10 mL is not necessarily gentle at 100.
- Do not assume the household version of a substance behaves like ours.
  The bottle under a sink at home is frequently far more concentrated
  than anything here, and **household products are never mixed
  together** — some combinations release toxic gases, and there is no
  version of that experiment that belongs anywhere.

## Waste is a Grade 12 problem too

Unit 5 puts dissolved metal ions in your beakers — copper, zinc, silver,
lead in some procedures. **None of it goes down the drain**, ever, in
any quantity, no matter how dilute it looks. Metal ion solutions go into
the labelled container, and so do the used electrodes and the salt
bridges.

Organic waste from Unit 2 is separated as well, and for a different
reason: solvents that reach a drain are both a fire risk in the plumbing
and a treatment problem downstream. Two waste streams, two containers,
and the investigation tells you which is which before you start.

## Before you start, know where these are

- [ ] Eyewash station — and that it runs for a full fifteen minutes
- [ ] Safety shower, and how to pull it
- [ ] Fire extinguisher and fire blanket
- [ ] First-aid kit and the spill kit
- [ ] The waste containers, and which is which
- [ ] The fume hood, and how to tell it is running
- [ ] The nearest exit, and the one you would use if that exit is the
      problem

> [!danger] Report everything, immediately
> Every spill, break, cut, burn, splash, and "I think some got on my
> hand". A small event handled in the first thirty seconds is nothing.
> The same event hidden for ten minutes becomes an incident report and
> sometimes an injury. **You will never be in trouble for reporting an
> accident** — the only thing that ends lab privileges here is not
> reporting one.
