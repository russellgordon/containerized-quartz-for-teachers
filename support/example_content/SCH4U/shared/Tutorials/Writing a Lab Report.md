Every formal report in this course uses the same nine sections. Eight of
them you already know from Grade 11. The ninth is new, it goes near the
top, and it is the section that makes this a Grade 12 report rather than
a longer Grade 11 one.

Some of the examples below come from investigations you have not run
yet. They are here because this is the page you will come back to all
semester, and it is more useful complete than it is convenient.

## The sections

| Section | What belongs there | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question | What you set out to find out, in one sentence | Restating the title |
| **Model** | The model you are reasoning from, what it assumes about your set-up, and what would count as it failing | Naming it and stopping |
| Prediction | What you expect, **derived from that model**, with a direction and a number where you can | "I think it will work" |
| Variables | Independent, dependent, and how each controlled one was held | Listing two and forgetting the third |
| Materials and procedure | Numbered steps, with quantities, concentrations, and the instruments named | Written as a story afterwards |
| Observations | Raw readings, tables, qualitative notes — no interpretation | Recording only the calculated answer |
| Analysis | One sample calculation in full, then the rest | Repeating the table in sentences |
| Conclusion | The answer, the evidence for it, and whether the model held | Claiming more than the data supports |
| Limitations | What the method could not settle, and **which way** each one pushed | A list of things you feel bad about |

## The model section, which is new

Last year a prediction needed a mechanism. This year the mechanism has
to be named as a model and bounded, in advance, in its own short
paragraph — three or four sentences is plenty.

Three questions, answered in order:

1. **Which model am I using?** By name.
2. **What does it assume about *this* set-up?** Not in general — here,
   with these solutions, at this temperature, in this glassware.
3. **What result would tell me the model does not apply?** Written
   before you measure anything.

That third question is the one that earns the marks, because it is the
only way a report can distinguish "my technique was poor" from "the
model does not describe what I built" when the numbers come out wrong.
Without it, every disagreement collapses into the same shrug.

| Model | What it assumes | Where it stops |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collision theory | Particles react when they meet with enough energy and the right orientation | Says nothing about the individual steps of a mechanism, and treats every collision as independent |
| VSEPR | Electron groups arrange themselves as far apart as possible | Predicts shape, not bond length or strength; strains at heavier elements and transition metals |
| Le Châtelier's principle | A disturbed system moves to partly oppose the change | Gives a **direction only**, never a magnitude, and misleads when one change alters two things at once |
| The coffee-cup calorimeter | No heat escapes; the solution behaves like water | Both assumptions fail measurably, and always in the same direction |
| Standard reduction potentials | 1 mol/L, 25 °C, 100 kPa, no current drawn | Your cell meets none of these exactly |
| The weak-acid approximation | Ionisation is small enough to ignore in the denominator | Fails in dilute solutions and for stronger weak acids; the usual cut-off is 5% |

> [!important] Naming the boundary is not hedging
> Students worry that saying where a model stops will look like a lack
> of confidence. It reads as the opposite. A report that states the
> boundary and then shows the data sitting comfortably inside it is
> making a *stronger* claim than one that never asked — because it has
> ruled something out. See [[How Marks Work]].

## Predictions need a mechanism, a direction, and now a boundary

A prediction without a "because" is a guess with a confident tone.

> ❌ The temperature will go up.
>
> ✅ The temperature will rise by roughly 6 °C, **because** the
> neutralisation of a strong acid by a strong base is exothermic and
> 0.0500 mol releases enough energy to warm 100 g of solution by about
> that much. **If it rises by very much less**, the likeliest reasons
> are heat escaping the cup or the reaction not going to completion, and
> those two are distinguishable by whether the loss depends on time.

The second version can be wrong in a *useful* way. Where you can, put a
number on it — "somewhere in the fifties of kJ/mol" is a real prediction
and it is checkable. "It will be lower" is a direction, which is the
minimum. "It might be different" is not a prediction at all.

> [!note] Write the prediction before you measure
> Not after, and not "roughly what I was expecting". Record it, with the
> model and the boundary, in your [[Chemistry Journal]] before the first
> reading. That record is the only way to find out whether your
> reasoning is improving, which is the actual skill being taught.

## Procedure: written so it can be repeated

Past tense, numbered, with quantities, concentrations, temperatures, and
the instruments named specifically. "25.0 mL of 0.100 mol/L sodium
hydroxide solution was delivered from a burette, with the solution at
21.4 °C" — not "some base was added". A beaker, a graduated cylinder,
and a pipet are three different claims about how well you know that
volume; see [[Measuring Well]].

Temperature is not optional in this course. Every equilibrium constant,
every $K_a$, and every standard potential you compare against was
measured at a stated temperature, and a procedure that never says what
yours was cannot be compared with any of them.

The test is not whether it reads well. It is whether another group could
follow it and get your result without asking you a single question.

## Observations, and the sample calculation

Observations hold the numbers the instruments gave you, before any
arithmetic, with units, in a table. State each instrument's resolution
once, at the top: *thermometer to 0.1 °C; burette read to two decimal
places, good to about ±0.02 mL per reading.* That single line does a
great deal of work later.

Analysis holds **one calculation written out in full**, with units
carried through every line, followed by a table of the results of
repeating it. Nobody wants to read the same arithmetic five times, and
nobody can check any of it if you show none.

$$\begin{aligned} q &= mc\Delta T = (100.0\ \text{g})(4.18\ \text{J/g}^\circ\text{C})(6.4\ ^\circ\text{C}) = 2.68 \times 10^3\ \text{J} \\ \Delta H &= -\frac{q}{n} = -\frac{2.68 \times 10^3\ \text{J}}{0.0500\ \text{mol}} = -5.4 \times 10^4\ \text{J/mol} = -54\ \text{kJ/mol} \end{aligned}$$

Two significant figures, because $\Delta T$ had two — and $\Delta T$ is
almost always the weakest number in a calorimetry experiment, which is
worth saying out loud in the analysis rather than leaving the reader to
work out. Carry the unrounded value from line one into line two and
round once, at the end.

Where a result is a logarithm, the rules change and they change in a way
that is easy to get wrong. [[Working with Logarithms in Chemistry]] has
the whole of it; the short version is that the decimal places in a pH,
not the total digits, carry the significant figures.

## Conclusions: answer, evidence, model

Three moves, in order, and the third is the Grade 12 one.

> The neutralisation released 54 kJ/mol, against the value the data
> booklet gives for a strong acid with a strong base at 25 °C — roughly
> 57 kJ/mol. Our figure is about 5% below it.
>
> The thermometer resolves 0.1 °C, so a rise of 6.4 °C carries roughly
> ±3%, and the two other groups obtained 53 and 56 kJ/mol. Our result
> and theirs agree within that, so there is one measurement here made
> three times rather than three results.
>
> The gap against the booklet is a different matter, because it is in
> the same direction for all three groups. That is what the calorimeter
> model predicts: it assumes no heat escapes, heat plainly does escape,
> and every joule that leaves makes the measured value smaller in
> magnitude. So our number is best read as a **lower bound** on how
> exothermic this reaction is, and the model held — its stated
> assumption failed in the direction it was always going to fail in.

That third paragraph is where the marks are. It does not say the numbers
"were close". It says how close they had to be before closeness meant
anything, then checks, then explains the part that did not agree using
the model's own assumptions rather than an apology.

## Limitations: which way did each one push?

Naming a limitation is Grade 10. Saying what it **did to your number**
is Grade 11. Saying which of the model's assumptions it broke is this
course.

| Limitation | Direction it pushes | So your result is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat escaping the calorimeter | Measured $\Delta T$ down | A floor — the reaction is more exothermic than this |
| Energy absorbed by the cup and thermometer | Measured $\Delta T$ down | Same direction, so the two do not cancel |
| Sampling before the system had stopped changing | Product concentrations down | $K_c$ calculated too small, if you started from reactants |
| Overshooting the endpoint of a titration | Titrant volume up | Concentration of the unknown calculated too high |
| A burette rinsed with water, not with titrant | Titrant diluted, so more is needed | Concentration of the unknown calculated too high |
| An oxidised electrode surface | Measured cell potential down | Below $E^\circ$, and not for the interesting reason |
| Solutions well away from 1 mol/L | Cell potential shifted | Off $E^\circ$ in a direction you can predict and should |

Every row has a direction. That is not a coincidence — a limitation you
cannot assign a direction to is usually one you have not thought about
yet, and working out the direction is often what tells you how to fix
it.

> [!warning] A limitation is not a blunder, and they get different treatment
> A **limitation** would still be there if you ran the method perfectly:
> the resolution of the thermometer, heat you cannot keep in a cup, a
> concentration you had no way to hold at exactly 1 mol/L. It belongs in
> this section, and it earns marks.
>
> A **blunder** is a mistake: wrong solution, misread 2.35 as 2.53,
> knocked the flask. It belongs in your notes and in a repeated trial,
> not here. Listing "human error" as a limitation is claiming credit for
> the wrong thing, and it is the single most common sentence I strike
> out. [[Mistakes Are Data]] sorts one from the other.

## Before you submit

- [ ] Model named, its assumptions about *this* set-up stated, and a
      failure condition written before measuring
- [ ] Prediction derived from that model, with a direction and a number
      where possible
- [ ] All three kinds of variable named, and controls say *how*
- [ ] Instruments named specifically wherever a quantity was measured
- [ ] Temperature recorded, and stated wherever a booklet value is used
- [ ] Every measurement carries a unit and the right number of
      significant figures — logarithms by their own rule
- [ ] Instrument resolutions stated once, near the data
- [ ] One sample calculation in full, with units on every line
- [ ] Conclusion answers the question using your own numbers
- [ ] Any comparison between numbers checked against the uncertainty
- [ ] Every limitation carries a direction, and the ones that matter are
      tied back to an assumption in the model section
- [ ] One specific change you would make, not "be more careful"
