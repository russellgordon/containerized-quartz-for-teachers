Scientific writing is not fancy writing. It is writing that cannot be
misunderstood, by a reader who was not in the room and who is looking
for the weak spot.

Grade 11 made you precise about substances and quantities. This course
makes you precise about **processes** — how far, how fast, in which
direction, driven by what — and about the models you are using to
describe them. Most of the marks lost on this page are lost to sentences
that are almost right, which is a much harder thing to notice than a
sentence that is plainly wrong.

## Say what you measured, not what you felt

> ❌ The reaction went really fast and got super hot.
>
> ✅ The temperature rose from 21.0 °C to 47.5 °C in 30 s.

Adjectives are the reader's job. Give them the number and let them
decide whether it was fast.

## Keep observation and interpretation in separate sentences

An observation is what an instrument or your senses recorded. An
interpretation is what you think it means. Mixing them is how a shaky
inference gets smuggled in as a fact.

> **Observation:** The solution darkened over about 20 s and then stopped
> changing; the absorbance held at 0.412 for the next four minutes.
>
> **Interpretation:** The system had reached equilibrium, since a
> constant absorbance means constant concentration.

Notice what the observation does not say. It does not say "the reaction
finished" — that phrase already contains an interpretation, and in this
course it contains a wrong one.

## Claim what your data supports, and no more

| Too strong | Honest |
| --- | --- |
| This proves the reaction is exothermic. | The temperature of the solution rose by 6.4 °C on mixing, which supports the reaction being exothermic. |
| The equilibrium constant is 48. | Our concentrations give $K_c = 48$; the concentrations carry two significant figures and the expression squares one of them, so this is a two-figure result at best. |
| The reaction stopped. | The concentrations stopped changing, to within what the colorimeter can resolve. |
| The results were wrong. | Our $\Delta H$ was 5% below the booklet value, in the direction heat loss from the calorimeter would push it. |
| The cell produced 1.10 V, so the table is confirmed. | The cell produced 1.04 V against a tabulated 1.10 V; our solutions were not at 1 mol/L, which is a condition the tabulated value assumes. |

> [!important] "Prove" is not a science word
> Evidence *supports*, *is consistent with*, or *fails to support*. One
> lab period proves nothing at all, and a reader who sees "proves" in
> your first paragraph trusts everything after it a little less. This is
> the single easiest mark in the course to keep.

## The words this course uses precisely

Each pair below is used loosely in ordinary speech and precisely here.
Mixing them up costs marks and, worse, produces sentences that are
simply not decodable.

| Pair | The distinction |
| --- | --- |
| **Strong** and **concentrated** | Strong is how completely a substance ionises in water. Concentrated is how much of it there is per litre. A dilute solution of a strong acid is perfectly ordinary, and so is a concentrated solution of a weak one. Still the most commonly swapped pair in the course |
| **Favourable** and **fast** | A reaction can release a great deal of energy and take years. Direction and rate are settled by different things — [[Enthalpy]] and [[Collision Theory and Catalysts]] answer different questions |
| **Stable** and **unreactive** | A substance that sits unchanged may be at a low energy, or it may simply have no fast route anywhere. Which one you mean changes the whole explanation |
| **Complete** and **at equilibrium** | A reaction at equilibrium has not finished; it has stopped changing. Both directions are still running |
| **Shifts** and **changes** | The *position* of equilibrium shifts when you disturb a system. The equilibrium *constant* changes only when the temperature changes. Saying "$K$ shifted right" is two errors in three words |
| **Rate** and **rate constant** | Changing a concentration changes the rate. It does not change $k$. Temperature and a catalyst change $k$ |
| **Oxidised** and **oxidising agent** | The species oxidised is the one that lost electrons. The oxidising agent is the other one — the species being reduced. They are never the same substance |
| **Amount** and **mass** | *Amount* means a number of moles. A mass is in grams. "A large amount" and "a large mass" are different claims |

Two more that are worth a sentence each rather than a table row.

**"Neutralisation gives a neutral solution" is false**, and it is false
in a way that Unit 4 is built on. A strong acid with a strong base does
give a neutral solution. A weak acid with a strong base gives a solution
whose pH sits above 7 at the equivalence point, because the anion left
behind reacts with water. That is why an indicator has to be chosen
rather than assumed — see [[Buffers and Titration Curves]].

**Molecules do not want anything.** "The reaction wants to relieve the
stress", "the system tries to get back to equilibrium", "electrons want
a full shell" — these are all shorthand, and they are all fine as
thinking aids and unacceptable in a conclusion. The rates change; the
concentrations respond; nothing intends anything. If you find a verb of
desire in a sentence, replace it with what actually changed.

## Sentences that give away a misunderstanding

These come up every year and they are worth recognising in your own
drafts.

| Sentence | What is wrong with it |
| --- | --- |
| "The catalyst increased the yield." | A catalyst changes the rate of both directions equally. It gets you to the same place sooner |
| "$K$ is large, so the reaction is fast." | $K$ says where the system ends up, not how long it takes to get there |
| "Le Châtelier's principle caused the shift." | A principle summarises what happens; it does not cause anything. The rates changed |
| "Electrons flow through the salt bridge." | Electrons go through the external wire. Ions move through the bridge to keep both solutions electrically neutral |
| "The anode is the negative electrode." | True for a galvanic cell and false for an electrolytic one. What is always true is that oxidation happens at the anode |
| "Adding a catalyst lowers the activation energy of the reaction." | It provides a different pathway with a lower activation energy. The original pathway is still there |
| "The acid was neutralised, so the pH is 7." | Only for a strong acid with a strong base |

## Write chemistry so it can be read

Formulas need their subscripts, charges, and states to mean anything.
$\ce{H2O}$ is water; H2O is a typing accident that happens to
be readable. Ions carry their charge properly: sulfate is
$\ce{SO4^2-}$, not SO4-2 — the charge sits after the subscript, and
the number comes before the sign.

Four conventions that carry real meaning in this course:

- **The arrow states a claim.** A single arrow, written `->`, says
  the reaction proceeds to completion. A double arrow, written
  `<=>`, says both directions are running at once. In
  Units 4 and 5 that is the difference between two different chemical
  claims, not a stylistic choice:

$$\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) <=> CH3COO-(aq) + H3O+(aq)}$$

- **States are not optional.** `(aq)`, `(l)`, `(g)`, and `(s)` decide
  what goes into an equilibrium expression at all — pure solids and
  pure liquids stay out of it, and without the state labels the reader
  cannot check your work.
- **Coefficients go in front, subscripts stay put.**
  $\ce{2H2O}$ is two water molecules;
  $\ce{H4O2}$ is not a substance. Changing a subscript to
  balance an equation changes what reacted.
- **Capitals matter.** Co is cobalt; CO is carbon monoxide.

For organic compounds there is one more. A condensed structural formula
such as $\ce{CH3CH2OH}$ tells a reader which atoms are
attached to which, and a molecular formula such as
$\ce{C2H6O}$ does not — and in a unit whose entire
first lesson is that two substances can share a molecular formula, that
distinction is the content. Name compounds systematically, per
[[Naming Organic Compounds]]; common names are fine in conversation and
ambiguous on paper.

## Units and digits are part of the claim

A number without a unit is not an answer. 4.2 what — grams, moles,
volts, kJ per mole? And a unit without a number is a category, not a
measurement.

The digits matter in the same way. Writing 0.0787132 mol/L when your
measurement supports two significant figures is a claim about your
equipment, and it is a false one. Two Grade 12 traps in particular:

- **A logarithm counts its digits differently.** A pH of 2.47 is a
  two-figure result, because the digits before the decimal point came
  from the exponent. [[Working with Logarithms in Chemistry]] has the
  rule and the reasoning.
- **Sign conventions are part of the number.** $\Delta H = -890$ kJ/mol
  means the reaction releases 890 kJ per mole. Writing "the reaction
  releases $-890$ kJ" says the opposite of what you mean, and it is a
  surprisingly common slip.

More on the general case: [[Significant Figures and Units]].

## Passive voice is fine here

"The solution was heated to 40 °C" keeps the reader's attention on the
procedure rather than on who was holding the beaker. Method sections are
one of the few kinds of writing where that is the right choice, so
ignore anybody who tells you to purge it.

Conclusions are different. "We concluded that the model held within our
uncertainty" is clearer than "it was concluded that…", because in a
conclusion it matters that a person made a judgement, and hiding the
person hides the judgement — which is precisely the part being assessed.
