## In order of speed

1. **Ask in class, at the time.** If you are confused, so are three
   other people who have not said anything yet.
2. **Check the concept page.** Most "I don't get it" moments are
   answered there, and the backlinks show you which class we did it in.
3. **Work through [[Getting Unstuck]].** It is a twenty-minute routine
   built around one question: is this a chemistry problem, a model
   problem, or an arithmetic problem? Sorting that out solves a
   surprising share of what looks unresolvable.
4. **Come to a help session.** Drop in; no appointment, no warning
   needed. The times are below.
5. **Email me.** Say what you tried. It makes the answer far more useful
   and it usually saves an entire round trip.

## Bring the specific thing, and bring the numbers

One calculation that comes out wrong beats "equilibrium". One step of a
titration curve where you lose the thread beats "acids and bases". The
narrower the question, the faster and better the answer — and narrowing
it yourself often solves it before you get to me.

Bring the working, not a summary of the working. "I get pH 2.87 and the
key says 3.37" tells me in one glance that a square root or a factor of
ten has gone astray, which is a thirty-second conversation. "I did it
wrong" is a ten-minute one.

## Help with a result, not just a method

The most useful conversation of the week is usually somebody arriving
with data that did not do what they expected — an equilibrium constant
well below the booklet value, a cell potential that came out negative,
three trials that agree beautifully with each other and not with anybody
else. That is not a failure to be quietly fixed before anyone sees it.
It is the interesting case, and it is exactly what
[[Mistakes Are Data]] is about.

Bring the notebook with the raw readings in it, and the temperature. A
calculated answer on its own can only be checked for arithmetic; the
readings can be checked for chemistry.

> [!note] Ask early
> Chemistry builds, and this course builds harder than last year's did.
> A shaky grip on intermolecular forces in Unit 1 is awkward. The same
> gap arriving in Unit 4, where you need structure, energy, and rate all
> at once to make sense of equilibrium, is four weeks wide instead of
> one. Nobody has ever irritated me by asking a question too early.
>
> One specific thing worth raising the first week you notice it: if the
> **arithmetic** is what is in your way — logarithms, exponents, solving
> for $x$ — say so plainly. We will fix the arithmetic, which is quick,
> rather than re-explaining chemistry you already understand, which is
> not.
