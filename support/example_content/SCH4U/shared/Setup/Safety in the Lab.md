Before your first investigation, you and a parent or guardian both sign
this. No signature, no lab work — there is no discretion in that, for
anybody.

You signed something very like it last year. This one is longer, and the
extra items are not padding: this course puts flammable organic solvents,
concentrated bases, and gas-producing electrolytic cells on the same
benches you used in Grade 11. The reference behind it, including how to
read a label and a safety data sheet, is [[Lab Safety and WHMIS]].

## I agree to

- [ ] Wear eye protection from the moment lab work starts until the last
      person has finished cleaning up
- [ ] Tie back long hair, secure loose sleeves, and wear closed-toe
      shoes on lab days
- [ ] Read the whole investigation before touching any equipment
- [ ] Follow the procedure as written, and ask before changing anything
- [ ] Never eat, drink, chew, or taste anything in the lab, and wash my
      hands before leaving
- [ ] Never pipette by mouth, with any liquid, including water
- [ ] Waft odours toward me rather than inhaling directly
- [ ] Treat all glassware near a heat source as hot, because hot
      glassware looks exactly like cold glassware
- [ ] Point the open end of a heated tube away from everyone, including
      me
- [ ] Never heat a sealed container, and never seal a reaction that
      produces a gas
- [ ] Add acid to water and never water to acid
- [ ] Never return unused chemical to the stock bottle
- [ ] Check where the waste from my procedure goes **before** I start,
      and put nothing down the drain unless I have been told to
- [ ] Know where the eyewash, safety shower, fire extinguisher, fire
      blanket, spill kit, and exits are **before** I need them
- [ ] Report every spill, break, cut, burn, and splash immediately,
      however small
- [ ] Leave my station cleaner than I found it
- [ ] Never remove chemicals or equipment from the room

## The four Grade 12 additions

Each of these covers something that was simply not present in last
year's room.

- [ ] **Flammable solvents:** I will keep organic solvents away from
      every flame and every hot plate, heat them only in a water bath,
      use them in the fume hood, and keep the container closed when I am
      not pouring — because it is the vapour that burns, and the vapour
      travels
- [ ] **Corrosive bases:** I will treat sodium hydroxide solutions with
      the same seriousness as acid, wash any splash for a full fifteen
      minutes, and report it even if it does not hurt — because alkali
      damage goes deeper and announces itself later
- [ ] **Electrolysis:** I will never seal a cell, never allow a flame in
      the room while a cell is running, disconnect the supply before
      touching anything, and stop and tell the teacher if I smell
      anything sharp
- [ ] **Metal ion waste:** I will put every solution containing
      dissolved metal ions, and every used electrode and salt bridge,
      into the labelled container and never down the drain

## The two that carry over from Grade 11

You designed procedures last year and you will design more of them this
year, on chemistry with less margin in it.

- [ ] Before running any procedure I designed, I will have it checked by
      the teacher — including what could go wrong, what I would do if it
      did, and where the waste goes
- [ ] I will not change the amount, the concentration, or the rate of
      addition in a procedure without asking first

That check is not a formality and it is not a judgement of your plan.
Working scientists have their procedures reviewed by somebody else, for
exactly the reason you need it: the person who wrote the plan is the
person least able to see what it assumes.

The second item matters more this year. Scale is a safety variable in
its own right, and it is one you now have the chemistry to understand: a
reaction produces heat in proportion to how much material is reacting,
and it sheds heat in proportion to its surface. Ten times the volume is
nowhere near ten times the cooling. "It worked fine when we did less of
it" is not a reason to do more of it — it is a description of the last
safe version.

## Things that are demonstrations here

Some procedures are run by me, at the front, and there is no version of
the lesson where that changes:

- Anything involving **combustion inside a calorimeter**
- Anything using a **concentrated** acid or base rather than the dilute
  solutions the investigations specify
- Any electrolysis where **chlorine** is a plausible product

You will still get the data and still do the whole analysis. The part
you are giving up is standing next to it.

## Conduct that ends lab privileges

Horseplay, ignoring an instruction, working with equipment before the
check, or interfering with somebody else's safety. You will complete the
work as a written assignment instead, and we will talk with home. This
is not about being strict — a lab only works when everyone in the room
can rely on everyone else.

> [!danger] The one that matters most
> **Tell me the moment something goes wrong.** A small spill handled in
> thirty seconds is nothing at all. The same spill hidden for ten
> minutes is an incident, and occasionally an injury. You will never be
> in trouble for reporting an accident — not once, not ever. The only
> thing that gets you in trouble here is not reporting one.
>
> Sodium hydroxide is the reason this callout is at the bottom of a
> Grade 12 page rather than a Grade 11 one. It is the splash that people
> do not report, because at the two-minute mark it genuinely does not
> feel like anything. Report it anyway.
