## About the calculator

Get one you can use without thinking, and use the same one all
semester. Last year's advice was about not losing an exponent. This year
there are four keys you must be able to find without looking, and two of
them did not come up at all in Grade 11.

| Key | What you will use it for |
| --- | --- |
| `log` | Turning a concentration into a pH |
| $10^x$ | Turning a pH back into a concentration — usually the second function of the `log` key |
| `EE` or `EXP` | Entering $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ without typing it as a multiplication |
| $x^2$ and $\sqrt{\ }$ | Equilibrium calculations, where $x$ is very often a square root |

Two checks, in the first week, that take ten seconds and prevent a
fortnight of confusion:

- `log` of 100 must give exactly **2**. If it gives 4.605, the key you
  pressed was `ln`, and every pH you calculate with it will be 2.303
  times too large.
- $10^x$ of 2 must give exactly **100**. If it gives 7.389, you have
  $e^x$.

Then find out how many digits your calculator shows before it switches
to scientific notation, because a value like $1.0 \times 10^{-14}$
displayed as a long string of zeros is very easy to miscount. The whole
of [[Working with Logarithms in Chemistry]] assumes you have made
friends with this device.

## Lab days

Check the class page the night before. If it links an investigation, it
is a lab day, and the following are not negotiable:

- [ ] Closed-toe shoes. You will be sent out, and there is nothing I can
      do about it
- [ ] Long hair tied back before you come in, not at the bench
- [ ] Sleeves that can be rolled or fastened; nothing dangling from
      wrists or neck
- [ ] Bags at the front of the room, not underfoot
- [ ] The investigation page read, not skimmed — including where the
      waste goes, which this year is often not the drain

> [!note] Why the shoe rule has no exceptions
> Everything that goes wrong in a lab goes down. Broken glass, hot
> liquid, corrosive solution, and spilled solvent all end up on the
> floor, and the floor is where your feet are. This is a school board
> requirement and not a preference of mine, so please do not spend a lab
> period negotiating it with me.

One addition for the organic unit. On days when solvents are in use,
clothing that covers your arms is a good idea rather than a rule, and
loose synthetic sleeves are the worst possible choice near any heat
source. [[Lab Safety and WHMIS]] explains why the vapour rather than the
liquid is what you are guarding against.

## About the notebook

Bound, in ink, in order, with nothing torn out. That combination is what
turns a pile of notes into a **record**: readings written as they happen
rather than reconstructed afterwards, mistakes struck through with a
single line and initialled rather than scribbled out, and the odd
observation kept even when you cannot explain it.

Three habits specific to this course:

1. **Write the raw reading** as the instrument gave it, before any
   arithmetic. A calculated answer with no reading behind it cannot be
   rechecked.
2. **Write the resolution of each instrument once**, at the top of the
   page, so that three weeks later you can still say what your numbers
   were worth.
3. **Write the temperature.** Every constant you will compare against —
   $K_c$, $K_a$, $K_{sp}$, $E^\circ$ — was measured at a stated
   temperature, and a reading with no temperature beside it cannot be
   compared with any of them. This is the habit Grade 11 did not need
   and Unit 4 will not forgive.

[[Measuring Well]] explains why all three pay off, and
[[Reading a Data Table]] explains what the third one is protecting you
from.
