You built a cell, measured its potential, and watched one electrode lose
mass while the other gained it. That is the whole of this discussion,
run at two scales: the one we want, packaged and sold, and the one we do
not, eating a pipe under a street.

Same reaction, same bookkeeping, opposite intentions.

## The question

Two halves, and we will take them in order.

First: where is an electrochemical cell genuinely the right answer to an
energy problem, and where is it being asked to solve a problem it cannot?
Second: what does electrochemistry do to us when nobody is trying to get
work out of it — in plumbing, in vehicles, in the infrastructure that is
already in the ground?

## A battery is a container, not a source

This distinction decides most of the argument, so make it early.

A **battery** stores energy that was put into it somewhere else. Charging
one is an electrolytic process; discharging it is a galvanic one, and the
electricity that charged it had to be generated by something. Calling a
battery an energy source is like calling a bucket a spring.

A **fuel cell** is different in kind. It converts continuously for as
long as fuel is supplied, and the hydrogen–oxygen cell produces water:

$$\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)}$$

That equation is the reason fuel cells attract the word "clean", and it
is a half-truth until you say where the hydrogen came from. Hydrogen is
not mined. It is made — most often from methane, which releases carbon
dioxide in the making, or by electrolysis, which needs electricity that
came from somewhere itself. The cell is clean at the point of use. The
system is only as clean as its supply.

| Technology | What it actually is | What it needs | What it leaves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary cell | A store, used once | Manufacture, then disposal | A cell that cannot be recharged |
| Secondary cell | A store, refilled electrically | Electricity, and a supply of metals | A pack to be collected and processed |
| Fuel cell | A converter | A continuous fuel supply | Water, and whatever making the fuel produced |
| Electrolytic cell | A consumer of energy, used to drive a reaction uphill | Electricity, continuously | The product you wanted, plus gases at the electrodes |

The strongest case for cells is usually not "cleaner". It is
**location**: a remote site, an emergency supply, a sensor on a pipeline,
a place where running a line is impossible or absurd. Where the argument
is about siting rather than about emissions, it is much harder to
dismiss — and that is the case the curriculum asks you to assess.

## What the cell costs before it ever runs

Be even-handed here, in both directions. This is the part where
arguments in this room go boosterish or go alarmist, and both are lazy.

**Getting the metals out.** Lithium reaches a cell by one of two routes.
Brine is pumped to the surface and concentrated by evaporation, which
uses land and water, often in dry regions where water is already
contested. Hard rock is mined and processed, which takes considerable
energy and leaves tailings. Neither route is free, and the honest
comparison is against the extraction that the alternative would have
required, not against nothing. Cobalt and nickel bring their own supply
questions, including working conditions in some producing regions —
research what is documented rather than repeating what you have heard.

**Getting them back.** Recycling a battery is harder than recycling a
bottle and the reasons are chemical and mechanical rather than
attitudinal. Packs are welded and bonded so they will survive vibration
for a decade. Cell chemistry differs between manufacturers and changes
between model years, so a recycler cannot assume what is inside. And
recovery has historically been driven by the metals worth the most,
which has meant cobalt and nickel ahead of lithium. Recycling capacity
is being built, and treating it as already solved is as wrong as
treating it as impossible.

> [!danger] Cells are safe in service and dangerous in a bin
> A lithium cell that is crushed, punctured, or shorted can heat itself
> faster than it can shed heat, and once that starts it does not need
> any air from outside to keep going. This is why a cell must never go
> into household waste or ordinary recycling: crushed in a truck or a
> sorting line, it starts fires, and those fires are a documented and
> recurring problem for waste facilities.
>
> Lead–acid batteries fail differently. Charging one produces hydrogen
> at the negative plate — the electrolysis of water, the same reaction
> you will run deliberately in [[Corrosion and Electrolysis]] — so a
> battery on charge sits in an atmosphere that will ignite. No flames,
> no sparks, no sealing the space.
>
> In this room, the rule is short: cells go into the container I show
> you, and nothing gets charged, shorted, or opened at a bench.

## Corrosion is the same chemistry, running uninvited

Rusting is a galvanic process happening on a single piece of metal.
Iron is oxidised at one patch, oxygen is reduced at another, and the
metal itself carries the electrons between them. That is why both water
**and** oxygen are required, and why road salt accelerates the whole
business — dissolved ions make the water a better conductor, so the
circuit closes more easily.

Put two different metals in contact with an electrolyte between them and
you have built a cell whether or not you meant to. The metal more easily
oxidised becomes the anode and corrodes preferentially, which is exactly
what [[Redox Bookkeeping]] predicts and exactly what a plumber means by
not joining copper directly to galvanized steel.

That prediction is also the cure. A **sacrificial anode** — a block of
magnesium or zinc bolted to a ship's hull, buried beside a pipeline, or
screwed into the top of a domestic water heater — is deliberately chosen
to be the metal that corrodes, so the steel does not. It is consumed,
and it has to be replaced, and a water heater that failed early has
often simply had its anode used up years earlier.

## Drinking water, which is the health issue the curriculum names

This is the case to take most seriously, because it is where
electrochemistry becomes a public health question rather than a
maintenance one.

Metal enters drinking water by corrosion of the pipe carrying it. In
older systems the concern is lead — from lead service lines, and from
the lead-bearing solder used at joints before it was prohibited.
Utilities manage this by controlling the water's chemistry: adjusting pH
and alkalinity, or dosing with orthophosphate, so that a sparingly
soluble scale forms on the inside of the pipe and stands between the
metal and the water. That scale is doing the work.

The uncomfortable consequence, and the reason this belongs in a
discussion rather than a lecture: **a change to the water can disturb a
protective layer that took years to form.** A new source, a different
disinfectant, a shift in pH — each is a chemistry change that the
existing pipe has to re-equilibrate with, and the metal that comes off
during that adjustment goes to the tap. Health Canada publishes a
guideline for lead in drinking water; find the current value yourself
rather than taking one off this page, and notice while you are there
that the guideline has been tightened over time as the evidence
developed.

Related and different: copper plumbing can develop pinhole leaks, and
aluminium, iron, and copper all leach at rates that depend on the
water's pH and its dissolved gases. The general lesson is the one this
whole unit teaches — the pipe and the water are a system, and a system
at equilibrium is not the same thing as a system that is inert.

## What to bring

- [ ] One electrochemical technology, named, with the half-reactions if
      you can find them
- [ ] What it is competing against, and on what basis
- [ ] One extraction or disposal consequence, from a source you can cite
- [ ] One corrosion problem in real infrastructure, and the chemistry of
      why it happens
- [ ] One measure that prevents it, and what that measure costs or
      consumes
- [ ] The strongest objection to your own position

For the material side, **Natural Resources Canada** publishes on
minerals and mining in Canada; the **International Energy Agency**
publishes on batteries and the minerals that go into them; **Health
Canada** and your provincial ministry publish drinking water guidelines
and the reasoning behind them. Bring what a source says and where it
says it, and judge it against [[What Counts as Evidence]] first.
