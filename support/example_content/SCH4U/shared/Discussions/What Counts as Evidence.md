Last year the hard question was whether the gap between two numbers was
bigger than the uncertainty in the measurement. You should still ask it,
and this year you should be able to ask it without being reminded.

Grade 12 puts a second question underneath it. Almost nothing you argue
about this semester is a bare observation. It is an observation read
through a **model** — collision theory, VSEPR, Le Châtelier, a table of
standard reduction potentials compiled under conditions your bench does
not meet. So: *what would this evidence look like if my model were
wrong?* If you cannot answer that, you are not defending a finding. You
are defending a habit.

## The question

Two groups ran the same tests in
[[Identifying an Unknown Organic Compound]] and came away naming
different compounds. Neither made an obvious mistake, and both can point
at the evidence for their answer. What would settle it, and what would
only feel like settling it?

Then the sharper version, which is the one we will spend most of the
period on. A group measures the equilibrium concentrations in a reaction
and calculates $K_c = 48$. The data booklet gives 54 at the same
temperature. Is that a disagreement, or is that agreement?

Before you answer, notice what the question is hiding. For

$$\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}$$

the constant is $K_c = \frac{[\ce{HI}]^2}{[\ce{H2}][\ce{I2}]}$,
and the numerator is **squared**. A 5% error in the measured $[\ce{HI}]$
lands as roughly 10% in $K_c$ before anything else has gone wrong. The
gap between 48 and 54 is about 12%. Now answer.

## Five claims to rank

1. One carefully run trial, by a group whose technique you respect,
   never repeated.
2. A result you reproduced yourself at the bench in twenty minutes.
3. A prediction from a model that has never failed you yet, with no
   measurement behind it today.
4. A value from the course data booklet, measured under standard
   conditions by people none of us will ever meet.
5. A figure from a report published by an organisation with a stake in
   the answer, which cites its own measurements and describes its method.

Rank them from most to least convincing. Then find someone who ranked
them differently and work out **why** — the disagreement is almost
always about what question is being decided, not about the claims.

Claims 3 and 5 are the new ones. Claim 3 is the one people over-rate,
because a model that has worked all semester feels like evidence and is
not; it is a summary of evidence collected somewhere else, under
conditions somebody chose. Claim 5 is the one people dismiss too fast,
and the rest of this page is mostly about it.

## What a source has to survive

The curriculum names five things to test a research source for — logic,
accuracy, reliability, adequacy, and bias. They are not synonyms, and
each fails in its own way.

| Test | The failure it catches | What it sounds like when it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logic | The conclusion does not follow from the data shown | Three graphs, then a claim none of them measured |
| Accuracy | The numbers are wrong, or wrongly converted | Units silently swapped; a value quoted to more digits than anyone could measure |
| Reliability | You cannot tell where the number came from | No method, no date, no instrument, no source |
| Adequacy | It is a good source for a different question | A perfectly sound study of one thing, used to settle another |
| Bias | Somebody's interest shaped what got measured or reported | Only the favourable comparison is shown, and the unfavourable one is not mentioned |

**Adequacy is the one this course will catch you on.** A tabulated
$K_a$ measured at 25 °C is an excellent number and it is not evidence
about your flask at 31 °C. A standard reduction potential is measured at
1 mol/L, and your cell is not. Neither source is unreliable. Both are
being asked a question they did not answer.

| Kind of claim | Check it against |
| --- | --- |
| A tabulated $K_a$ or $K_{sp}$ | [[Reading a Data Table\|the conditions it was measured at]] |
| A titre from your own burette | [[Measuring Well\|what the instrument can resolve]] |
| A pH you calculated | [[Working with Logarithms in Chemistry\|what the digits of a logarithm mean]] |
| A prediction from a model | [[Dynamic Equilibrium\|the boundary the model states for itself]] |
| A claim about a technology | [[Energy Choices\|who benefits from the number]] |

## Bias is not the same as dishonesty

This is where the argument usually goes wrong, in both directions.

A manufacturer's data sheet, a government department's report, and a
campaigning organisation's briefing all have interests. Having an
interest does not make a number false, and it is not an accusation to
say so. What an interest does is shape **which measurements got made,
which comparisons got shown, and which uncertainties got mentioned** —
and those are usually invisible on the page.

So the useful move is never "they are biased, therefore ignore them". It
is: *what would this source have published if the result had gone the
other way?* Then go and find a source with a different interest and see
whether the two agree. Where interested parties agree, you have
something. Where they disagree, the disagreement itself tells you where
to look.

> [!warning] The most persuasive-looking evidence you will ever generate
> Five trials agreeing to three decimal places tells you your technique
> is repeatable. It tells you **nothing** about whether the value is
> right, because tight agreement is exactly what a systematic error
> produces. This year the same trap wears a new costume: an ICE table
> that gives a clean answer, from concentrations that were all read off
> the same badly calibrated instrument. The arithmetic cannot detect it.
> The only thing that can is a comparison against something outside your
> own procedure.

## When your own reading beats the booklet, and when it cannot

There is a real case for ranking claim 4 first and a real case for
ranking it last, and which case is right depends entirely on what you
are trying to decide.

If the question is *what is the standard reduction potential of the
copper half-cell*, the booklet wins and it is not close. Your voltmeter,
your unpolished electrode, and your approximate concentrations are not
competing with a value measured under controlled conditions.

If the question is *what did this cell do this afternoon*, the booklet
cannot answer it at all. It never met your cell. Your reading is the
only evidence in existence, and the booklet's role is to tell you what
you should have seen — which makes the difference between them the
interesting result rather than the error.

Most arguments in this room are really arguments about which of those
two questions is on the table.

## Two traps that carry over, and get worse

**"Consistent with" is not "supports".** Almost anything is consistent
with a vague enough prediction, and models make this worse rather than
better, because a good model is consistent with a great deal. Le
Châtelier's principle can be made to sound consistent with almost any
observed shift after the fact. Evidence that is *hard to explain any
other way* is what carries weight.

**Landing on the accepted value is not proof your method was sound.**
Two errors of opposite sign cancel, and the result looks excellent. If
you cannot account for how your procedure produced that number, agreeing
with the table is luck you have not yet distinguished from skill — and
[[How Marks Work]] says plainly which of those two it pays.

> [!question]- The complication I will raise if nobody else does
> Every ranking above assumes you are free to go and check. Frequently
> you are not: the measurement needs equipment no school has, or the
> underlying data sits behind a paywall, or the question is about a
> system nobody can run twice. At that point you are not weighing
> evidence any more — you are deciding **whose account to trust**, which
> is a different skill and one that science does not exempt you from.
> Scientists trust each other's measurements constantly, because there
> is no alternative. So what makes that trust reasonable rather than
> lazy? Answer that without saying "peer review", which is a mechanism
> and not a reason.

Bring your ranking in writing, and bring one number from your own work
with an uncertainty attached to it and one number from a published
source with its conditions attached. Afterwards, add an entry to your
[[Chemistry Journal]] naming one place your ranking moved and what moved
it. Related: [[Mistakes Are Data]], [[Reading a Data Table]], and
[[Writing a Lab Report]].
