Health and safety in Ontario is not a matter of how considerate your
employer happens to be. It is law, it applies to the shop you are
standing in, and it will apply to the first job you are paid for.

## The Occupational Health and Safety Act

The *Occupational Health and Safety Act* — the OHSA — gives every worker
in Ontario three rights.

- **The right to know.** You must be told about the hazards you will meet
  and given the information, instruction, and training to work safely.
  Being handed a machine with no training is neither normal nor legal.
- **The right to participate.** Workers take part in health and safety at
  their workplace, through a health and safety representative or a joint
  health and safety committee, and you may raise a concern without
  needing anyone's permission first.
- **The right to refuse unsafe work.** If you have reason to believe the
  work is likely to endanger you or someone else, you may refuse it. You
  report the refusal to your supervisor, stay in a safe place, and it is
  investigated. You need not be right; you need to be honest.

The Act also sets out who is responsible for what. Employers must take
every precaution reasonable in the circumstances to protect workers.
Supervisors must ensure workers follow the law and use the required
protective equipment. Workers must work safely, use the equipment
provided, and report hazards. Nobody's duty replaces anyone else's.

> [!important] On a placement, you are a worker
> A student on a work placement is a worker under the Act. The employer
> whose site you are on carries employer duties towards you, exactly as
> for the people they pay — so you are entitled to training before you
> touch equipment, and you keep the right to refuse. Before a placement
> starts, ask who your supervisor is and what insurance coverage applies
> to you there. Your school arranges it, and asking is reasonable.

## WHMIS 2015

WHMIS — the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System — governs
hazardous products at work, and it gives you three things.

- **Labels.** A supplier label carries pictograms, a signal word —
  *Danger* or *Warning* — hazard statements saying what the product does,
  and precautionary statements saying what to do about it. Decant a
  product at work and that container gets a workplace label. An
  unlabelled container is never used.
- **Safety data sheets.** Every hazardous product has an SDS, in a
  standard sixteen-section format, available to you at the workplace. It
  gives the hazards, the protective equipment, safe handling and storage,
  first-aid measures, and what to do about a spill.
- **Training.** You must be trained on WHMIS, and on the specific
  products you use, before you use them.

Some products are labelled under other laws instead — pest control
products are regulated federally, and applying them in Ontario is
licensed work you will not do in this course.
