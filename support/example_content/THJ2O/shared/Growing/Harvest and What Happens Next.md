A grower is not paid for what they grow. They are paid for what arrives
in saleable condition, and the distance between those two numbers is
decided in the hours immediately after picking. At the farm you will
notice the harvest crew moves fast in the morning and that almost
nothing sits in the sun. That is not habit; it is money.

## Pick at the right moment

Every crop has a stage at which it should come off, and it is rarely "as
big as possible". Sweet corn, peas and beans are best young and tender.
Winter squash and onions need full maturity or they will not store.
Tomatoes for the market up the road are picked riper than tomatoes going
three provinces away. Learn the maturity signs — colour, size, firmness,
how easily it separates from the plant — and check them rather than
guessing. Harvest in the cool of the early morning where you can; a crop
picked at two in the afternoon is already warm and already behind.

## Handle it as though it bruises, because it does

Every bruise, scrape and torn stem is a wound, and a wound is where rot
starts. Cut cleanly with a sharp knife or snips rather than tearing. Set
produce down instead of dropping it. Do not overfill containers, and
never stack bins on the produce itself.

## Get the heat out, then keep it out

Field heat is the enemy. Move harvested crops into shade immediately and
into cooling as soon as possible. As [[The Plant's Own Limits]] explains,
a harvested plant is still respiring, and respiration speeds up with
warmth — cooling simply slows the crop's own metabolism down. Most
produce also wants high humidity in storage so it does not shrivel,
except for crops cured dry, such as onions and garlic.

> [!note] Not everything improves after picking
> Some crops go on ripening off the plant — tomatoes and apples, for
> instance — and release a ripening gas as they do. Others, including
> sweet corn and most leafy greens, only decline from the moment they
> are cut, and some are damaged by that same gas. Putting ripening fruit
> in a cooler with lettuce shortens the life of the lettuce.

## Grade, record, and keep it clean

Sort as you go; culls left in a bin spoil their neighbours. Record the
date, quantity, grade and storage conditions — that record is what lets
you compare seasons. Anything a person will eat raises the standard:
wash your hands before handling produce and after every break, use clean
containers rather than the ones that were holding tools, keep produce
off bare ground, wash only with potable water, and do not harvest if you
are unwell. The whole sequence — pick, cool, grade, pack, record — is
what you run for real in [[The Production Run]], using the equipment
described in [[Systems That Do the Work]].
