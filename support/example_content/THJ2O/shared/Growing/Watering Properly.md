More plants are killed by watering than by drought, and almost all of
them are killed with good intentions. Watering is a judgement made plant
by plant, not a chore performed on a schedule, and making it quickly is
one of the most valuable skills in this course.

## Check before you pour

Never water because it is Tuesday. Check first, however suits the job.

- **Push a finger in.** For a bed or a large pot, go down about 5 cm.
  Dry at that depth means water; damp means wait.
- **Lift the pot.** A watered pot and a dry one of the same size feel
  completely different; do this deliberately and your hand becomes a
  moisture meter.
- **Distrust the surface.** The top centimetre dries first and tells you
  almost nothing.
- **Look early in the day.** Many plants wilt in afternoon heat and
  recover by evening; that is not thirst.

## How to water

Water the **soil**, not the plant. Wet foliage sitting overnight invites
fungal disease, and water landing on leaves mostly evaporates. For
**containers**, apply slowly until water runs freely from the drainage
holes, then stop; a pot half-watered develops a dry core that later
water runs straight past. For **beds and new plantings**, water deeply
and less often — a long slow soak sends roots downward, while a daily
sprinkle trains them to stay at the surface, where they die in the first
dry week. Mulch afterwards, and water in the morning.

> [!warning] Overwatering looks exactly like underwatering
> Both give you a wilted, drooping, yellowing plant — because in both
> cases the roots have stopped delivering water. Waterlogged soil drives
> out the oxygen roots need, they suffocate and rot, and the plant wilts
> while sitting in mud. If a wilted plant's soil is wet, adding water
> will finish it. Check the soil before you decide, every time.

## Doing it safely

Water and electricity share the greenhouse and the shop. Keep cords and
outlets clear of spray, report anything damaged before you use it, and
never handle a plugged-in device with wet hands. Coil hoses off walkways
and mop wet floors — the commonest injury at a watering bench is a slip.
This is the practice you will be judged on in [[The Growing Trial]].
