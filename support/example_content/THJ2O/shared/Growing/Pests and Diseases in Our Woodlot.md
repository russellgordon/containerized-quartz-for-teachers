Our job in the woodlot is to look, identify and record — we do not treat
anything. Identification and monitoring are the two IPM steps most often
skipped, and a careful record this year is worth more to next year's
class than any intervention. You will rarely see the pest itself; you
see what it left behind. Dress for it — [[Personal Protective Equipment]].

## Reading the signs

| What you can see | What it may mean | Next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small D-shaped exit holes in ash bark, crown thinning from the top, shoots low on the trunk, bark stripped by woodpeckers | Emerald ash borer, which has killed ash across southern Ontario | Photograph, tag the tree, [[The Pest and Disease Report\|record it]] |
| Round exit holes about the width of a pencil in maple and other hardwoods; a large black beetle with white spots and long banded antennae | Asian long-horned beetle — a regulated pest | Do not move the wood; report to the federal inspection agency |
| Whole branches of an elm yellowing and wilting in summer while the rest stays green | Dutch elm disease, spread by elm bark beetles | Map the tree; note how much of the crown is affected |
| An oak wilting and bronzing from the top of the crown down, leaves discolouring from the edge inward and dropping in midsummer; dark streaking in the wood of a cut branch | Oak wilt, a fungal disease first confirmed in Canada in 2023 at Niagara Falls, and since found in Springwater Township near Barrie and at Niagara-on-the-Lake; a red oak can die within a season | Do not prune or wound the tree. Photograph it and report it |
| Dark green stripes or bands between the veins of beech leaves, seen best looking up at a leaf against the light; later the leaves thicken, curl and go leathery | Beech leaf disease, caused by an introduced nematode; first found in Ontario in 2017 and now through the lower half of southern Ontario | Photograph a leaf from below, against the sky; report it |
| A white waxy fuzz on beech bark, later sunken cankers, small orange-red fruiting bodies in late summer, a thin crown and small yellow leaves | Beech bark disease — a scale insect arrives first, a canker fungus follows years later; advancing through most of the beech range in Ontario | Map the tree, and mark any smooth, uninfested beech as well; those are the ones worth keeping |
| White woolly balls the size of a pinhead where hemlock needles join the newest twigs; needles dropping and the crown thinning from dark green to grey | Hemlock woolly adelgid, first found in Ontario in 2012 and now established near Lake Erie and Lake Ontario | Do not move hemlock branches or nursery stock; report it |
| Oak, birch, aspen and maple leaves chewed to holes or back to the midrib in early summer; buff, felt-like egg masses on trunks and branches from late July | Spongy moth, in Ontario since 1969 and outbreaking on a roughly seven to ten year cycle; the 2021 peak defoliated 1.77 million hectares of Ontario forest | Count the egg masses on your tagged trees — that count is next year's forecast |
| Turf lifting like loose carpet; skunks and raccoons digging it up | White grubs feeding on grass roots | Peel back a square and count grubs |
| Raised ridges across a bed, with small soil mounds | Moles tunnelling after soil insects | Note the extent; a symptom as much as a cause |
| Silvery flecking and tiny black specks on leaves | Thrips | Tap a leaf over white paper and look |

## Where a report goes

Tell your teacher first, because a report leaves here under the school's
name rather than yours. From there it takes one of two routes. Pests the
federal government regulates go to the Canadian Food Inspection Agency —
Asian long-horned beetle, hemlock woolly adelgid, spotted lanternfly, and
oak wilt, which has its own Ontario address,
`OakWiltReportingOntario-Fletrissementduchene@inspection.gc.ca`. Everything
else goes to Ontario's Invading Species Hotline, 1-800-563-7711, or onto
EDDMapS, where a verifier checks your photograph before the record joins a
public map. Send the photograph, the date and the location as precisely as
you can give it. A report that turns out to be a false alarm costs nobody
anything; a find nobody reported costs a great deal.

**Spotted lanternfly — the one we do not have.** Every organism above lives
here. This one does not: as of July 2026 the Canadian Food Inspection
Agency had confirmed no established population anywhere in Canada, only
scattered sightings and interceptions, several of them in Ontario, and the
insect has been federally regulated since 2018 to keep it that way. That
makes it a watch-list species rather than a woodlot pest, and the
difference changes what you are doing: everywhere else on this page you are
recording something known to be present, and here you would be reporting a
first. The adult is about 25 mm long, with grey forewings spotted black and
scarlet hindwings banded black and white; the egg masses are grey, waxy
smears about the same length, on bark, stone or anything left outside.
Look on grapevine and on tree-of-heaven, and for the sticky honeydew and
the black sooty mould that grows on it. If you think you have one, take a
photograph, catch it and keep it in a sealed container, mark the exact
spot, and tell your teacher the same day.

## Monitoring that means something

Walk the same route each time — a fixed transect with numbered, tagged
trees. Record the date, the weather and the observer, and photograph
from the same spot each visit; see [[Photographing Your Work]]. Separate
what you observed from what you concluded. Two visits a year for five
years is a data set; one heroic visit is an anecdote. Confirm every
identification against a second source ([[Where to Find Out]]), and never
move firewood.

## The woodlot itself is the hazard

**Ticks.** Blacklegged ticks carry Lyme disease and are established in
parts of Ontario. Wear light-coloured long sleeves and long trousers
with the cuffs tucked into your socks, stay on the trail, and shower and
check your body and clothing within a couple of hours of coming out. If
you find one, tell your teacher, save it, and watch for an expanding
rash or flu-like symptoms over the following weeks.

**Overhead.** Never stand under a dead tree, a leaning tree or a hanging
broken limb, and stay out of the bush entirely in wind.

**Plants that burn.** Learn poison ivy before you go — three leaflets,
and the oil transfers from clothing and tools. Do not touch giant
hogweed or wild parsnip; their sap causes severe burns on skin exposed
to sunlight. Mark the spot and report it rather than clearing it, and
read [[Hazards on a Green Industry Site]].
