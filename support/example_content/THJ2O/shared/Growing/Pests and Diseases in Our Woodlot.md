Our job in the woodlot is to look, identify and record. We do not treat
anything. Identification and monitoring are the two IPM steps
professionals say are most often skipped and that a class can genuinely
do well, and a careful record from this year is worth more to next
year's class than any intervention we could make.

## Reading the signs

You will almost never see the pest itself. You see what it left behind,
and you work backwards.

| What you can see | What it may mean | Next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small D-shaped exit holes in ash bark, crown thinning from the top, shoots low on the trunk, bark stripped by woodpeckers | Emerald ash borer, which has killed ash across southern Ontario | Photograph, tag the tree, [[The Pest and Disease Report\|record it]] |
| Round exit holes about the width of a pencil in maple and other hardwoods; a large black beetle with white spots and long banded antennae | Asian long-horned beetle — a regulated pest | Do not move the wood; report to the federal inspection agency |
| Whole branches of an elm yellowing and wilting in summer while the rest stays green | Dutch elm disease, spread by elm bark beetles | Map the tree; note how much of the crown is affected |
| Buff-coloured egg masses on trunks in winter; oak leaves stripped bare in early summer | Spongy moth, a defoliator that runs in multi-year cycles | Count egg masses on marked trees each winter |
| Turf lifting like loose carpet; skunks and raccoons digging it up | White grubs feeding on grass roots | Peel back a square and count grubs |
| Raised ridges across a bed, with small soil mounds | Moles tunnelling after soil insects | Note the extent; a symptom as much as a cause |
| Sticky leaves, curled new shoots, clusters of small soft insects | Aphids | Check for predators before calling it a problem |
| Silvery flecking and tiny black specks on leaves | Thrips | Tap a leaf over white paper and look |

## Monitoring that means something

Walk the same route each time — a fixed transect with numbered, tagged
trees. Record the date, the weather and the observer, and photograph
from the same spot each visit; see [[Photographing Your Work]]. Separate
what you observed from what you concluded, and say how confident you
are. Two visits a year for five years is a data set; one heroic visit is
an anecdote. Confirm any identification against a second source before
you put a name in a report — [[Where to Find Out]] says which sources
count. Never move firewood between places: it is the fastest way a
wood-boring insect travels, and moving it is restricted for good reason.

## The woodlot itself is the hazard

**Ticks.** Blacklegged ticks carry Lyme disease and are established in
parts of Ontario. Wear light-coloured long sleeves and long trousers
with the cuffs tucked into your socks, stay on the trail, and shower and
do a full body and clothing check within a couple of hours of coming
out. If you find a tick, tell your teacher, save it, and watch for an
expanding rash or flu-like symptoms in the weeks after.

**Overhead.** Never work or stand under a dead tree, a leaning tree or a
hanging broken limb, and stay out of the bush entirely in wind.

**Plants that burn or blister.** Learn poison ivy before you go — three
leaflets, and the oil transfers from clothing and tools. Do not touch
giant hogweed or wild parsnip; their sap causes severe burns on skin
exposed to sunlight. If you find either, mark the location and report
it — do not clear it. Read [[Hazards on a Green Industry Site]] and
dress according to [[Personal Protective Equipment]] before we leave.
