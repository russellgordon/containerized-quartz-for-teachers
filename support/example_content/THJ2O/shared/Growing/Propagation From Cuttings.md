A seed gives you a plant related to its parents. A cutting gives you the
parent again. That is why the nursery trade runs on cuttings: when a
customer buys a named cultivar they are buying a plant propagated
vegetatively from the original, and every one is genetically identical.

## Three kinds, by how woody the shoot is

**Softwood** cuttings are taken in late spring and early summer from new
growth that bends and snaps rather than folding. They root fastest and
wilt fastest, so they need humidity from the moment they are cut.
**Semi-hardwood** cuttings come later, from shoots that have begun to
firm up — slower, tougher, more forgiving. **Hardwood** cuttings are
taken in late autumn or winter from dormant, fully woody stems; with no
leaves to lose they are easiest to handle, and they are how we produce
the willow and red-osier dogwood stakes used in [[Planting a Shoreline]].

## Taking and sticking a cutting

Work in the morning, when the parent plant is fullest of water, and
choose healthy, vigorous shoots that are not flowering.

1. Cut a piece roughly 10 to 15 cm long, just below a node, because that
   is where roots form most readily.
2. Strip the leaves from the lower third to half. Any leaf buried in the
   medium will rot.
3. Cut large remaining leaves back by about half to reduce water loss.
4. Push the cutting into moist, open medium that drains freely, deep
   enough to stand up, and firm it in.
5. Keep humidity high and light bright but indirect. Gentle warmth under
   the flat speeds rooting.

Some species root faster with a rooting compound. Whether we use one,
and how, is your teacher's decision made with the product label in hand.

Keep everything clean: a sharp knife or sharp secateurs, blades wiped
between parent plants, clean flats, fresh medium. Sharpening is in
[[Maintaining Your Tools]] and grip in [[Hand Tools]]. Cut away from
your body and your other hand every single time — a propagation knife is
sharp enough that a slip is a stitches injury, not a scratch. After
three to six weeks, tug very gently; resistance means roots.

> [!success]- Why your cuttings failed (click to expand)
> **Blackened at the base, mushy** — too wet, or leaves were buried.
> **Shrivelled but green** — humidity too low, or leaves left too large.
> **Bleached and crisp** — direct sun on a closed flat cooked them.
> **Still firm, nothing happening** — wrong season for that species, or
> the wood was too old, or it simply needs longer.
> **Some rooted, some not, no pattern** — mixed parent material; take
> from one healthy plant next time and record which.

Everything stuck at [[The Propagation Bench]] gets a label and a date,
and the strike rate tells you whether your technique is improving.
