Soil is not dirt. It is a mixture of mineral particles, decayed organic
matter, water, air and an enormous amount of life, and the proportions
decide what will grow in it. Roots need water and oxygen at the same
time, which sounds contradictory until you understand that the spaces
between particles hold one or the other.

## Texture — what the mineral part is made of

| Particle | Feels like | Drains | Holds water | Holds nutrients |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sand | Gritty | Very fast | Poorly | Poorly |
| Silt | Smooth, like flour | Moderate | Well | Moderately |
| Clay | Sticky when wet, hard when dry | Very slow | Very well, but grips it | Very well |
| Loam | A balance of all three | Well | Well | Well |

Judge texture with your hands. Wet a small amount to putty consistency
and squeeze it between thumb and finger: sandy soil will not hold a
shape, silty soil feels smooth and forms a short ribbon, and clay forms
a long, shiny ribbon. Do this at the beds, at the farm and at the top of
the shoreline bank and you get three answers in one class.

## What texture does not tell you

**Structure** is how particles clump into crumbs, and compaction
destroys it — driving or walking on wet soil squeezes the air out and
roots stop. **Organic matter** is the most useful thing you can add,
improving drainage in clay and water holding in sand at the same time.
**pH** decides whether nutrients already present are available to the
plant. **Depth and drainage** decide whether anything large can live
there at all. A soil test is how you learn the last two.

## Growing media are not soil

What goes into a pot is deliberately not field soil. A container mix is
built from peat or coir for water holding, perlite or coarse sand for
drainage and air, bark for structure, and compost for nutrients. Field
soil in a pot collapses, holds water at the bottom and suffocates roots.
Choose the mix for the job: fine, open and low in nutrients for
[[Propagation From Seed]]; very free-draining for cuttings; heavier and
richer for plants staying in a container all season.

## Handling soil and media safely

Wear gloves and cover any cut before you start — soil is where tetanus
bacteria live, so keep your immunizations current and report any wound
you get on site. Dry peat, compost and potting mix throw a fine dust you
should not be breathing: dampen material before moving it, work in
ventilated space, and follow [[Personal Protective Equipment]]. Wash
your hands before you eat, every time.
