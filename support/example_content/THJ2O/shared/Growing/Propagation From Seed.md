Seed is the cheapest way to make a great many plants, and the least
forgiving of sloppiness. A tray sown carelessly on Monday looks
identical to a tray sown carefully — for about ten days. Then one comes
up evenly and the other comes up in patches, and there is nothing you
can do to fix it.

## Before you open the packet

Read the packet: species and cultivar, packed-for date, and usually
depth, spacing and days to maturity. Seed does not last forever, so
check the date and expect less of anything two or three years old. Fill
your flats with a **seed-starting mix**, not soil from the beds — field
soil compacts in a container, drains badly, and brings weeds and disease
with it. See [[Soils and Growing Media]]. Moisten the mix until it is
damp like a wrung-out cloth, and fill without packing it down.

> [!tip] Write the label before you sow the seed
> Species, cultivar, date, and your initials. Fifteen seconds, and it is
> the difference between a bench of data and a bench of anonymous green
> things. A labelled empty cell is recoverable; an unlabelled full one
> is not.

## Sowing

**Depth** is what people get wrong. Cover a seed to roughly two or three
times its own width. Big seeds go deep; fine seeds are pressed onto the
surface and barely covered, and a few kinds need light and are not
covered at all — the packet will say. Too deep, and the seedling runs
out of stored food before it reaches the light.

**Spacing.** Sowing thickly wastes seed, makes thinning miserable, and
traps humid still air around the stems, which is what disease wants.

**Water** from below where you can, or with a fine rose that will not
wash seed out of place. Keep the mix evenly moist, never soaking, and
cover the flat to hold humidity until the first seedlings appear.

**Warmth** drives germination, but as soon as seedlings emerge they need
bright light immediately, and slightly cooler conditions make sturdier
plants. Pale and stretched means not enough light.

## Damping off

This soil-borne fungal problem topples seedlings at the soil line,
usually overnight and in a spreading patch. Prevent rather than cure:
clean flats, fresh mix, thin sowing, air movement across the bench, and
no overwatering — see [[Watering Properly]].

Thin to the strongest, pot on when the first true leaves are developed,
and harden anything going outdoors over about a week of increasing
exposure before [[Transplanting and Establishment]]. Record every sowing
at [[The Propagation Bench]]: date, mix, temperature, days to emergence,
and the percentage that came up.
