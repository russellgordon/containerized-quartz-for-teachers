The strip of land where water meets land is called the riparian zone,
and it does more work per square metre than anything else we manage.
Stand at our waterfront after a hard rain and you can see what happens
where it has been removed: the bank slumps, the water goes brown, and
the mown grass that was supposed to look tidy is holding nothing
together at all.

## Why plant it

Deep, fibrous roots bind the bank against wave action and spring ice.
Stems and leaves slow runoff so sediment and nutrients settle out before
they reach open water — the practice described in [[Doing It Better]].
Shade over the water's edge keeps the shallows cooler. And a vegetated
edge is habitat: cover for young fish below the surface, nesting and
feeding above it. A mown lawn to the waterline provides none of this.

## What to plant, in zones

Plant natives, and put each species where its roots want to be.

- **At the water's edge**, where soil is saturated: sedges and rushes,
  blue flag iris, and buttonbush.
- **On the bank**, wet in spring and drier in summer: red-osier dogwood,
  native willows, speckled alder, ninebark, elderberry and nannyberry —
  fast, tough, easy to propagate.
- **Above the bank**: eastern white cedar, serviceberry, and native
  grasses such as Canada wild rye, switchgrass and big bluestem.

Know the invasive species before you plant. Common buckthorn, purple
loosestrife, phragmites, garlic mustard and dog-strangling vine are all
present in southern Ontario and all will outcompete a new planting.

## How we do it

Plant in early spring while stock is dormant. **Live stakes** — dormant
hardwood cuttings of willow and red-osier dogwood, made as in
[[Propagation From Cuttings]] — are pushed into moist soil with the buds
pointing up and most of their length below ground; they root where they
stand and are the cheapest bank stabilization there is. Containerized
shrubs go in following [[Transplanting and Establishment]], and grasses
and sedges as plugs, planted close. Where bare soil is moving, a
natural-fibre erosion control blanket pinned to the slope holds it while
roots take hold. Then stop mowing to the edge, and water through the
first two seasons. Get permission first: find out who owns the land and
check with your local conservation authority, because work near water is
regulated in Ontario and altering a shoreline without approval is an
offence, not a paperwork problem.

Water is the hazard. Work in pairs and never alone at the edge; wet clay
is far more slippery than it looks, and the water stays cold enough to
take your breath long past spring. Follow your teacher's direction on
lifejackets and how close anyone works to the edge, cover any cut before
you start, and wash your hands afterwards. Dress according to
[[Personal Protective Equipment]] — waterproof boots, gloves and long
trousers, because the vegetation above the bank holds ticks.
