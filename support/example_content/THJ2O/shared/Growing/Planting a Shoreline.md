The strip of land where water meets land is the riparian zone, and it
does more work per square metre than anything else we manage. Stand at
our waterfront after a hard rain and you can see what happens where it
has been removed: the bank slumps and the water goes brown.

## Why it matters

Deep, fibrous roots bind the bank against wave action and spring ice.
Stems and leaves slow runoff so sediment settles out before it reaches
the water — see [[Doing It Better]]. A vegetated edge also shades the
shallows and is habitat. A mown lawn does none of this.

## What to plant, in zones

- **At the water's edge**, saturated soil: sedges and rushes, blue flag
  iris, and buttonbush.
- **On the bank**, wet in spring and drier in summer: red-osier dogwood,
  native willows, speckled alder, ninebark and nannyberry.
- **Above the bank**: eastern white cedar, serviceberry, and grasses
  such as Canada wild rye, switchgrass and big bluestem.

Know the invasives first: common buckthorn, purple loosestrife,
phragmites, garlic mustard and dog-strangling vine all outcompete a new
planting. Cover any cut on your hands before you start.

## How we do it, safely

Plant in early spring while stock is dormant. **Live stakes** — dormant
hardwood cuttings of willow and red-osier dogwood, made as in
[[Propagation From Cuttings]] — are pushed into moist soil buds upward
and root where they stand, the cheapest bank stabilization there is.
Containerized shrubs follow [[Transplanting and Establishment]], grasses
go in as plugs, and then you stop mowing to the edge and water for two
seasons. Get permission first: work near water is regulated in Ontario,
so check who owns the land and ask your conservation authority. Work in
pairs, never alone at the edge — wet clay is slippery and the water is
cold. Follow your teacher on lifejackets, and wear boots and gloves.
