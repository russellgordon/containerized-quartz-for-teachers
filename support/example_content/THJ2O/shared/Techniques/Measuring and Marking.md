The green industries run on two measuring systems at once and will not be
tidying that up for you. Lumber is sold in imperial — a two-by-four, an
eight-foot length — while soil, seed, and land area come in metric.

## Reading a tape

The big numbers are inches or centimetres; the small marks between them
are where people go wrong. On an imperial tape the marks halve and halve
again: a half, a quarter, an eighth, a sixteenth. Count them rather than
guessing, and say the measurement out loud before you write it down.

Hold the tape flat against the work, not arched over it, and pull the
hook so it sits against the edge. The hook is loose on purpose: it slides
by exactly its own thickness, so the tape reads correctly whether you
hook an edge or push against one.[^1] Read square-on. One inch is 25.4 mm
exactly, and a metre is a little over three feet three inches.

## Squaring and marking

A rectangle that is not square becomes a gate that will not close. Check
it two ways. **Diagonals** — corner to corner, both ways; equal diagonals
mean a square rectangle, on a picture frame or a bed ten metres across.
**3-4-5** — from a corner, mark three units along one side and four along
the other, and when the marks are exactly five apart, that corner is 90
degrees.

Mark with a sharp pencil or a knife, never a felt marker; a fat line is
three millimetres of argument. Draw the line right across, then put an X
on the waste side so you cut on the correct side of your own mark.
Measure every cut from one reference edge — measuring each cut from the
last one stacks the errors up.

## Working out what a job needs

Bed area in square metres. Soil volume in cubic metres — length by width
by depth, with depth in metres, not centimetres. Plant counts from a
spacing. Lineal metres of lumber from a cut list, plus a waste allowance.
Do it before you order, show your working, and check it against the plan
using [[Reading a Working Drawing]]. Getting it wrong on a real job is
not a mark deduction — it is a second delivery charge.

[^1]: This is called *hook slop*, and it is a feature. A hook that has
    been bent no longer slides correctly — one reason tapes are not
    dropped or left in the mud.
