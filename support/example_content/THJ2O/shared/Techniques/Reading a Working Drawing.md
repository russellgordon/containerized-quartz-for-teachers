A working drawing is an instruction, not a picture. Somebody drew it so
that a person who was not there can build the thing correctly without
asking. You will read three kinds in this course — a garden plan, a field
plot layout, and a greenhouse or structure drawing — and they share one
grammar.

## Plan, elevation, section

**Plan** is the view from directly above, as though the roof were lifted
off; almost every landscape drawing is a plan, because a garden is a
shape on the ground. **Elevation** is the view from the side, level with
the thing — you need it because a plan tells you where a raised bed sits
and only an elevation tells you how tall it is. A **section** is a cut
through, which is what you need for a bed wall, where the interesting
information is the layers you cannot see from outside.

## Scale

Scale is the honest lie every drawing tells. A ratio of 1:50 means one
unit on paper is fifty of the same units on the ground, so 40 mm on the
page is two metres of path. Landscape plans commonly use 1:50, 1:100, or
1:200; a shop drawing may be 1:10 or full size.

Two rules. Read the scale in the title block before you measure anything.
And never scale a dimension off a drawing when a written dimension exists
— paper stretches, photocopiers shrink, and the written number is the one
the designer meant.

## Dimensions and symbols

Dimensions sit on thin lines with arrows at each end. Check whether they
run from a single reference edge or add end to end, and check the parts
sum to the overall. When they do not, ask before you cut.

Symbols are a shorthand you learn once. On a planting plan a circle is a
plant, its size is the mature spread rather than the pot, and a letter
code inside points to the plant list beside the drawing. Hatching means
surfaces — gravel, paving, mulch, turf. A north arrow tells you where the
sun comes from, which is information, not decoration.

> [!example] Read the title block first, every time
> That box in the corner carries the drawing's name, scale, date, units,
> and revision number. A sheet marked Rev B and one marked Rev C are
> different jobs. People have built an entire fence in the wrong place
> because they picked up last week's sheet.

Read the whole sheet before you touch a tool, find the baseline the
dimensions come from, and set that out on the ground first with string
and pegs. You will draw one of these for [[The Site Project]] and build
from one during [[The Shop Build]].
