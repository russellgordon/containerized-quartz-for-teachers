A working drawing is an instruction, not a picture. Somebody drew it so a
person who was not there can build the thing correctly without asking.
You will read three kinds — a garden plan, a field plot layout, and a
greenhouse drawing — and they share one grammar.

## Plan, elevation, section

**Plan** is the view from directly above, as though the roof were lifted
off; almost every landscape drawing is a plan, because a garden is a
shape on the ground. **Elevation** is the view from the side — a plan
tells you where a raised bed sits, and only an elevation says how tall it
is. A **section** is a cut through, which is what you need for a bed
wall, where the information is the layers you cannot see.

## Scale

Scale is the honest lie every drawing tells. A ratio of 1:50 means one
unit on paper is fifty of the same units on the ground, so 40 mm on the
page is two metres of path. Landscape plans commonly use 1:50, 1:100, or
1:200; a shop drawing may be 1:10 or full size. Two rules: read the scale
in the title block before you measure anything, and never scale a
dimension off a drawing when a written one exists — paper stretches,
photocopiers shrink, and the written number is what the designer meant.

## Dimensions and symbols

Dimensions sit on thin lines with arrows at each end. Check whether they
run from one reference edge or add end to end, and that the parts sum to
the overall — when they do not, ask before you cut.

Symbols are shorthand you learn once. On a planting plan a circle is a
plant, sized to the mature spread rather than the pot, with a letter code
pointing to the plant list; hatching means gravel, paving, mulch, or turf.

> [!example] Read the title block first, every time
> That box in the corner carries the drawing's name, scale, date, units,
> and revision number. A sheet marked Rev B and one marked Rev C are
> different jobs — people have built a fence in the wrong place after
> picking up last week's sheet.

Read the whole sheet before you touch a tool, find the baseline the
dimensions come from, and set it out on the ground with string and pegs.
You draw one for [[The Site Project]] and build from one in
[[The Shop Build]].
