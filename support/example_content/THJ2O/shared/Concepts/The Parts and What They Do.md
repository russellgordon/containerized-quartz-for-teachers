Every part of a plant is doing a job, and most of the mistakes made in
this trade come from damaging a part without knowing which job you just
stopped. String-trimming the base of a young maple, planting a shrub
with its root flare buried, stripping the leaves off a cutting — each
one is a person who knew the plant's name but not its plumbing.

## Below ground

**Roots** do three things at once: they anchor the plant, they take up
water and dissolved nutrients, and in many species they store food over
winter. The fine root hairs near the tips do almost all of the
absorbing, and they are what tears away when you yank a seedling out of
a flat. Most tree roots are far shallower and wider than people expect —
think of a dinner plate, not a carrot.

## The stem and what covers it

The **stem** — the trunk, on a tree — holds the leaves up in the light
and moves material in two directions. Water and nutrients travel up from
the roots; sugars made in the leaves travel back down to feed them. Both
pathways sit in a thin layer just under the **bark**, which is why a
wound running right around a trunk kills a tree even though the wood
inside looks fine. **Buds** are next year's growth, already formed and
waiting, so find the bud before you make a pruning cut.

## Above ground

**Leaves** are the factory: a broad flat surface to catch light, tiny
pores underneath that let carbon dioxide in and water vapour out, and
the green pigment that does the work described in
[[How a Plant Actually Works]]. **Flowers** are the reproductive
structure — the parts that make pollen, the parts that receive it, and
the petals and scent whose only purpose is to get an insect to make the
delivery. What follows is the **fruit**, a ripened ovary wrapped around
**seeds**, each one an embryo with a packed lunch.

> [!note] The plant does not care what you call the vegetable
> A tomato, a cucumber and a squash are all fruit, botanically — they
> hold the seeds. A carrot is a root, celery a stem, lettuce a leaf. Use
> the kitchen word at the market and the botanical word in your work
> record; both are correct in their own place.

Build these terms into [[The Plant Identification File]], and remember
them again in [[Transplanting and Establishment]].
