The green industries are not automatically green. Growing things is
still an industrial activity: it moves soil, uses water, burns fuel and
applies materials that do not stay where you put them. Being honest
about that is not an attack on farming or landscaping — it is the first
half of the job. You cannot reduce an effect you will not name.

## Water

Whatever you put on the ground is one rainfall away from being somewhere
else. Nutrients from fertilizer and manure travel with runoff and with
water moving through the soil, and when they reach a lake they feed
algae rather than crops.[^phosphorus] Pesticides move the same way, and
can drift on the wind during application. Bare or worked soil moves too:
sediment washing off a field or an unplanted bank clouds the water,
buries the gravel that fish spawn in, and carries attached nutrients
with it. Irrigation and washing take water out as well — on a dry
August, a farm, a golf course and a town draw from one watershed.

## Air and climate

Engines are the obvious source: tractors, mowers, trimmers, chainsaws
and trucks all emit air pollutants and greenhouse gases. Less obvious is
the soil itself — tilling releases carbon that had been stored in
organic matter, and nitrogen fertilizer and manure produce their own
greenhouse gas emissions. Livestock operations add to it directly.

## Energy, noise and land

A heated greenhouse through a Canadian winter and a cold room through a
Canadian August are both large, continuous energy demands. Noise is a
genuine effect on neighbours and on wildlife. And clearing land removes
what was there before — a hedgerow, a wetland, a woodlot edge.

Every one of these has a practice that reduces it, and every practice
costs somebody money, time or convenience. That tension is this unit.
Read the reductions in [[Doing It Better]], see one built at the water's
edge in [[Planting a Shoreline]], and account for both sides in
[[The Site Project]].

[^phosphorus]: Phosphorus is the nutrient that most often drives algal
    blooms in fresh water. Lake Erie's recurring summer blooms are the
    best-known Ontario example; farm and urban runoff both contribute.
