Walk into the woodlot in November and the sorting starts before anyone
says a word. Some things overhead are bare, some are still green, and
everything at knee height is either woody and standing or brown and
collapsed on the ground. You have just used three of the four questions
that separate one plant group from another. Asking them on purpose turns
a walk in the bush into work someone would pay you for — a nursery
order, a pruning schedule and a planting plan all depend on getting the
group right before you get the name right.

## Four questions that sort almost everything

You will not know every species. You do not have to. Get the group
first, and the identification key does the rest.

| Question | The answers you will get | Why it matters on the job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Woody or herbaceous? | Woody keeps a permanent above-ground stem; herbaceous dies back to the ground | Decides whether you prune it or cut it down |
| How long does it live? | Annual, biennial, perennial | Decides how often it is replanted, and what it costs |
| Does it hold its leaves? | Deciduous drops them; evergreen keeps them through winter | Decides winter screening, and who rakes in October |
| Broadleaf or conifer? | Broad flat leaves and true flowers; or needles and cones | The first branch of nearly every identification key |

## Habit — the shape a plant grows into

Within the woody group, habit is the next split, and it is a trade term
rather than a scientific one. A **tree** carries a single dominant trunk
and holds its crown above your head. A **shrub** branches from near the
ground into several stems of similar size. A **vine** cannot hold itself
up and borrows someone else's structure. A **groundcover** spreads
sideways and stays low. Pruning can push the same species toward two of
these, which is exactly why the catalogue price differs.

## Three sites, every group

At the farm, nearly everything in the field is a herbaceous annual —
sown, grown, harvested and gone inside one season, which is why that
soil is worked every year. In the woodlot, nearly everything of any size
is a woody perennial that has stood since before you were born. Along
the shoreline you meet the third case: herbaceous perennials, mostly
grasses and sedges, whose tops die each autumn while the roots hold the
bank all winter.
