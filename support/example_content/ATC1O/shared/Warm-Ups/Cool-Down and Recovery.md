The last five minutes of class are the ones most often skipped and the ones
that decide how you feel tomorrow. A cool-down is not a reward for
finishing. It is the second half of the warm-up, and it does three specific
jobs.

## What the cool-down is doing

**Bringing the system down gradually.** During hard work your heart is
pumping blood into working legs and your legs are pumping it back. Stop
dead and the return pump stops while the heart is still racing, which is
why people go light-headed after sprinting to a finish. Two or three
minutes of easy walking and swinging solves it.

**Returning muscle towards resting length.** This is the moment for long,
held stretches — the ones that have no place in a warm-up. Everything is
warm, so range comes easily. Hold each one for a slow thirty seconds and
breathe out into it. If it is sharp, it is too far.

**Getting you out of the studio.** Ninety seconds lying still, noticing what
worked and what hurt, is how the class becomes something you can write about
later in [[Your Movement Journal]] instead of a blur.

## Between studio days

Recovery is where training actually happens — muscle adapts during rest, not
during the class. So:

- **Sleep** is the single largest recovery factor available to a fifteen
  year old, and it is free.
- **Water and food** on a studio day are not optional. Dancing on empty is
  not discipline; it is how you get injured.
- **Soreness that arrives a day or two later** is normal after new work.[^1]
  Move gently, keep going, and it fades.
- **Pain that is sharp, in a joint, swelling, or worse each session** is not
  soreness. Stop and tell your teacher the same day.

That last point is an ethical practice as much as a safety one. Hiding an
injury to stay in a piece puts you out for longer and leaves your group
without a dancer at the worst possible moment — and quietly tells everyone
else in the room that pushing through is what we admire here. It is not.
Say something early; [[Getting Help]] is how.

[[The Standard Warm-Up]] and this page are two ends of the same idea: one
prepares the body for load, the other returns it to rest.

[^1]: Delayed onset muscle soreness usually peaks a day or two after
    unfamiliar work, especially work with a lot of lowering and landing in
    it. It is not a sign of damage and not a measure of how good the class
    was.
