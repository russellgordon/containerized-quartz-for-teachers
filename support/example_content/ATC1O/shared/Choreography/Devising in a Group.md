Groups do not fail at choreography because nobody has ideas. They fail
because forty minutes disappear into discussion, or because one person makes
everything, or because a disagreement about a transition turns into a
disagreement about a person. All three are avoidable, and avoiding them is
a skill you are being assessed on.

## Get on your feet in the first ten minutes

Whatever your stimulus is — an object, an image, a piece of text, a
question — the first move is to put it in your bodies rather than talk about
what it might mean. Four people improvising with a length of fabric for five
minutes will generate more usable material than the same four people
planning for half an hour. Talk after you have something to talk about.

## Roles, rotated

At any moment somebody should be **outside**, watching from the front,
because dancers inside a piece genuinely cannot see it. Somebody should be
**keeping time**, so the last ten minutes are not a surprise. Somebody
should be **writing down what you decided**, because a decision nobody
recorded gets remade next class. Rotate all three every rehearsal, so no one
becomes the permanent choreographer or the permanent scribe.

## Deciding and disagreeing

When two ideas compete, do not vote. Try both for three minutes each and
look at them — dance is settled on the floor, and the room will usually
agree once it has seen the two versions. Vote only when trying is impossible.

Disagree about the dance, never the dancer. "That doesn't read from the
front" is a useful sentence. "That's bad" is not, and "you always do this"
is a different conversation that does not belong in a rehearsal. If the
quiet person in your group has not spoken in twenty minutes, ask them
directly — they have usually noticed the thing nobody else has.

> [!question]- Four moves when your group is stuck (click to expand)
> Cut the weakest thirty seconds and see if anyone misses it. Run the whole
> thing at double speed, then at half. Give the section to one dancer alone
> and watch what the others were hiding. Or go back to the stimulus and make
> two minutes of fresh material without discussing it first.

Write down who made what as you go. Building on somebody's idea is how
devising works and is entirely fair; taking it without saying so is not, and
your programme note for [[The Showing]] is where the group credits itself
honestly. [[Composition From a Stimulus]] covers where the raw material
comes from, and [[Showing and Feedback]] is what to do with the version you
have at the end of today.
