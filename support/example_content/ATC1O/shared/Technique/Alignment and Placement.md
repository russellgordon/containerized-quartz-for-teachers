Alignment is how your bones are stacked when you are not asking them to do
anything clever. Placement is what you do to keep that stack while you
move. Almost every correction you will hear this year is one or the other,
so it is worth understanding once, properly, rather than being told to
"stand up straight" for four years.

Your skeleton is built to carry you. The axial part — skull, spine, ribs —
is the column; the appendicular part — arms, legs, and the girdles that
attach them — hangs off it and travels. When the column is stacked, bone
passes weight down to the floor and your muscles are free to move you. When
it is not stacked, muscles have to hold you up instead, and they get tired,
tight, and eventually sore.

## The stack, from the floor up

Weight through the middle of each foot, not gripped into the toes. Knees
soft and tracking over the second toe. Pelvis level, neither tucked under
nor tipped forward. Ribs closed over the pelvis. Shoulders wide and resting
down. Head balanced on top of the spine, ears roughly over shoulders. The
spine keeps its natural curves — flattening them is not good posture, it is
a different fault.

## Four faults, and how each one feels

- **Locked or hyperextended knees.** The leg feels "solid" and effortless,
  because you are hanging on the joint instead of using muscle. It hides
  balance problems now and irritates the knee later.
- **Rib flare.** Trying to look lifted, the front ribs push forward and the
  lower back overarches. It feels tall. It is actually a hinge, and your
  turns will wobble at exactly that point.
- **Tucked pelvis.** Gripping the seat to "straighten" the back. Feels
  strong, shortens your reach in every direction, and jams the hips.
- **Sinking into one hip.** Standing on one leg, the standing hip slides
  out sideways. Feels comfortable. Loads the joint unevenly.

> [!warning] Turnout comes from the hip, never from the feet
> If you twist your feet outwards on the floor and let your knees follow,
> the twisting happens at the knee, which is not built to twist. Find the
> rotation at the hip joint first and let the feet end wherever the hip
> allows — that will be different for every person in the room, and a
> smaller turnout that is honest is worth more than a wide one that is
> borrowed from your knees.

Alignment is not a shape you achieve and keep. It is a thing you return to,
hundreds of times per class, which is why it is the whole point of standing
centre in [[A Technique Class]]. [[How the Body Moves]] takes the skeleton
in more detail, and [[The Safe Dancer]] is where you record which of these
faults is yours.
