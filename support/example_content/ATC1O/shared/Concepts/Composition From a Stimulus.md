Handed a length of fabric and told to make a dance, most people do the same
three things: wave it, wrap themselves in it, and then stand there. That is not
a failure of imagination. It is what happens when a stimulus is treated as a
toy instead of as a question.

A **stimulus** is a starting point outside your own habits — an object, an
image, a sound, a process, a text. Its job is to give you something to answer,
so the movement comes from somewhere other than what your body already does by
default.

## Interrogating it before you dance

Spend two minutes on the source before you move with it, and ask what it
*does*, not what it looks like. How does it move when nobody is holding it —
does it fall, drift, roll, resist? What is its weight, and what does carrying
that weight do to your spine? What is it for in ordinary life, and what happens
if you use it wrongly? And what could it stand for — a hockey bag is a hockey
bag, and it is also everything a person drags around with them.

That last question is what turns an exercise into a composition. If your object
is a symbol, the dance has to make that legible, usually through how the
dancers *treat* it rather than by labelling it. An object three dancers avoid
looking at has already told the audience something.

> [!success]- A stimulus does not have to be an object (click to expand)
> Some of the strongest starting points are processes rather than things. A
> tide going out. Ice forming. A crowd leaving a building. These give you
> structure as well as material, because a process has an order built into it,
> and that order can become your form. Try describing the process in five
> stages, then give each stage a movement quality before you choreograph a
> single step.

## Respond, do not illustrate

The trap is showing the audience your stimulus rather than answering it. If
your source is rain, a piece where everyone flutters their fingers downward is
a picture of rain. A piece where a group holds still and one dancer keeps
having to change position is *about* rain. The second one is choreography.

If your stimulus belongs to a culture that is not yours — a piece of music, an
artwork, a ceremony — find out what it is and whether it is meant to be
borrowed at all. We take that question up directly in
[[Does a Dance Belong to a Culture?]].

Once you have material, the ordinary tools apply: build phrases, arrange them
into a form, and test the piece with and without accompaniment, which is the
subject of [[With Music and Without]]. [[The Composition Study]] assesses your
finished response — including whether the connection is visible to someone who
was not in the room when you started.
