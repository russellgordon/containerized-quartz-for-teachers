The piece is learned. Everyone knows the counts, the spacing works, the ending
is clean. And in this afternoon's run it still read as a group of people
accurately doing steps. What is missing is not more rehearsal of the material.
It is the decision about what the audience should *feel*, and the technique for
delivering it.

Mood in performance is not summoned by feeling things very hard backstage. It
is built from a small number of controllable choices, and it can be rehearsed
like anything else.

## The tools you actually have

- **Gaze.** The most powerful signal you own. Lowered reads as inward or
  grieving; fixed past the audience reads as resolve; eye contact with another
  dancer creates a relationship the audience will try to explain.
- **Face.** Less than you think. A face doing nothing in particular, jaw
  unclenched, lets the body speak. One working overtime performs the emotion.
- **Breath.** Audible breath changes the temperature of a piece. Holding it —
  which nervous dancers do constantly — makes everything look tighter.
- **Dynamic quality.** The same phrase sustained rather than sharp is a
  different mood, and the most reliable tool, because it is *in* the movement.
- **Stillness.** Held long enough to be a choice, it is the loudest of these.

## Rehearse it, do not hope for it

Decide the mood of each section in words, write it down, and rehearse it. "Bar
one to sixteen: wary. From the lift: certain." Then run it and check what a
viewer actually perceives, because your internal experience of a mood and its
external reading are often different — a discovery best made in
[[Video of Yourself]] rather than in front of an audience.

Consistency matters most in a group. Six dancers with six private moods produce
no mood at all. That is worth ten minutes of talking at a rehearsal.

> [!tip] Match the mood to the message, not to your nerves
> Under pressure most performers default to one of two settings: apologetic,
> with a dropped gaze and shrinking movement, or relentlessly cheerful, with a
> fixed smile. Both are nerves rather than choices, and both will contradict
> the piece. If your work is sombre, a smile is not warmth — it is noise.

Everything else on stage should agree with the mood you chose. Lighting, sound,
and what you wear do the same job as your gaze, which is why [[Stagecraft]] and
this page are really one subject. Bring both to [[The Showing]], where an
audience will read every one of these signals whether or not you chose them.
