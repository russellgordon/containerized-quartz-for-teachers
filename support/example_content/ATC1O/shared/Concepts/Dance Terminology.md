A teacher says "flat back" and half the room moves before the sentence is
finished. That is what terminology buys: a whole shape, transmitted in two
syllables, with no demonstration needed. In a studio where time is the scarce
resource, that is not fussiness. It is speed.

Every dance form carries its own set of these words, and they are not
interchangeable between forms. Learning them is part of learning the form
itself, not an add-on for the written work.

## What a term actually holds

Take a handful from jazz dance, the family of forms most of you have seen
without knowing the names for what you saw:

- **Isolation** — moving one part of the body while everything else stays
  genuinely still. Ribcage without shoulders. Head without spine.
- **Flat back** — the torso hinged forward from the hip joints with the spine
  held long, rather than curled.
- **Rock step** — a transfer of weight onto one foot and back again, going
  nowhere, which is precisely why it can set up anything.
- **Pivot turn** — turning on the ball of a supporting foot to face a new
  direction.

Each of those names something *precise*. "Flat back" is not "bend over"; the
whole content of the term is the flat. Use the word loosely and you have thrown
away the only thing it was carrying.[^origins]

## Learning them so they stay

Terminology sticks when it is attached to your body, not to a page. Say the
word out loud while you do the movement — pairing sound with sensation is what
makes it retrievable later. Group terms by what they do, not alphabetically:
travelling steps together, turns together, torso shapes together. Write each
term and a one-line physical description in your own words the same day, not
the teacher's words. Then build a short sequence containing five terms and
nothing else. If you can perform it, you know them.

You need this vocabulary in two very different places: on the floor, where
[[Learning a Sequence]] goes at the pace of the slowest word you do not know,
and on the page, where [[Writing About Dance]] turns "the bit where she went
down" into something a reader can picture.

[^origins]: Terms often carry their form's history. Ballet's vocabulary is in
    French because ballet was codified at the French court in the seventeenth
    century; jazz terminology is largely plain descriptive English, developed
    in American studios and on stages far more recently. When you meet a term
    in [[Three Forms We Practise]], the language it is in is usually telling
    you where the form was formalised.
