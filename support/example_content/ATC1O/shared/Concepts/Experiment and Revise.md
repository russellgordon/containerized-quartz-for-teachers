The version you showed today is a draft. That is an uncomfortable sentence in a
studio, because a draft in dance is not a page you can put in a drawer — it is
something you performed, in front of people, with your body. Which is why the
next step should be deliberate. Experimenting and revising are two different
things, and they happen in that order: experimenting widens the options,
revising narrows them.

## Experiment: make more than one version

The most useful experiment is the simplest — build the *same* idea twice under
different conditions, and compare. A version with a strict structure and a
version with none. One with the track you chose and one in silence. One that
lasts ninety seconds and one that lasts thirty.

This feels wasteful. It is not. Until there are two versions you have nothing
to judge, and "it's fine" is the only available verdict. With two, the
questions get sharp: which holds attention longer? Which says the thing you
meant? A structure can make material clearer or flatten it, and you cannot know
which until you have seen both. [[With Music and Without]] is built on this
comparison.

## Revise: change something specific

Revision is not "run it again with more energy". It is a named change to a
named part: cut a section, slow a transition, give one dancer a different
relationship to the group, change the ending, remove the fourth repetition
nobody needed.

Feedback is the raw material, and you get it from peers and teacher in
[[Showing and Feedback]]. Peers tell you what an audience actually perceived,
which you cannot know from inside the piece. Teachers tell you what is fixable
in the time remaining. Both are useful; they are not the same information.

> [!warning] Do not throw the piece out
> When a showing goes badly, the reflex is to scrap everything and start again
> two days before the deadline. Almost every time, the problem is one section,
> one transition, or an ending that stops rather than finishes. Before you
> discard anything, film the version you have. A deleted piece cannot be
> compared to its replacement, and you may be losing the best ninety seconds.

Write down each revision and why — not for marks, but because in three weeks
you will not remember whether the ending changed because it was unclear or
because someone was away. [[The Composition Study]] asks you to account for
your choices, and the account is only honest if it was written while they were
being made.
