Nothing you danced this period was invented in this room. Every form has an
address — a place, a period, and a group of people who made it because they
needed it for something. Knowing that address changes how you dance the form,
because most of what looks arbitrary in a technique turns out to be a solution
to a problem someone actually had.

Forms are also not sealed. They meet each other, borrow, and become new things,
usually in cities, usually because people who were not from the same place
ended up living on the same street.

## Tap dance, as a worked example

Tap did not descend from one tradition. It came out of the contact between West
African dance traditions carried to North America by enslaved people and their
descendants, and the step and clog dancing of Irish and British immigrants —
two traditions that both put the rhythm in the feet, meeting in port cities and
tenements across the nineteenth century. By the 1920s and 1930s the form was on
stages and in films, recognisably its own thing: percussive, improvisatory, and
built for an audience to *hear*.

That history is legible in the movement. The upright carriage and intricate
footwork of the step traditions, the rhythmic complexity and grounded weight of
the African traditions — both are still there. You can see the argument in the
body.

## What to ask about any form

- **Where and when** did it take shape, and who were the people who made it?
- **What was it for** — worship, celebration, courtship, resistance,
  spectacle, passing time on a ship's deck?
- **Where was it done** — a court, a street, a field, a hall, a stage? Low
  ceilings and hard floors produce different dance than open ground.
- **What changed** when it travelled, was filmed, was taught in studios, or was
  put on a stage for people who were not part of it?
- **Is it a living tradition** with people who hold it now? Usually yes.

> [!note]- On "traditional" and "modern" (click to expand)
> Be careful with the word *traditional*. It often gets used to mean "old and
> finished", as if people dancing a form today were re-enacting rather than
> practising. Forms that are centuries old are still being made new by people
> alive right now — new steps, new music, new occasions. A form is not a
> historical artefact just because it is not from this decade.

Tracing one form properly — its origins, what shaped it, how it arrived where
it is now — is the work of [[The Form Study]], and the forms you practise on
the floor are introduced in [[Forms From Around the World]].
