You now have phrases. Several of them, built in class, sitting in your journal
and in your body. The next question is the one that separates a pile of
material from a dance: **in what order, and by whom?**

That is choreographic form — the architecture deciding which phrase happens
when, who is doing it, and what the audience has already seen by the time each
moment arrives. Two groups given identical phrases and different forms will
produce works that look nothing alike.

## Forms you can use this week

| Form | The arrangement | What it does to an audience |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AB | One phrase, then a contrasting one | Sets up a difference and lets you feel it |
| ABA | Phrase, contrast, return | The return lands differently — you have changed, or it has |
| Canon | The same phrase, entered at staggered times | Turns one phrase into texture; makes a group look enormous |
| Call and response | One dancer or group offers, another answers | Builds relationship without a word |
| Theme and variation | One phrase, returned to and altered each time | Rewards attention; teaches the audience to watch for change |
| Narrative | Ordered by the events of a story | Clear, and the hardest to keep from becoming mime |

None of these is advanced. Canon is what a round is in music, and you have been
singing rounds since Grade 2. What makes it choreography is choosing it on
purpose, for a reason you can state.

## Form is a decision, not a container

The mistake is to make all the material first and then look for a shape to pour
it into. Form works better as a question asked early: *what should the audience
understand at the halfway point?* An ABA answers that differently than a canon
does.

Structure also settles the problem every group has: four people with four
phrases will otherwise negotiate the order by whoever talks loudest. "We are
making this in canon" ends that argument in ten seconds and gets you back on
the floor. [[Structures for Making]] collects the ones we use most.

## Transitions are part of the form

The joins between sections are choreography too, and they are where unfinished
work shows. If your dancers walk casually into position, you have told the
audience the dance stopped. Make the arrival material — a slow turn, a change
of level, a pause held long enough to read as intentional.

You will build sections from your own material in [[Phrase Building]], and
[[The Phrase]] assesses the arrangement, not only the steps.
