Here is a task with rules: take the eight-count phrase you learned in technique
class and perform it backwards. Not reversed in direction — reversed in order,
last count first, so the phrase runs from its end to its beginning. Nothing
else may change.

That is a movement problem. It has a fixed input, a stated constraint, and no
single right answer, and it will teach you more about the phrase than
practising it forwards another twenty times. You cannot reverse something you
do not actually know.

## Operations you can apply to any phrase

| Operation | What you change | What it exposes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retrograde | Perform from last movement to first | Which movements were only transitions |
| Inversion | Every high becomes low, every reach a fold | How much was shape and how much was direction |
| Change of tempo | Same movement, half or double speed | That energy is not independent of timing |
| Change of body part | An elbow performs the gesture, not a hand | How much of the phrase lived in one limb |
| Change of level | The whole phrase moved to the floor, or upright | Which movements needed the floor to make sense |
| Change of facing | The phrase turned upstage or to the side | What the audience was actually reading |

Pick one. Applying two at once produces something unrecognisable, which is a
different exercise and usually a less instructive one.

## Accuracy first, then transformation

Retrograde is unforgiving about vagueness. If you were fudging counts five and
six, running the phrase backwards will stop dead there. So the first stage is
always the same: reproduce the original precisely, at speed and slowly, until
you can name what happens on each count. That is the work of
[[Learning a Sequence]], and it is not optional here.

Then transform on paper before you transform in the body. Number the movements,
write the new order, and walk it through marking — half-scale, no performance.
Improvising a retrograde in real time is how groups spend a whole period
producing nothing.

## Why we bother

Choreographers use these operations constantly, because generating brand-new
material for every second of a work is neither possible nor desirable — an
audience has to recognise something in order to notice that it has changed. A
phrase you have inverted and slowed is still your phrase, and now you have two
minutes of related material instead of eight counts. You will use at least one
transformation in [[The Phrase]], and the habit carries straight into
[[Phrase Building]].
