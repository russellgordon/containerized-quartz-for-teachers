Two things in this course are not about physics and are marked anyway:
posing a question precise enough to answer, and finding out what is
already known without being fooled. Grade 12 asks more of both than
Grade 11 did, because the questions you are given get vaguer as you go.

## A question you can actually answer

"Does the angle affect the range?" cannot fail and cannot succeed. Tighten
it in three moves:

1. **Name what you will change**, and its levels. Launch angle → 20°, 30°,
   40°, 50°, 60°.
2. **Name what you will measure**, and how. Range → horizontal distance to
   the first mark, in metres, from five trials.
3. **Name what stays fixed.** Launch speed, launch height, projectile,
   surface.

> "How does launch angle (20°–60°) affect the horizontal range of a
> projectile launched at a fixed speed from a fixed height?"

**A hypothesis is not a guess.** It is a prediction with a mechanism
attached: *range will peak near 45° and be symmetric about it, because
increasing the angle raises the flight time and lowers the horizontal
speed, and the product of the two is greatest there.* A prediction with a
mechanism is testable and interesting when it fails; one without is
neither.

## Reading what has already been done

| Where | Good for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Review articles | The state of a field, with references worth following | A review is somebody's summary; go to the primary work for detail |
| Primary papers and experiment reports | What was actually measured, and how | Sample sizes, error bars, and whether the conclusion matches the data |
| Preprint servers (arXiv) | Current work, months before publication | No peer review yet — a preprint is a claim, not a finding |
| Government and agency data | Energy, radiation, and safety figures | Definitions change between years; read the footnotes |
| Manufacturer and standards documents | What a real device does | Written to sell or to comply, not to explain |
| News and video | What is being claimed publicly | The gap between the paper and the headline is where the errors live |

Read a paper in this order: abstract, then conclusions, then methods,
then results. Methods are where a paper is judged, and they make far more
sense once you know what the authors think they found.

## Judging a source

Five questions, in order, and the first failure ends it: **who wrote it,
what is the evidence, when, who benefits, and does anyone independent
agree?**

That last one catches most bad claims: three articles can look like three
sources and trace back to one press release. Follow each claim to its
origin before counting it as corroboration.

Bias is not the same as wrong. A manufacturer's measurements can be
accurate and still need an independent check; an advocacy group's report
can be excellent evidence. Note the interest, look for the independent
check, and say in your writing what you found.

> [!warning] Physics has its own failure mode here
> The internet is full of confident, fluent explanations of relativity
> and quantum mechanics that are wrong in ways a Grade 12 student cannot
> yet detect. Your defence is not cleverness; it is checking a claim
> against a source with an author, a date, and an institution behind it —
> and asking me when two sources disagree.

## Recording as you go

From the first source, keep: author or organisation, date, title, where
you found it, and **one sentence in your own words**. That sentence
prevents accidental copying later, and it turns a bibliography into notes
you can actually write from.

Cite where you use a claim, not only at the end. "The 2015 SNO result
established that…" tells a reader what your sentence rests on at the
moment they need to know.
