$$F_s = -kx \qquad E_e = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$$

A spring pulls back in proportion to how far it is stretched. The minus
sign says "back": the force opposes the displacement, which is what makes
oscillation possible.

## k is the spring, not the stretch

The spring constant, in newtons per metre, describes the spring itself. A
stiff spring has a large $k$. It is found from the SLOPE of a force
against extension graph, not from one pair of readings — see
[[Hooke's Law and Simple Harmonic Motion]].

## Where the ½ comes from

The force is not constant while you stretch it, so the work is the area
under the line — a triangle of base $x$ and height $kx$, giving
$\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$. Every "½" in physics is either an average or a
triangle, and this one is both.

## The limit of proportionality

Real springs obey Hooke's law only up to a point; past it they deform
permanently. Finding that point experimentally is one of the more
satisfying moments in the lab, and one of the few where you are allowed to
break the equipment on purpose.
