Thomas Young, in 1801, settled a century-old argument by letting light
interfere with itself.

## The geometry

With slit separation $d$, screen distance $L$, and fringe spacing
$\Delta y$:

$$\lambda = \frac{d\sin\theta}{n} \qquad \text{and, for small angles,} \qquad \lambda \approx \frac{d\,\Delta y}{L}$$

That second form is the one you use in the lab, and it means a metre
stick and a laser can measure a wavelength of 650 nm — a length far too
small to see, measured with equipment you can hold.

## What each variable does

| Change | Effect on the fringes |
| --- | --- |
| Wider slit separation $d$ | Fringes closer together |
| Longer wavelength (red vs blue) | Fringes further apart |
| Screen further away | Fringes further apart |

## Why it mattered so much

Because it is very hard to explain with particles, and Newton's authority
had made particles the default for a hundred years. One experiment,
carefully done, overturned it — which is a lesson about evidence as much
as about light.
