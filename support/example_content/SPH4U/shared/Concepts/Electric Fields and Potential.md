The electric field is the force a small positive test charge would feel,
per unit charge:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{F_e}{q} = \frac{kQ}{r^2}$$

Field lines point away from positive charges and toward negative ones,
never cross, and crowd where the field is strong.

## Potential and potential difference

$$V = \frac{kQ}{r} \qquad \Delta V = \frac{\Delta E}{q}$$

Potential is energy per unit charge — a scalar, which makes it far easier
to work with than the field. The "voltage" of Grade 11 circuits is a
potential DIFFERENCE, and this is where that word finally means something.

## Between parallel plates

The field is uniform, and

$$\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta V}{d}$$

Uniform fields are the electric equivalent of a flat table: they make
problems solvable, which is why every textbook charged particle travels
between two plates.

## Where this shows up

Photocopiers, inkjet printers, electrostatic precipitators in smokestacks,
cathode-ray tubes, and the deflection plates in an oscilloscope. All of
them are a charge steered by a field you can compute.
