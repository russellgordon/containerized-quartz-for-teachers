Light interferes like a wave and ejects electrons like a particle. De
Broglie's move was to ask whether matter does the same in reverse:

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

Every particle has a wavelength, inversely proportional to its momentum.

## Why you have no wavelength worth mentioning

For a person walking, $\lambda$ comes out around $10^{-35}\ \text{m}$ —
inconceivably smaller than anything that could diffract you. For an
electron, it is comparable to atomic spacing, which means crystals should
diffract electrons.

They do. Davisson and Germer saw it in 1927, and electron microscopes have
exploited it ever since: shorter wavelength than light means finer detail.

## What this does NOT mean

It does not mean an electron is "sometimes a wave and sometimes a
particle", as if it switched. It means neither classical picture fits, and
the honest description is mathematical: a wave function whose square gives
the probability of finding the particle somewhere.

Being uncomfortable with that is the correct response. Physics was too,
for a decade, and the discomfort produced most of twentieth-century
physics.
