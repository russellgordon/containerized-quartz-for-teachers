In 1930 a conservation law was in trouble, and the way physics got out of
the trouble is the best argument this course can make for taking
conservation laws seriously.

## The problem: beta decay did not add up

When a nucleus undergoes beta decay it emits an electron. If that were the
whole story, the electron would always leave with the SAME energy — the
difference in mass–energy between the parent and daughter nuclei, exactly
as in alpha decay, where the emitted particle does come out at one sharp
energy.

Instead, experiments through the 1920s found a **continuous spectrum**:
electrons emerged with every energy from nearly zero up to a maximum, and
only the ones at the very top of the range carried the full amount.

Two conservation laws were being violated at once:

| Law | What was missing |
| --- | --- |
| Conservation of energy | Most decays released less energy than the mass difference allowed |
| Conservation of momentum | The electron and the recoiling nucleus did not come out back to back |

Niels Bohr was prepared to give up conservation of energy inside the
nucleus. That is how serious this looked.

## The move Pauli made

In December 1930 Wolfgang Pauli wrote to a physics conference he was not
attending — he was at a ball in Zürich — proposing what he called a
"desperate remedy": an undetected particle, emitted alongside the
electron, carrying away the balance of the energy and momentum.

From the conservation laws alone, its properties followed:

- **Electrically neutral**, because charge already balanced.
- **Very small mass**, because the maximum electron energy matched the
  full mass difference.
- **Spin one half**, so that angular momentum balanced too.
- **Almost non-interacting**, because nobody had seen one.

Enrico Fermi built the theory around it and named it the *neutrino* — the
little neutral one. It was 26 years before Cowan and Reines detected one
directly, in 1956.

> [!important] What this episode is actually about
> Pauli did not invent a particle because it was elegant. He calculated
> what a particle would have to be like for energy and momentum to still
> balance, published the prediction, and left it to be checked — and it
> could have been refuted at any point in those 26 years. That is the
> difference between a scientific rescue of a law and an excuse for one.

## Where it goes from here

Neutrinos turned out to be produced in enormous numbers by the Sun, and
detectors underground found only about a third of the predicted flux — a
discrepancy that stood for thirty years. The resolution, established at
the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory and honoured with the 2015 Nobel Prize,
is that neutrinos change type in flight, which they can only do if they
have mass after all. See [[Where This Physics Leads]] for the Canadian
end of that story, and [[The Standard Model]] for where the particle sits
now.
