Velocity is always measured relative to something. Usually the ground, and
usually silently — which is fine until the something is itself moving.

$$\vec{v}_{AC} = \vec{v}_{AB} + \vec{v}_{BC}$$

Read the subscripts as a chain: A relative to B, plus B relative to C,
gives A relative to C. The inner letters cancel, which is a genuinely
useful bookkeeping trick.

## The river problem

A canoe pointed straight across a moving river does not go straight
across. Its velocity relative to the ground is the vector sum of its
velocity relative to the water and the water's relative to the ground. To
land directly opposite you must point upstream — and the required angle
comes from the vector triangle, not from intuition.

## Why this matters beyond canoes

Aircraft and wind, ships and current, a ball thrown inside a moving bus.
And it sets up the question Unit 5 answers: if every velocity is relative
to a frame, is there anything that is not? Light, it turns out — see
[[Special Relativity]].
