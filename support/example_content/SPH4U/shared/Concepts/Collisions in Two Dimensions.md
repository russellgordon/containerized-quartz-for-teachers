Momentum is a vector, so conservation applies **independently in each
direction**:

$$\sum p_{x,before} = \sum p_{x,after} \qquad \sum p_{y,before} = \sum p_{y,after}$$

That is two equations, and it is the reason two-dimensional collisions are
solvable at all.

## The method

1. Choose axes — usually along the initial motion of one object.
2. Resolve every velocity into components.
3. Write the two conservation equations.
4. Solve, then rebuild the final velocities as magnitude and angle.

## The elastic special case

When a moving object strikes an identical stationary one elastically and
off-centre, the two move away at $90^\circ$ to each other. Every pool
player knows this without knowing why; the why is the two conservation
laws applied together, and it is a satisfying derivation.

## Measuring it

Video of a collision on an air table, tracked frame by frame, gives you
the four velocity vectors — see [[Collisions in Two Dimensions (Lab)]].
Momentum will balance to within a few percent; kinetic energy will not,
and quantifying that gap is the point.
