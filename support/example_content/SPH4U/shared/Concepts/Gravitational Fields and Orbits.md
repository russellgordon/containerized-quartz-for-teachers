An orbit is circular motion whose centripetal force is gravity. Set the
two expressions equal and everything else follows:

$$\frac{Gm_Em}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{Gm_E}{r}}$$

The satellite's own mass cancels — a satellite's orbit does not depend on
how heavy it is, only on how far out it is.

## Higher means slower

Counter-intuitively, a higher orbit is a slower orbit. The International
Space Station at 400 km travels at about 7.7 km/s and circles in 90
minutes; a geostationary satellite at 36 000 km travels at 3.1 km/s and
takes exactly one day — which is what makes it useful.

## Weightlessness

Astronauts are not beyond gravity; at the station's altitude $g$ is still
about 90% of its surface value. They are in free fall, together with their
spacecraft, which is what "weightless" means — see
[[Inertial and Non-inertial Frames]].

## Where GPS comes in

GPS satellites need clocks accurate to nanoseconds, and both special and
general relativity shift those clocks measurably. The correction is
engineered in — physics from Unit 5 doing daily work in Unit 3's orbits.
