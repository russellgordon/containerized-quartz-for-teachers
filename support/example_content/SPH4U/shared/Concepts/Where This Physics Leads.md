Grade 12 physics is a prerequisite course, which means most people meet it
on the way to somewhere. Here is where it actually goes, and some of the
people who built the ideas you are being taught.

## The work this course prepares you for

| Field | What the work is | What it needs from this course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Engineering — aerospace, mechanical, civil | Designing things that must not fail, under loads you predicted | Vectors, dynamics, energy and momentum, and the habit of stating assumptions |
| Engineering physics and photonics | Lasers, sensors, optical fibre, quantum devices | Wave optics, interference, and the quantum unit |
| Medical physics | Imaging and radiation therapy, planned to the millimetre | Fields, energy, modern physics, and uncertainty you can defend |
| Geophysics and remote sensing | Reading the Earth from a distance | Fields, waves, and instrumentation |
| Aviation and air traffic control | Motion in three dimensions, in real time | Kinematics and relative motion, and quick, correct arithmetic |
| Computing and data science | Simulation, modelling, and the physics inside the graphics | Modelling habits more than any single topic |
| Materials and nanotechnology | Making matter behave in a new way | The quantum unit, plus chemistry |
| Nuclear and energy systems | Reactors, grids, and the transition off carbon | Energy, momentum, and nuclear physics |
| Physics research | Asking a question nobody has answered yet | Everything, plus the tolerance for being wrong in public |
| Teaching | Explaining it to the next people | The topics you find hardest now are the ones you will teach best |

Two honest notes. Several of these are college programmes or technologist
routes rather than long degrees, and the work is no less demanding — the
person who aligns a beamline and the person who theorises about it are
both indispensable. And almost every job on that list involves more
writing than students expect, which is why
[[Writing About Physics]] exists and is marked.

## Whose shoulders this stands on

**Elsie MacGill** (1905–1980) — Elizabeth Muriel Gregory MacGill — was
the first woman in the world to earn a degree in aeronautical engineering
and the first to design an aircraft. She led production of the Hawker
Hurricane at Canadian Car and Foundry in Fort William, redesigning the
aircraft for Canadian winters and running a plant of several thousand
people. She did this while walking with crutches after contracting polio,
and she later served on the Royal Commission on the Status of Women.

**Gerhard Herzberg** (1904–1999) fled Germany in 1935 and spent most of
his career at the National Research Council in Ottawa, where his work on
molecular spectroscopy won the 1971 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. His
spectra are how astronomers know what interstellar clouds are made of —
the same emission-line physics you meet in [[Emission Spectra]].

**Allan Carswell** (1933–2023) founded Optech in Toronto and spent
decades building lidar — laser ranging instruments. The lidar on NASA's
Phoenix lander, which observed snow falling on Mars in 2008, was his
group's. He gave away most of the proceeds of the company.

**Donna Strickland**, at the University of Waterloo, shared the 2018 Nobel
Prize in Physics for chirped pulse amplification, developed during her
doctorate. It is the reason a laser can be intense enough for eye surgery
without destroying the equipment that produced it.

**Art McDonald**, at Queen's University, shared the 2015 Nobel Prize for
showing that neutrinos change type as they travel and therefore have
mass — measured two kilometres underground in a nickel mine near Sudbury,
because that much rock was the only way to screen out everything that was
not a neutrino. That result is the sequel to
[[Predicting the Neutrino]], which is where this course meets the
particle first.

> [!note] Why this page is in a physics course rather than a careers talk
> Because ideas have histories, and the histories say something about who
> got to do science. MacGill's degree was granted at a time when several
> Canadian faculties would not admit women at all; Herzberg's career
> happened here because a country he had lived in decided he did not
> belong there. Knowing that is part of understanding how the field
> arrived where it is.

## What to do about it

If a row of that table interests you, the useful next step is not more
reading. It is one conversation with somebody doing the job, and one
piece of hands-on experience — a co-op placement, a lab tour, a summer
research assistantship. Both tell you more in an afternoon than a term of
browsing, and both are more persuasive on an application than a mark.
