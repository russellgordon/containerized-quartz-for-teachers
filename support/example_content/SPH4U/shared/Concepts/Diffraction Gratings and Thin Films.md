Two everyday places the same interference physics shows up.

## Gratings

Thousands of slits instead of two. Each contributes, so the bright maxima
become very sharp and very bright, and different wavelengths separate
cleanly:

$$n\lambda = d\sin\theta$$

That sharpness is why a grating, not a prism, is the instrument of choice
for measuring spectra — including the emission spectra that Unit 5 needs.

## Thin films

Light reflects from both the top and the bottom of a thin layer — oil on
water, a soap bubble, the coating on a camera lens. The two reflections
interfere, and which colours cancel depends on the thickness, which is why
the colours swirl as the film thins.

Anti-reflective coatings are this, engineered: a layer a quarter of a
wavelength thick makes the two reflections cancel, so more light gets
through the lens instead of bouncing back.

## The scale that makes it work

Both effects need path differences comparable to a wavelength — hundreds
of nanometres. That is why a soap film shows colour and a window pane does
not.

## Four ways white light comes apart

Colour separation is the same story told four times: something in the
path depends on wavelength.

| Where you see it | The mechanism | Why the colours separate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A prism, and a rainbow | **Refraction** | The refractive index is slightly larger for blue than for red, so blue bends more |
| A diffraction grating, and a CD | **Diffraction and interference** | The maxima sit where $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, and that angle depends on $\lambda$ |
| A soap film, and an oil slick | **Thin-film interference** | Which wavelength cancels depends on the film's thickness at that point |
| A sunset, and a blue sky | **Scattering** | Short wavelengths scatter far more strongly out of the beam |

**Polarization** belongs on that list too, though it separates
directions rather than colours: reflected glare off water is partly
polarized, which is why polarizing sunglasses remove it and ordinary
tinted ones do not. See [[Polarization]].

> [!question] The one worth arguing about in class
> A rainbow and a CD both split sunlight into a spectrum, but the order
> of the colours in a CD's reflection can reverse between orders while a
> rainbow's never does. Which mechanism explains which, and how would you
> settle it experimentally?
