> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Time:** one class. **Pairs.** Two graphs and a prediction.

Find a spring's constant from a graph, then use it to predict the period
of an oscillation you have not yet timed.

## Safety

A loaded spring stores real energy. Do not stretch past the point where
it stops returning, keep masses low over the bench, and stand clear when
adding the last mass.

## Part 1 — The spring constant

Hang masses in steps, recording extension each time. Plot force against
extension — [[Graphing Data]] has the conventions. The slope is $k$ — and the graph shows you where Hooke's law
stops holding, which a single measurement never would.

## Part 2 — The prediction

From your $k$ and a chosen mass, predict the period using
$T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$. Write it down with an uncertainty. Then time twenty
oscillations and compare.

## Part 3 — The dependence

Predict, then test: what happens to the period if you double the mass?
Quadruple it? Change the amplitude?

## Analysis

1. Was your predicted period within uncertainty of the measured one?
2. Amplitude should not matter. Did it? Below what amplitude did your
   data behave, and what does that tell you about the model's limits?
3. The spring itself has mass. Which way would that bias your period, and
   is the effect visible in your data?
