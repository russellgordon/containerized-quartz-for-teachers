Half this course happens out loud at the whiteboards; the other half
happens when you put reasoning into words someone else can follow.
Writing about mathematics has one rule that does most of the work:

> [!important] Convince a sceptic, not a marker
> Write for a classmate who missed today and doubts you — not for a
> teacher scanning for keywords. If your sceptic could ask "but why is
> that step allowed?", answer it before they can.

## The vocabulary is the toolkit

Mathematical claims come in strengths, and the words signal which one
you are making:

| You are saying… | Say it like this |
| --- | --- |
| I see a pattern but haven't tested it | "I **conjecture** that…" |
| I tested cases and it held | "It **seems to hold because**… (tried 3 cases)" |
| I can say why it must be true | "It **must** be true, **because**…" |
| I found where it breaks | "**Counterexample:** at $x = \pi$…" |

A conjecture that turns out wrong is not an error — it is the engine of
the whole subject. Our class discussions run on them; so does
[[Mistakes Are Data]]. In Grade 12 the top row of that table gets a
promotion: an identity proof is nothing but "it must be true, because"
sustained for a whole page, every line a claim of equality your sceptic
has already had answered — the discipline [[Trigonometric Identities]]
turns into a craft. And when the writing is about a *model*, one more
strength matters: saying where it stops. "This curve fits the first six
hours but should not be trusted past the next dose" is not a weakness
in your work — by the standard of [[What Makes a Model Good]], it is
the most professional sentence on the page.

## Sentence stems that unlock a stuck reflection

- I noticed that… and I wonder whether…
- The step I trust least is… because…
- Another method that should give the same answer is…
- My estimate was… and the difference tells me…

## The finished-solution standard

Before calling written work done, walk [[Showing Your Thinking]]'s
checklist: the question restated, the reasoning visible, units carried
through, and an answer *sentence* — $t = 8.6$ is a value; "the next
dose is safe during the ninth hour" is an answer.

The same standard applies to your [[Math Journal]], with one addition:
name what *you* did and thought, not just what the group did. "We
proved the identity at the board" hides the learning; "I suggested
rewriting everything in sines and cosines, and the two sides finally
started walking toward each other" shows it — and that is the evidence
[[How Marks Work]] actually counts.
