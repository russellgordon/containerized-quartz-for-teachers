## What you are making

A rollercoaster's height profile, seen from the side, is a curve with
a job. Yours is a polynomial $h(x)$ — height in metres above the
ground, $x$ metres along the track — and it must meet four
constraints:

- the ride begins and ends at platform height, $10$ m up;
- it dips below ground into a tunnel exactly once, so the profile
  crosses ground level exactly twice;
- each ground crossing is *gentle* — the profile visibly flattens as
  it crosses, which is something multiplicity can buy you;
- the highest point clears $25$ m.

You finish with the **equation in factored form**, a labelled graph,
a paragraph per constraint pointing at the exact algebra that
enforces it, and two solved questions about your own design: *where
exactly is the ride $15$ m high?* — a polynomial equation — and
*over which stretch is it underground?* — a polynomial inequality.

## Milestones

- [ ] Profile sketched by hand with every constraint marked, before
      any equation exists
- [ ] Zeros chosen, with multiplicities that make the tunnel
      crossings gentle — [[Zeros and Multiplicity]] is the lever
- [ ] Leading coefficient tuned in [[Using Desmos]] until the peak
      clears $25$ m and both ends sit at platform height
- [ ] The $15$ m question solved algebraically — [[The Factor Theorem]]
      earns its keep here — then confirmed on the graph
- [ ] The underground stretch stated as an interval and defended with
      a sign argument

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]], the reasoning is the product: a profile that
misses one constraint, with the miss measured and the fix argued,
outranks a perfect curve that cannot say why it works. On the due
date your pair defends the design at the boards. The [[Math Journal]]
entry on the constraint that fought back hardest completes the
evidence.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| Picture first | A hand sketch with constraints marked, before algebra |
| Algebra that enforces | Each constraint traced to a factor or coefficient |
| Gentle by design | Multiplicity chosen on purpose, and explained |
| Exact where it counts | The $15$ m crossings solved, not read off |
| An honest interval | The tunnel stretch defended with a sign argument |

> [!success]- If the ends will not both sit at $10$ m
> Symmetry is free money: place your tunnel crossings symmetrically
> about the middle of the track and build from factors that mirror
> each other — whatever the left end does, the right end must copy.
> Then remember that your zeros define a whole *family* of coasters;
> one known point pins down the member, and the leading coefficient
> falls out.
