If mathematics class only ever asked you to do things you already
knew how to do, it would be a photocopying service. The learning
happens in the stretch — standing at a whiteboard with your random
group, holding an identity none of you can prove yet. But not all
struggle is equal. *Stuck-and-thinking* means you still have a next
move to try: test both sides at $\frac{\pi}{6}$, rewrite everything
in sines and cosines, shrink the problem to a lower degree.
*Spinning* means repeating the last move harder and hoping. The
first builds mathematicians; the second just builds frustration —
and telling them apart from the inside is a skill this course
teaches on purpose, with [[Getting Unstuck]] as its field guide. One
course from now, nobody hands you the method before the problem;
this room is where you practise not needing them to.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Recall a problem that finally gave in after real struggle. What
   did the moment of giving-in feel like — and would being handed the
   answer have produced anything like it?
2. An identity proof is strange struggle: you can see the destination
   the whole time — the two sides are printed right there — and
   still not have a road. Does knowing where you are going make the
   struggle easier or crueller? Why?
3. Why does this class put you in *random* groups at *vertical*
   whiteboards? What do standing up and a shared marker do to
   struggle that a worksheet at a desk does not?
4. The group beside yours is stuck on a proof you have cracked. When
   does helping them actually take something from them — and what
   does good help look like instead?
5. Where is the line between productive struggle and genuinely being
   out of your depth? Is admitting the second a failure, or a move?

This is why every practice set here folds its answers away and why
nothing is ever timed: the callouts protect your struggle from your
own curiosity. Attempt first, peek second — the order is the whole
point.
