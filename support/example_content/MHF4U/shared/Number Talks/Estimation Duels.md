A quantity goes on the board — a logarithm nobody's calculator is
allowed to touch, the angle the minute hand sweeps in twenty
minutes, how many times louder two speakers are than one — and two
duellists commit to estimates before anyone computes. Each defends a
reason. Then the class brackets: surely too low, surely too high,
squeezed until cornered.

## How we play

1. Commit in writing first. A number without a reason scores nothing.
2. Defend: what did you compare it to, and which way did rounding push you?
3. Bracket as a class, then calculate the reveal.

> [!example]- One duel: $\log_2 100$
> - "More than 6 — because $2^6 = 64$, and 64 falls short of 100."
> - "Less than 7 — because $2^7 = 128$ overshoots. So it is cornered
>   between 6 and 7 before anyone computes anything."
> - "100 looks closer to 128 than to 64, so I want to say 6.8 — but
>   doubling is not a ruler. The *factor* halfway between 64 and 128
>   is $64\sqrt{2} \approx 90$, and 100 is past that. I said 6.7."
> - The reveal: about 6.64. The $\sqrt{2}$ argument had it cornered.

## One variation

Radian duels: how many radians in one full turn of a wheel? Commit
before you compute — a surprising share of every room says $\pi$.
The reveal is a little past 6, and *why* it is exactly $2\pi$ is the
question [[Wrapping the Circle]] hands your group, once
[[Radian Measure]] gives the answer its name.

> [!tip] Bracketing is a life skill
> An answer you cannot bracket is an answer you cannot check. Naming
> "too low" and "too high" first is [[Checking Your Own Work]] done
> in advance — before the mistake instead of after it. Logarithms
> need it most: missing a logarithm by one means missing the quantity
> it measures by a whole factor of the base.
