Every function in this course does something; today's question is
which ones can be *undone*. Your group's list starts easy —
$y = x + 3$, $y = 5x$, $y = x^3$ — and ends with the one that
breaks your toolbox: $y = 2^x$. Solve $2^x = 10$.

## The task

For each function on the list, describe the undoing: the operation
that recovers $x$ from $y$, every time. Then face $2^x = 10$. There
is no button you already own — so trap the answer instead. Between
which whole numbers must it lie? Squeeze the interval: can your
group pin it to one decimal place? Two? Meanwhile, draw the graph of
the undoing function itself — the graph that answers "$2$ to what
power gives this?" for every positive input — without knowing its
name. What is its domain, and what does the graph do near the edge
of that domain?

> [!tip]- Facilitation notes — for the teacher
> The easy undoings buy the key idea cheaply: reflection in
> $y = x$, inputs and outputs trading jobs. Do not rescue the group
> facing $2^x = 10$ — the bracketing *is* the mathematics:
> systematic trial converging on a number that certainly exists.
> When a group demands to know what the number is called, ask what
> they would like to call it, and save the standard name for
> consolidation. Watch for the domain discovery: the undoing accepts
> only positive inputs, because $2^x$ only ever outputs positive
> numbers. Fast groups: is the undoing of $y = x^2$ a function at
> all? Why does $y = 2^x$ dodge that problem?

## What mathematics tends to surface

Inverses as reversal, first: adding undone by subtracting, cubing by
cube-rooting — and each undoing's graph is the original reflected in
$y = x$. Then the honest wall: the undoing of $2^x$ must exist (the
reflection argument proves it), yet it has no name in the room. The
bracketing hunt — $2^3 = 8$, too small; $2^{3.5} \approx 11.3$, too
big — converges on a number that is real, needed, and nameless. That
ache for a name is the point, and [[The Logarithm]] answers it
minutes after the boards come down.

## Where it leads

The name arrives the same day, and the rest of the unit puts it to
work: laws inherited from the exponent laws, equations solved by
undoing, and the scales — Richter, decibels, pH — that measure the
world in exponents. Your bracketing instinct survives too: it is
how you will sanity-check every logarithm a calculator hands you.

> The bracketing, the graph, and the naming all belong to your group
> at the boards — the name only means something after the ache for
