Your group gets string, scissors, and a circle — and a question
older than the degree: if the radius itself were your unit, how far
around the circle would one radius-length of *arc* take you? How
many such steps make a full turn?

## The task

Cut a piece of string exactly one radius long. Walk it around the
circle's edge, marking where each radius-length of arc ends. How
many steps fit in a full turn — and is the count a whole number?
Does it depend on the size of the circle? Test a second circle
before you answer. Then the real work: each step defines a new unit
of angle, one radius-length of arc as seen from the centre. Measure
a right angle in the new unit. A straight angle. Convert $60°$ —
and find the exchange rate between the new unit and degrees.

> [!tip]- Facilitation notes — for the teacher
> Physical string matters: the arc refuses to lie straight in your
> hands, which is exactly the misconception worth confronting — a
> group that lays the string across as a chord gets exactly six
> steps, so let the two counts collide at the boards. Give
> different-sized circles to different groups, so the independence
> of the count from the circle is discovered communally, not
> announced. Do not say "radian" until the count is settled and
> someone asks what the unit is called. Fast groups: how long is
> the arc that an angle of one new-unit cuts from a circle of
> radius $r$? Of radius $2r$?

## What mathematics tends to surface

The count comes out the same for every circle — a little more than
six steps, tantalisingly close to $6.28$ — and someone eventually
halves it and gasps: $\pi$ is in the string. That
circle-independence is the whole point. The radian is not arbitrary
the way the degree is ($360$ was a Babylonian bookkeeping choice);
the circle itself defines it, and the exchange rate — $\pi$ of the
new units make $180°$ — falls out of the full turn.
[[Radian Measure]] makes it official.

## Where it leads

Radians sit under everything in this unit: the special angles become
fractions of $\pi$, the graphs of sine and cosine take their true
period $2\pi$, and Unit 4's rates of change only come out clean
because the radian ties arc to angle with no exchange rate at all.
The measuring you did today is [[The Unit Circle]] in embryo.
