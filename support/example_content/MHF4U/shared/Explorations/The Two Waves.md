Your group gets two waves on one grid — $y_1 = \sin(4x)$ and
$y_2 = \sin(5x)$ — and a single instruction: add them. Not the
formulas; the *graphs*, point by point, until you can draw
$y_1 + y_2$ and say what kind of thing it is.

## The task

Build the sum at the boards: at each marked $x$, stack the two
heights and plot the total. Where do the waves agree and reinforce?
Where do they cancel? Draw enough of the sum to commit to a shape —
then defend the big claims. Is the sum periodic? What is its period?
Is its amplitude even constant? Sketch the slow curve that seems to
hug the sum from above and below, and write its equation if you
dare. Then the musical question: these two waves are two tones,
nearly in tune — what would their sum *sound* like?

> [!tip]- Facilitation notes — for the teacher
> Gridded board space and a shared table of $x$-values keep the
> pointwise addition honest; groups that freehand it miss the
> cancellation entirely. The productive argument is about amplitude:
> "it is $2$" versus "it keeps changing" — both camps are seeing
> something true, and naming the envelope resolves the fight. Play
> the two tones (any online tone generator; $440$ Hz against
> $444$ Hz works) only *after* the boards commit to a shape — the
> room hears its own graph throb. Fast groups: what happens to the
> envelope as the two frequencies move closer together? Further
> apart?

## What mathematics tends to surface

Interference, in both directions: where the waves agree the sum
swells to nearly twice either height, and where they disagree it
dies to almost nothing — and the swelling-and-dying is itself slow
and periodic. The envelope your group sketches is a sinusoid too,
throbbing at *the difference* of the two frequencies — the pulse
musicians call beats. The claim that this messy sum equals a neat
product of two sinusoids is the unit's deepest identity, and
[[Compound Angles]] holds the tools that prove it.

## Where it leads

The identity the envelope demands — a sum of sines rewritten as a
product — is proved and put to work in [[The Sound of Two Waves]],
where your own stopwatch tests it against real sound. And adding
functions point by point returns in Unit 4 as a general move, when
combining functions becomes the whole game.
