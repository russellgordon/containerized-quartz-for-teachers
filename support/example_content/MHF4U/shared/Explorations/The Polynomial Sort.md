Twelve graphs, no equations, no scales. Your group's envelope holds
them all, and the instruction is a single sentence: sort these
graphs into groups, and be ready to defend the rule that built each
group.

## The task

Sort the twelve cards at the boards — every card must land
somewhere, and every pile needs a rule you can say out loud. Then
the pressure: another team will try to break your rule using your
own cards. Push further: which cards could be degree $3$? Which
could not possibly be degree $4$, no matter the scale? And two of
the cards are impostors — not polynomials at all. Which two, and
what gives them away when nothing is labelled?

> [!tip]- Facilitation notes — for the teacher
> Print the cards without axes; scales invite plotting and kill
> arguing. Seed the impostors deliberately: an exponential (its
> asymptote is the tell) and a sinusoid — ask "could a polynomial
> come back down and up again exactly like this, one hundred
> times?". When a group sorts by "smoothness" or "prettiness", ask
> them to point to the exact place where two cards differ; the
> vocabulary of end behaviour and turning points tends to get
> invented within the minute. Groups that finish early can rank one
> pile by "number of hills" and conjecture a degree for each card.

## What mathematics tends to surface

End behaviour first: the arms of every graph point somewhere, and
sorting by where they point is secretly sorting by the degree's
parity and the leading coefficient's sign. Turning points force the
finer cuts — a polynomial of degree $n$ can turn at most $n - 1$
times, and groups discover the bound before they can state it. The
impostors — one graph flattening toward a ceiling, one repeating
forever — draw the family's boundary, and [[Polynomial Functions]]
names what lives inside it.

## Where it leads

Every rule your group defended becomes vocabulary this unit: degree,
end behaviour, turning points, symmetry. The finer question — what
happens *at* a zero — is the very next class, and by the unit's task
you will run today's reasoning in reverse: given the constraints,
design the graph. [[The Rollercoaster]] is the sort, played
backwards.

> [!note] The answer is not on this page
> No finished sort appears here. The rules — and the arguments that
> defend them — belong to your group at the boards.
