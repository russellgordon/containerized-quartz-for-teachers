At the boards, your group slid the two special triangles from Grade 11
inside a circle of radius 1 — and something collapsed pleasantly. With
the hypotenuse equal to 1, "adjacent over hypotenuse" is just
*adjacent*. On the unit circle, an angle $\theta$ measured from the
positive x-axis lands at the point

$$(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)$$

— cosine is the x-coordinate, sine is the y-coordinate, and tangent
is the slope of the arm. Trigonometry stops being about triangles and
becomes about *position on a circle*, which is what lets it describe
things with no triangle in sight: tides, seasons, sound.

## Coordinates, not triangles

The two special triangles give the first-quadrant values for
$\frac{\pi}{6}$, $\frac{\pi}{4}$, and $\frac{\pi}{3}$; the axes
themselves give $0$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$. That is five angles — and
the coordinates repeat around the circle from there. The circle also
explains things triangles never could: $\sin\theta$ can be negative
because a *coordinate* can be negative, which "opposite over
hypotenuse" could never justify.

## Symmetry does the memorising

Every other special angle is one of those five, reflected. The
**reference angle** is the acute angle between the arm and the
x-axis; the reflection changes signs, never sizes. So for
$\frac{7\pi}{6}$: the reference angle is $\frac{\pi}{6}$, the arm is
in the third quadrant where both coordinates are negative, so
$\sin\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{1}{2}$. Two facts — reference angle and
quadrant — replace a table of dozens. As
[[How to Study for Math]] argues, a fact you can rebuild beats a fact
you memorised, because it cannot fall out of your head the night
before an exam.

> [!question]- Self-check: what is $\cos\frac{5\pi}{6}$, exactly?
> $\frac{5\pi}{6}$ is $\frac{\pi}{6}$ short of $\pi$, so the
> reference angle is $\frac{\pi}{6}$ and the arm is in the second
> quadrant, where x-coordinates are negative:
> $\cos\frac{5\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.

These exact values are the raw material for everything in
[[Radian Measure Practice]], and the graphs of $\sin x$ and $\cos x$
in radians are just this circle unrolled — height of the point
against arc travelled, period $2\pi$ because that is one lap.
