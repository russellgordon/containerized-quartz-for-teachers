In [[Wrapping the Circle]], your group laid a string cut to the length
of the radius along the outside of a circle, end over end, and found
it fit a little more than six times — six and a bit, every group,
every circle size. That "six and a bit" is $2\pi$, and the unit you
were inventing is the **radian**: the angle whose arc, measured at
the centre of a circle, is exactly one radius long.

## A unit made of the circle itself

Degrees are a human convention — 360 owes more to Babylonian
astronomy than to circles. A radian is built from the circle's own
anatomy: arc length over radius. That makes it a pure ratio, no unit
symbol required, and it is why the formula $s = r\theta$ for arc
length works only in radians — the definition *is* the formula,
rearranged. Since the full circumference is $2\pi r$, a full turn is
$2\pi$ radians, and the conversion follows:

$$\pi \text{ radians} = 180°$$

Both faces of a radian measure are legitimate: exact multiples of
$\pi$ like $\frac{\pi}{3}$, and plain decimals like $1.05$ — the same
angle, dressed for different jobs. A radian is about $57.3°$:
noticeably bigger than a degree, and worth having a feel for before
[[Estimation Duels]] asks you to guess one.

## Converting and estimating

Multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180°}$ to enter radians, by $\frac{180°}{\pi}$
to leave. The special angles are worth knowing by heart in both
languages — not by memorising twelve facts, but by anchoring on
"$\pi$ is half a turn" and taking fractions of it:

| Degrees | $30°$           | $45°$           | $60°$           | $90°$           | $180°$ | $360°$ |
| ------- | --------------- | --------------- | --------------- | --------------- | ------ | ------ |
| Radians | $\frac{\pi}{6}$ | $\frac{\pi}{4}$ | $\frac{\pi}{3}$ | $\frac{\pi}{2}$ | $\pi$  | $2\pi$ |

From here on, this course speaks radians: graphs, equations, and your
calculator, which needs its mode switched *today* — a calculator in
degree mode answering a radian question is the quietest wrong answer
in mathematics. [[Radian Measure Practice]] builds the fluency, and
[[The Unit Circle]] puts the special angles to work.
