In [[The Reciprocal]], your group graphed a function and its
reciprocal on the same axes and hunted for the choreography: where
$f$ is large, $\frac{1}{f}$ hugs zero; where $f$ crosses zero,
$\frac{1}{f}$ has nowhere to go and blows up. A **rational function**
is any ratio of polynomials, and everything strange about them — the
breaks, the gaps, the forbidden inputs — comes from that one fact:
somewhere, a denominator might be zero.

## Built from division

The reciprocal of a line, like $f(x) = \frac{1}{x+2}$, inherits its
whole personality from the line: a vertical asymptote where the line
crosses zero, the same sign as the line everywhere, and outputs that
shrink toward zero as the line grows. The reciprocal of a quadratic,
like $\frac{1}{x^2 - 4}$, does the same with two crossings, so two
vertical asymptotes. With a linear numerator *and* denominator, like
$\frac{x-2}{3x+4}$, you get one x-intercept from the top, one
asymptote from the bottom, and end behaviour that settles at the
ratio of the leading coefficients. In every case the sketch follows
from reading the two polynomials separately — [[Asymptotes]] covers
the reading in detail.

## Solving rational equations

The real roots of a rational equation are the x-intercepts of its
graph — and only the numerator can produce them. $\frac{x-2}{x-3}=0$
has the single root $x = 2$; $\frac{1}{x-3} = 0$ has no roots at all,
which is exactly why that graph never touches the x-axis. For
messier equations, multiply through by the denominator and solve the
polynomial that remains — then audit.

> [!warning] A root you must refuse
> Multiplying by the denominator can manufacture solutions that make
> the original denominator zero. Every candidate goes back into the
> *original* equation before it earns the name "solution". A checked
> answer is an answer; an unchecked one is a rumour — the habit from
> [[Checking Your Own Work]], with real consequences now.
