Your mark reflects what you can do with civic evidence and civic
reasoning. It does not reflect which positions you hold — see
[[Our Working Agreement]], where that promise is made in full.

## What this course is

Civics and Citizenship is a **half credit**: 55 hours of scheduled
instructional time, which here is 42 periods plus a two-and-a-half-hour
final evaluation. It runs from **early November to mid-January**, picking
up the timetable slot Career Studies had for the first half of the
semester.

Ten weeks is not long. It is why the notebook starts in week one and why
almost nothing in this course is a review period.

## What earns a high mark here

- **A claim with a source attached.** A dated fact somebody else can
  check beats a confident sentence every time.
- **The other side, at its strongest.** Stating the best version of a
  position you reject, then answering it, is the clearest single signal
  of a strong civics answer. It appears in the criteria of most tasks in
  this course.
- **Precision about who holds power.** "The government" loses marks;
  naming the order, the body, and the instrument earns them.
- **Honesty about uncertainty and about outcomes.** "The evidence does
  not settle this, and here is what would" is worth marks, not lost ones.
  So is a civic action that did not work, reported accurately.
- **Your own judgement.** Every assessed piece ends in a position. A
  summary of an issue, however accurate, is not an answer.

## Late work, and revision

Talk to me before the deadline and we set a new one. Work that arrives
without that conversation is still marked, but in a ten-week course the
feedback reaches you too late to use, which is the real cost.

[[The Rights Case]] can be revised after feedback, because on a rights
argument the revision is the skill being assessed.

> [!tip] The criteria live on the task page
> Every task ends with a table of what earns the marks for that specific
> piece of work. Read it before you start, not after you finish — and if
> a criterion is unclear, ask, because that question benefits everybody.
