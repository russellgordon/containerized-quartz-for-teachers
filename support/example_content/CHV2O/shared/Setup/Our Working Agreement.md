This course studies questions that Canadians vote differently about.
Housing, immigration, policing, taxation, climate policy, language,
religion in public life, treaty obligations, what a right is for. On every
one of those, this room contains people who disagree, and their families
disagree even more.

That is not a hazard to be managed around. It is the material. What
follows is how we handle it.

## How we argue about public questions

- **Evidence, not assertion.** You are entitled to your own view and not
  to your own facts. Name your source when you state one, and accept
  being asked for it without treating the question as an attack.
- **Disagree with the position, not the person.** "That claim comes from
  the industry it exonerates" is an argument. "You would say that" is
  not.
- **State the other side at its strongest.** If you can only describe an
  opposing view in a form its holders would reject, you do not yet
  understand it. This is marked in this course, repeatedly.
- **Let people finish**, especially when you are certain they are wrong,
  and **change your mind out loud** when the evidence moves you. Nobody
  here loses standing for that. Several people gain it.
- **Nobody speaks for a group they belong to.** You are never the class
  representative of your background, your religion, your family's
  politics, or your nation.

## What you never have to disclose

**How your household votes is private, and it stays private.** You will
not be asked, and you should not ask a classmate. If someone volunteers
it, that is their choice and it does not become a fact about them that
gets used in the next argument.

You are also not required to hold a position on every issue. "I have not
worked out what I think about this yet" is an honest answer and, in
several of the questions we study, the most defensible one.

> [!important] Nothing in this course is graded on your politics
> Not one mark in this course depends on which position you take. They
> depend on the evidence you bring, the accuracy of your account of the
> other side, and whether your reasoning holds. A well-argued conservative
> answer and a well-argued progressive answer to the same question earn
> the same mark, and I will show you the criteria that make that true.
>
> If you ever think a mark reflected my views rather than your work, say
> so — to me, or to the department head if that is easier. That is a
> reasonable thing to ask about, and asking it is itself a civic skill.

## What I commit to

- **I do not campaign here.** I will explain what parties propose, what
  the evidence shows, and what the strongest arguments on each side are.
  I will not tell you how to vote, and I will answer "what do you think,
  sir?" by explaining why I am not going to answer it.
- **Facts are not treated as opinions.** Neutrality between parties is
  not neutrality between a claim with evidence and one without. Where a
  factual question has a well-supported answer, I will say so and show
  you the support.
- **Advance notice** before lessons touching residential schools,
  genocide, racism, or violence, in time for you to prepare or arrange
  something else.
- **Nobody is put on the spot** as the representative of a community, and
  no example is chosen to make a particular student uncomfortable.

If something we study is close to home, [[Getting Help]] lists the routes
out, including the ones that are not me.
