A bill is a proposed law, published in full while it is still being
argued about, and it is the single most useful primary source in this
course. You can read the exact words a government wants to put into force,
watch them change, and see who objected — all before it becomes law.

## The stages, federally

The House of Commons publishes its own account of the process, and the
sequence is fixed. **First reading** introduces the bill and prints it;
there is no debate. **Second reading** debates the principle of the bill —
whether it should exist at all — and a vote here is the one that matters
most politically. **Committee stage** goes through it clause by clause,
hears witnesses, and amends. **Report stage** takes further amendments in
the House. **Third reading** debates the bill in its final form. Then the
bill goes to the **Senate**, which runs its own readings and committee
work and can amend, sending it back. Finally **royal assent** is given by
the Governor General, and the bill becomes an Act — coming into force on
that date, or on a date set in the Act itself, or when Cabinet proclaims
it.

## And in Ontario

The Legislative Assembly's process is the same shape with one chamber
fewer: first reading with no debate, second reading on the principle,
committee for clause-by-clause study and witnesses, third reading on the
final form, then royal assent from the Lieutenant Governor. An Act
becomes law when it comes into force, which may be immediately or later.

Two practical consequences. A bill that passes third reading is not yet
law. And an Act that has received royal assent may still not be in force
— the commonest error in student work on this topic, and it is checkable
in thirty seconds.

## Reading one

Every bill carries the same furniture, and each part answers a question.

| Part | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Long title | The bill's stated purpose, in the government's own framing |
| Summary | A plain-language description, written by the drafters and not neutral |
| Preamble | Why they say it is needed — often the most quotable part |
| Clauses | What actually changes; watch for "is amended by striking out" |
| Coming into force | When any of it starts to matter |

Read the clauses before the summary. The summary is advocacy; the clauses
are the law.

> [!tip] Where to find them
> Federal bills, with their full text at every stage, votes, and
> committee evidence, are at parl.ca under LEGISinfo. Ontario bills are
> at ola.org, and Ontario's laws in force are at ontario.ca/laws. All
> three are free, all three are the actual documents, and none of them
> requires an account.

A bill also tells you who is against it and why, because Hansard records
the debate and committees publish witness testimony. That is often better
evidence of the range of perspectives on an issue than any news report
about it, and you can quote it.
