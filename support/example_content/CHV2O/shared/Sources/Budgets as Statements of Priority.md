A government's speeches tell you what it wants to be seen caring about.
Its budget tells you what it is actually paying for. When the two
disagree, the budget is the better evidence, because it commits money and
the speech does not.

## Where the money comes from

Different orders of government raise revenue in different ways, and this
is why they behave differently under pressure.

| Order | Main revenue sources |
| --- | --- |
| Federal | Personal and corporate income tax, GST, customs duties, employment insurance premiums |
| Provincial | Income tax, sales tax, health premiums and payroll levies, resource royalties, alcohol and gaming, federal transfers |
| Municipal | Property tax, user fees, development charges, provincial and federal grants |
| Indigenous governments | Own-source revenue, taxation powers under agreements, treaty entitlements, federal transfers |

Municipalities are the constrained ones. Property tax does not rise when
the economy grows the way income tax does, most Ontario municipalities
cannot run an operating deficit, and their largest costs — roads, water
pipes, transit — are long-lived assets. That is why a municipality talks
about grants so often: for anything large, it is asking someone else.

## Short term against long term

Every budget is doing two jobs at once. It funds this year — salaries,
services, the things that stop working immediately if unfunded. And it
funds plans whose payoff arrives after the next election: a transit line,
a water treatment plant, a research program, debt paid down.

The tension is structural, not a failure of character. Spending on
long-term assets means less now, and the people who benefit may not be
the people paying. Watch for the two ways governments manage it: capital
budgets kept separate from operating budgets, and multi-year plans that
commit successors who did not agree.

## How to read one without an accounting course

1. **Find the total, and the change from last year.** A percentage change
   tells you more than a large number.
2. **Find the biggest three line items.** In most provinces those are
   health, education, and debt servicing. Everything argued about in
   public is usually smaller than all three.
3. **Compare a line to last year's figure for the same line**, not to
   what a press release calls an increase. An announced increase spread
   over five years is one fifth of an increase.
4. **Distinguish committed from announced.** Money in the estimates has
   been voted. Money in a plan has not.
5. **Ask who is not in it.** A program with no line has no money,
   whatever the accompanying document says about its importance.

Federal budgets and estimates are at canada.ca and parl.ca; Ontario's are
at ontario.ca; municipal budgets are published by the municipality and
are usually the easiest of the three to read.

Then draw your conclusion out loud: name one priority this budget states
in words and one it states in dollars, and say whether they match. That
sentence — a judgement, supported by two figures — is what
[[The Issue Examination]] asks you to be able to write in ten minutes.
