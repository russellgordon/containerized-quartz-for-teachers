A court decision is the only civic document written by someone required to
explain their reasoning, in public, in a form that can be checked and
appealed. That makes it a strange and valuable source: the argument is the
document, and the losing side's case is set out in it too.

## What you are holding

Under the *Supreme Court Act* the Supreme Court of Canada sits nine
judges, appointed by the federal Cabinet, of whom three must come from
Quebec — a requirement of Canada's two legal systems, common law and
Quebec's civil law. Below it sit provincial appeal courts, superior
courts, and provincial courts, plus the Federal Court and specialised
tribunals. A decision binds the courts beneath the one that made it; a
Supreme Court decision binds every court in the country.

Canadian decisions carry a neutral citation — the year, the court, and a
number, as in *2015 SCC 5*. It identifies the decision independently of
any publisher, and it is what a marker will look for.

## The anatomy

**Style of cause** — the parties, in the form *Appellant v. Respondent*,
which tells you who brought the case and who is defending.

**Headnote** — a summary written by an editor, not by the court. Useful
for orientation and never quotable as the court's words.

**Facts** — what the court accepts happened. This is often the most
readable part and the part students skip.

**Issues** — the questions the court says it must answer. If your essay
is about a question not listed here, you are writing about a different
case.

**Reasons** — the argument. Majority reasons decide the case.
Concurring reasons agree with the result for different reasons. Dissents
lose, and are still worth reading: they set out the strongest case
against, written by a judge, and they sometimes become the majority
decades later.

**Disposition** — who won, and what happens now. Frequently the case goes
back to a lower court, which means nobody has finally won anything yet.

> [!success]- What is a decision evidence of, and what is it not?
> It is evidence of what the law is, of how a right was applied to a
> specific set of facts, and of the arguments both sides thought were
> their best. It is not evidence that the outcome was fair, that the
> underlying policy is wise, or that the affected community agrees. A
> court answers the question put to it, on the record put before it — and
> a party who could not afford to put a record before it does not appear
> in the reasons at all.

## Where to read them

Supreme Court of Canada judgments are free at scc-csc.ca, with plain
language case summaries alongside the full reasons. CanLII carries
decisions from every Canadian court and tribunal at no charge. Both are
searchable, and both give you the neutral citation you need to cite.

Read the summary first, then the facts, then the issues — and only then
the reasons. Going straight to the reasoning of a case whose facts you do
not know is the fastest way to misunderstand it.
