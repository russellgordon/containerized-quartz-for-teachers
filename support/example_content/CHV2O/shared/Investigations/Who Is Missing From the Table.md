Pick a body that decides something affecting you, and find out who is not
on it. This is a counting investigation before it is an argument, and the
counting is what makes the argument hard to dismiss.

## The question

**Whose beliefs, values and perspectives are represented in this
decision-making body, and whose are not — and what would it take to close
the gap?**

## Choosing a body

It has to make decisions, meet on a schedule, and publish something. Good
candidates: your school's student council; a school board and its
committees; a municipal council or one of its advisory committees; a
library or transit board; a community organisation's board; a youth
council; a residents' or tenants' association.

Avoid anything where you cannot obtain a membership list, and avoid
choosing a body in order to embarrass a specific person. The finding
should survive the individuals currently sitting on it.

## What to establish

1. **Who sits on it**, and how each got there — elected, appointed,
   ex officio, self-selected.
2. **Who was eligible** to be there, and who was not. Eligibility rules
   do more sorting than any election.
3. **Who is affected** by its decisions. Get a figure if one exists —
   census profiles, board enrolment data, and municipal open-data
   portals are all public.
4. **What the meetings ask of a member** — time of day, frequency,
   travel, unpaid hours, language, technology, whether childcare exists.
5. **Who spoke** at the last three meetings, from the minutes.

Points 4 and 5 are usually where the answer is. Most exclusion in Canada
is not a rule saying you may not join. It is a Tuesday afternoon meeting,
in one language, with no pay, that assumes you have a car.

> [!important] People, not categories
> You are describing a body, not auditing individuals, and you are not
> entitled to assign anyone an identity they have not claimed. Work from
> what is public — roles, rules, minutes, attendance, published
> demographics of the affected population. Do not speculate about a named
> person's background, and do not ask a classmate to represent a group.
> If your evidence would embarrass a real person rather than describe a
> structure, you have the wrong evidence.

## The argument you build

State the gap plainly, with your figures. Then answer the harder half:
does the gap matter for the decisions this body actually makes? Sometimes
it demonstrably does — a decision was made that the missing group would
have flagged. Sometimes you cannot show that, and saying so is stronger
than pretending you can.

Finish with two changes that would close the gap, and be honest about
what each costs and who would have to agree.

how you check your figures, and this feeds directly into
