Most civic frustration in Canada comes from complaining to the wrong
government. The pothole, the hospital wait, the visa, the transit fare and
the criminal sentence are decided in four different buildings, and a
letter sent to the wrong one is answered politely and filed.

The *Constitution Act, 1867* divided powers between two orders of
government — federal and provincial — and neither may simply take over
the other's field. Territories exercise powers delegated by Parliament.
Municipalities are created by their province, under Ontario's *Municipal
Act, 2001* and the *City of Toronto Act, 2006*, which means the province
can change what a municipality may do. Indigenous governments are
described in [[Indigenous Governance]]; their authority comes from
section 35, from treaties, and from negotiated agreements, not from a
delegation by any of the others.

## Who holds what

| Order | Decides, among other things | Who leads it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Federal | Criminal law, immigration and citizenship, defence, currency, postal service, First Nations matters under the *Indian Act*, most trade | Prime Minister and Cabinet |
| Provincial | Health care delivery, education, highways, most natural resources, property and civil rights, municipalities | Premier and Cabinet |
| Territorial | Much of what a province does, under powers delegated by Parliament | Premier, with a legislature; some use consensus government rather than parties |
| Municipal | Local roads, water, waste, transit, fire, zoning, parks, libraries | Mayor and council, elected every four years |
| Indigenous | Varies by nation and agreement — membership, land, education, child welfare, justice, resource decisions | Elected, hereditary, or both, depending on the nation |

Shared fields are the normal case, not the exception. Housing involves a
federal program, provincial legislation, a municipal zoning decision, and
often an Indigenous government's jurisdiction over its own land. When you
hear one order blame another, the first job is to find out who actually
holds the power in question.

## Three branches, inside each government

**The legislative branch** makes law — the House of Commons and Senate
federally, the Legislative Assembly in Ontario. **The executive branch**
carries it out: the Prime Minister or Premier, Cabinet, and the public
service, which is where most decisions that affect you are actually made.
**The judicial branch** interprets law and settles disputes, including
disputes about whether the other two branches stayed within the
Constitution.

In a parliamentary system the first two overlap on purpose: Cabinet
ministers sit in the legislature and are answerable to it daily. The
courts do not overlap with either, and that separation is what makes it
possible for a court to rule against the government that appointed the
judge.

That arrangement is doing more than tidying responsibilities. It is why a
change of government does not change who owns your house, why a law
passed in a hurry can still be struck down, and why the public service
keeps paying pensions through an election. Stability is the product; the
division of powers is the mechanism.

where a decision belongs in [[Who Should Decide Locally?]].
