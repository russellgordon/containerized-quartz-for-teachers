Every community says it represents everyone in it. The useful question is
not whether it means to, but whether you can check. Representation is a
claim about a real thing, and real things leave evidence.

## Four places to look

**Who is in the room where it is decided.** Councils, boards, committees
and consultations all keep records of who attends and who speaks. A
consultation held at two in the afternoon on a weekday has already chosen
its participants before anyone arrives.

**Who is asked, and how.** A survey that runs online only, in one
language, for eleven days, has a sample. It is not a cross-section, and
anyone reporting the result as "the community said" is overstating it.

**Who is named afterwards.** Read a report on a project and count who
appears as a decision-maker, who appears as a stakeholder, and who
appears as an affected population. Those three words describe three very
different amounts of power.

**What gets commemorated.** Street names, statues, plaques, school names,
holidays and land acknowledgements are all statements about whose story
the place belongs to.[^ack] Commemoration is cheap compared with
jurisdiction, which is exactly why arguments about it get heated: a
plaque can be a real recognition, and it can also be what gets offered
instead of a decision.

[^ack]: A land acknowledgement is a statement, not an agreement. Whether
it recognises anything depends on what the same institution does about
treaty obligations, which is why so many Indigenous organisations ask to
be judged on the second rather than the first.

## Pride in a country, honestly

Most Canadians report pride in the country, and there are defensible
reasons: public health care, comparative political stability, the
Charter, peaceful transfers of power for over a century and a half.
Pride is also what makes people willing to be taxed for strangers and to
accept a result they voted against, which no democracy can do without.

The same country ran residential schools, interned Japanese Canadians and
Ukrainian Canadians, refused Jewish refugees in the 1930s, and enforced
segregation. Both records belong to the same state, and neither cancels
the other. An account of Canada that holds only one of them is not
patriotism or criticism — it is just incomplete, and a reader can tell.

The test worth applying to a commemoration, a curriculum, or a national
holiday is whether a person whose family lived the harder half would
recognise themselves in it.

## What to do with this

Inclusion is measurable, so measure it. Pick one body that makes
decisions affecting you — a student council, a school board, a municipal
committee, a community organisation — and establish who is on it, who
chose them, who was eligible, and who among the affected population is
absent. Then ask what would have to change for that absence to close:
meeting times, notice, language, childcare, transport, a seat that does
not exist yet.

That investigation is [[Who Is Missing From the Table?]], and it is
assessed in [[Whose Voice Is Missing]].
