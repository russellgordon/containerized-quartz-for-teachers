First Nations, Métis and Inuit governments are governments. They pass
laws, run programs, hold elections, raise and spend money, negotiate with
Ottawa and with Queen's Park, and appear in court as governments. This
page is about how they work now — not about how they once worked, and not
as a comparison in which one system is the real one.

Section 35 of the *Constitution Act, 1982* recognises and affirms the
existing Aboriginal and treaty rights of the Aboriginal peoples of
Canada, and names those peoples as the Indian, Inuit and Métis peoples of
Canada. That is constitutional text, not policy, which means no
legislature can repeal it by an ordinary vote.

## Three kinds of arrangement, all in force at once

**Councils under the *Indian Act*.** The Act, first passed in 1876 and
still on the books as R.S.C. 1985, c. I-5, imposed a chief-and-council
model on First Nations and set the rules for electing it. Many First
Nations still hold elections under the Act; others have moved to the
*First Nations Elections Act*, and a large number govern under their own
community election codes. The council is real government — it delivers
services, passes bylaws and signs agreements — and it was imposed, both
at the same time.

**Governments that predate the Act, or run alongside it.** Confederacies,
clan systems and hereditary structures were governing before Canada
existed and did not stop. In some communities such a council and an
*Indian Act* council both operate, with a live disagreement about which
speaks for the nation. When you read reporting on a dispute in a First
Nation, that is often the disagreement underneath it.

**Self-government agreements and modern treaties.** Crown-Indigenous
Relations and Northern Affairs Canada reports 25 self-government
agreements involving 43 Indigenous communities, plus 2 education
agreements covering 35 communities — among them the Anishinabek Nation
Education Agreement here in Ontario. These are negotiated, not granted,
and each is different. In Nunavut, created 1 April 1999 out of the
Nunavut Agreement, Inuit self-government is expressed through a public
government serving every resident of the territory — an arrangement that
exists nowhere else in Canada.

> [!important] Do not generalise from one nation to all of them
> There are hundreds of First Nations in Canada, four Inuit regions, and
> Métis governments with their own registries and elected leadership.
> Their governing structures are not variations on one design. If your
> work says "Indigenous governments do X", it is almost certainly wrong.
> Name the nation, name where you read it, and prefer that nation's own
> published material — most publish their constitutions, election codes
> and council minutes.

## Treaties are the relationship, not the history unit

More than 40 treaties and other land agreements cover Ontario, according
to the province's own treaties map. They range from the Upper Canada
purchases of the 1780s to the Robinson-Superior and Robinson-Huron
treaties of September 1850, the North-West Angle Treaty in 1873, the
James Bay Treaty in 1905–06 with its later adhesions, and the Williams
Treaties of 1923. Wherever your school stands, you are on land covered by
one of them, or by a claim about one.

A treaty is an agreement between the Crown and a nation, with obligations
running both ways and no expiry date. Some of those obligations — annual
payments, harvesting rights, the size of a reserve — have been litigated
for decades, and courts continue to rule on what the Crown promised and
whether it delivered. Treaty questions are current legal questions, which
is why they appear in [[Who Decided This?]] and in
[[How Government Works]].

Find which treaty covers your community, read it, and find out which
nation's government is party to it. That is the starting point for
everything else here.
