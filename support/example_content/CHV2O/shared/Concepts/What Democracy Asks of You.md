Voting is the part everyone names first. In Canada you get three of them —
federal, provincial, municipal — and municipal elections come round every
four years, on a Monday in October, under the *Municipal Elections Act,
1996*. Done thoroughly, marking three ballots takes about half an hour a
decade. If that were the whole of democratic citizenship, the system would
rest on almost nothing.

What it actually asks is less scheduled and harder: that enough people
notice what is being decided, work out a position they could defend, and
put it somewhere it can be heard.

## Noticing is the first skill

A civic issue is a question a community has to settle together, because
settling it privately is not available. Whether you personally recycle is
a choice. Whether the town runs a collection is an issue. The test is not
how strongly people feel about it — it is whether the answer gets applied
to everyone once it is decided.

Issues sit at three scales at once, and the scale decides who you would
have to persuade: **local** — where a bus stops, whether a building goes
up, how a school board spends a grant; **national** — immigration levels,
criminal law, what the federal budget funds; **global** — emissions, the
treatment of refugees, whether a trade agreement should be signed. Most
issues that reach you are all three at once. A closed factory is a
family's income, a provincial employment file, and a trade negotiation.

## The same issue looks different from different places

This is the part that separates civics from opinion. On any live issue,
the groups involved are not disagreeing because some of them have not
heard the facts. They usually want different things, or carry different
risks from the same decision.

Take a proposal to build housing on a piece of open land. People who
cannot find a place to live, people who already own nearby, people who
use the land now, the municipality that would service it, and the nation
whose treaty covers it are not one public with one view. Describing an
issue means describing that spread — accurately enough that a person in
each group would say you had understood them.

> [!success]- Try this before you read on
> Name one issue in your community where you know two groups disagree.
> Can you state the *stronger* side of the view you do not hold, in a
> sentence its holders would accept? If not, you know the issue as a
> slogan, not as an issue.

## Why anyone bothers

People engage for reasons that repeat across a century of Canadian
examples: because a decision harmed them, because they were excluded from
making it, because they were asked, or because they thought it was wrong
and could not let it stand. The suffrage campaigners who won the
provincial vote in Manitoba in 1916, the unemployed men who rode the
freight cars east on the On-to-Ottawa Trek in 1935, Viola Desmond
refusing to move in a New Glasgow theatre in 1946 — none acted because
participation is a virtue. They acted because something specific was at
stake. Civic action is normally a response to something, and it does not
require you to be an unusually good person first.
