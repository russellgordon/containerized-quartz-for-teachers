Canadians agree on the list. Democracy, human rights, freedom, the rule of
law — almost nobody argues against any of them, which is why saying you
support them tells a listener nothing. The arguments in this country are
almost never between people who hold these values and people who do not.
They are between people who hold all four and have to decide which one
gives ground this time.

## The four, and where each came from

**Democracy** means the people governed choose those who govern, and can
remove them. **Human rights** means some things may not be done to a
person even by a government the majority elected. **Freedom** means the
state must justify its interference rather than assume it. **The rule of
law** means the same law binds the government that binds you, and that
what the law is can be known in advance.

None arrived at once. Magna Carta in 1215 established that a king was
subject to law — a bargain among barons, not a grant of rights to
ordinary people, and worth saying plainly. The *British North America
Act, 1867*, now titled the *Constitution Act, 1867*, built a Parliament
on the British model. The *Canadian Bill of Rights* in 1960 wrote rights
into federal statute, where an ordinary Act of Parliament could override
them. The *Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms* in 1982 put them into
the Constitution, where they bind every legislature in the country.

## Where they collide

| When this value | Pulls against this one | The argument sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Democracy | Human rights | A majority voted for it, so a court should not strike it down |
| Freedom | The common good | My business, my property, my choice — against a rule that protects others |
| The rule of law | Urgency | The proper process takes eighteen months and people are being harmed now |
| Free expression | Equality | Speech that degrades a group is still speech |
| Transparency | Privacy | The public paid for it, so the public should see it |

A position on a civic issue is, underneath, a ranking of these under
pressure. Two classmates can both value freedom and equality and disagree
completely because they rank them differently on one question. That is a
real disagreement about values, and calling it ignorance ends the
conversation without settling it.

## Values as a target

Because democratic values are held rather than enforced, they can be
worked on from outside. Foreign governments and organisations have run
influence operations against other democracies: buying advertising
without disclosing who paid, running networks of fake accounts, funding
groups already active on a divisive issue, and pushing manipulated audio
and video of politicians. Canada's own institutions treat this as an
ongoing risk — Elections Canada and the federal security agencies publish
on it, and Parliament has held public inquiries into foreign interference
in Canadian elections.

The aim is not usually to make you vote a particular way. It is cheaper
to make you believe the count was rigged, that every side is corrupt, and
that nothing you do matters — because a citizen who believes that
withdraws, and withdrawal costs the operation nothing to maintain.

Which is why [[Judging Civic Information]] is a civics skill and not a
media-studies one, and why your own position, written down in
[[Where You Stand Now]], should say which values it ranks and why.
