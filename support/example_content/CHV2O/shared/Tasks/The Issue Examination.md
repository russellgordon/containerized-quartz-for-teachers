**The final evaluation. Two and a half hours, in the examination period.**
Individual. Sources you have not seen before.

A dossier arrives on a civic issue you have not studied. Nothing in it
requires you to have memorised anything. Everything in it requires you to
do what this course has been practising since November.

## What you are given

Six to eight short sources on one issue: a clause or two from a bill, an
extract from a budget, a passage from a court decision or a tribunal
ruling, a government news release, two or three media items including at
least one from social media, and a statement from an affected group. They
will disagree with each other, and at least one of them will be
unreliable in a way you are expected to notice.

## The three parts

1. **Assess the sources** — about 40 minutes. For each: who produced it,
   when, for whom, and what it is evidence of. Identify which sources
   are more and less credible, and say why in terms of the source rather
   than in terms of whether you like its conclusion. Name any bias you
   find, and say what it would take to check the claim.
2. **Analyse the issue** — about 60 minutes. Using the sources, explain
   what is at stake, who is affected, which governments hold the relevant
   powers, and where the positions genuinely conflict. Use the concepts
   of political thinking to reason with, not as vocabulary to display:
   significance, perspective, stability and change, objectives and
   results.
3. **Judge, and reflect** — about 30 minutes. A question is posed that
   the dossier does not settle. Answer it with your reasoning and say
   what further evidence you would want. Then a short reflection: name
   two skills this course built that you expect to use outside it, and
   describe a situation where you would use one.

The remaining twenty minutes are for reading and planning. Use them —
the strongest papers every year spend the first ten minutes not writing.

## What is being examined

| Criterion | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| Credibility | Provenance and purpose established fast, bias named specifically |
| Use of sources | The sources are used against each other, not listed one by one |
| Concepts | The four concepts do reasoning work in your answer |
| Judgement | A defensible position with its uncertainty stated |
| Communication | Written for a reader, with sources referred to precisely |
| Transferable skills | The reflection is concrete about a real situation |

## Preparing for it

The last three periods before the examination work old dossiers against
the clock. Bring [[Your Civic Notebook]] to them: the weekly
current-events entries are the best preparation there is, and rereading
your November entries is the fastest way to see how much sharper you have
become.

> [!tip] Establish who made a source before you read it for content
> Producer, date, purpose — thirty seconds per source, before you read a
> word for meaning. It is the same habit as
> [[Judging Civic Information]], and every year it separates the strong
> papers from the weak ones more reliably than anything else.
