## Purpose

Show that the **angle** at which light strikes a surface changes how much it
heats — the mechanism behind seasons.

## Materials

- Lamp with a bare bulb, thermometer or temperature probe
- Dark card, protractor, ruler, clamp stand

## Procedure

1. Fix the lamp at a constant distance from the card — 20 cm works.
2. Set the card at $90^\circ$ to the beam. Record temperature every 30 s for
   5 minutes.
3. Let the card cool to room temperature.
4. Repeat at $60^\circ$, then $30^\circ$.
5. Keep distance, bulb, and starting temperature identical.

> [!warning] The variable that ruins this lab
> If the card starts warm from the previous trial, the results are meaningless.
> Wait for room temperature every time, and record the starting value to prove
> you did.

## Analysis

1. Graph all three trials on one set of axes.
2. Which angle warmed fastest? Explain in terms of energy spread over area.
3. Toronto's noon Sun is about $70^\circ$ above the horizon in June and about
   $23^\circ$ in December. Connect that to your results.
4. Why is the *hottest* part of summer in late July rather than at the June
   solstice, when sunlight is most direct? See [[Astronomical Phenomena]].
