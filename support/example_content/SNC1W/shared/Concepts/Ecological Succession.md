## The idea

Leave bare ground alone and life arrives in a predictable order, each stage
changing conditions in ways that make the next stage possible.

```mermaid
graph LR
    A["Bare rock"] --> B["Lichens and mosses"]
    B --> C["Grasses and weeds"]
    C --> D["Shrubs"]
    D --> E["Fast-growing trees"]
    E --> F["Climax community"]
```

- **Primary succession** starts where there is no soil at all — new volcanic
  rock, a retreating glacier. It is slow, because soil has to be built first.
- **Secondary succession** starts where soil survives — after a fire, a flood,
  or an abandoned farm field. Much faster.

> [!note] Each stage builds the next one's habitat
> Lichens crumble rock into grit. Their dead bodies add organic matter. That
> mixture is soil, and soil is what a grass seed needs. The pioneers make
> themselves obsolete.

## The honest complication

"Climax community" suggests a final, permanent state. Ecologists have grown
cautious about that word — disturbance is normal, and many ecosystems never
settle. Treat it as a useful simplification rather than a law.
