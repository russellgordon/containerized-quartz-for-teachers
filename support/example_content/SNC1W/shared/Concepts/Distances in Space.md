## The idea

Space is mostly space. Ordinary units fail immediately, so astronomers use units
scaled to what they are measuring.

| Unit | Length | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Astronomical unit (AU) | $1.50 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{km}$ | Within the solar system |
| Light-year (ly) | $9.46 \times 10^{12}\ \mathrm{km}$ | Between stars |
| Parsec (pc) | $3.26\ \mathrm{ly}$ | Professional astronomy |

## A light-year is a distance, not a time

$$
d = vt = (3.00 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{km/s})(3.15 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{s}) = 9.46 \times 10^{12}\ \mathrm{km}
$$

## Scale it down to something you can walk

Shrink the Sun to the size of a **basketball** (24 cm):

| Object | Scaled distance |
| --- | --- |
| Earth | 26 m away, and 2 mm across |
| Jupiter | 134 m away |
| Neptune | 775 m away |
| Proxima Centauri | **7 000 km** away — Toronto to Lisbon |

> [!important] This is the fact to take away
> On a scale where the entire solar system fits on a school field, the nearest
> other star is on another continent. Every science-fiction voyage quietly
> ignores this.

## Looking out is looking back

Light takes time. The Andromeda galaxy is 2.5 million light-years away, so you
see it as it was 2.5 million years ago. Telescopes are time machines pointed the
only direction they can go.
