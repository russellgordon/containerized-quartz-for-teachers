This course asks you to break things, be confused in public, and
eventually knock on a stranger's door to ask about their week. That
takes norms — and they bind everyone in the room, including your
teacher.

> [!danger] The core agreement
> Nobody's half-working program gets laughed at. Every program you
> have ever admired was a broken program first, usually hundreds of
> times.

## What we agree to

- **Predict before you run.** Saying out loud what code will do — and
  being wrong — is the fastest way to learn to read it.
- **Bugs are information.** An error message is the computer being as
  helpful as it knows how to be. We read it before we groan at it; see
  [[Mistakes Are Data]].
- **Drivers swap.** In pair work the keyboard changes hands on the
  timer, every time, with no exceptions for "but I am faster".
- **Your accounts are yours; theirs are theirs.** Nobody uses a
  machine somebody else is signed into, and you lock your screen when
  you step away.
- **Honest about help.** A classmate's idea, a forum answer, an AI
  assistant's suggestion — all fine WHEN NAMED, in a comment, the way
  [[Writing Code Others Can Read]] shows. Passing off borrowed or
  generated work as your own thinking is the one unforgivable bug.
- **Clients are people, not props.** When you interview someone, you
  arrive on time, you ask permission before using their name, and you
  do not disappear halfway through the project. Their information
  stays private unless they say otherwise —
  [[Interviewing Your Client]] spells this out.
- **We argue with ideas, never at people.** That governs every page in
  [[Discussions/index|Discussions]], especially the uncomfortable
  ones.

## Why this is not just being nice

A room that shares half-broken programs learns faster, because
problems get found while they are still cheap to fix. That is
precisely how professional software teams work. None of the above is a
school rule with a technology coat of paint; they are the working
norms of the field, learned early.
