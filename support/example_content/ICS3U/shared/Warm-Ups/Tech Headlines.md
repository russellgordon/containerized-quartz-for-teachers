One minute, one current technology story, three questions: what is
being *claimed*, what is actually *known*, and who *benefits* if you
believe it? Technology news runs hot — every product is revolutionary,
every risk is an apocalypse — and this routine is the thermostat. It
also stocks the shelves for every conversation we hold, from
[[Should It Exist]] to [[When Code Hurts]].

## How to run it

1. One student brings the story: the headline and two sentences of
   summary, no more. The job rotates, so everyone takes a turn.
2. The room answers the three questions in order. Keep *claimed* and
   *known* strictly apart — that boundary is the whole skill.
3. "Who benefits?" goes last. Who is selling something, who wrote the
   headline, and who was not asked?
4. Park anything worth a longer argument on the board for a future
   discussion.

## One variation

Same story, two sources: read a company's own announcement beside a
news report about it, and list what each one leaves out. The gap is
usually more instructive than either document.

> [!tip] "We do not know yet" is a finding
> The strongest sentence said in this warm-up is often that the honest
> answer is not known — the evidence is thin, the study was small, the
> demo was staged. Practise saying it plainly. It is rarer in
> technology writing than it should be, and it is the same standard
> [[Computers and Society]] holds every claim to.
