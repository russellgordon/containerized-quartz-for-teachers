The question that comes up in Unit 4, every year, in some form: is this
worth doing if I am not going to be a programmer? The useful answer is
that "programmer" is one job in a field with dozens, that most of them
involve talking to people at least as much as typing, and that the
thing you just did — find somebody's problem, build something modest,
hand it over — is what the work actually looks like.

## There is more than one door

| Pathway | What it looks like | Fits you if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| University | three to five years, degrees in computer science, software engineering, or a mix of computing and another subject | you like the theory as much as the building, and want research or specialised work open to you |
| College | two to three years, diplomas and advanced diplomas in programming, networking, IT support, game development, data | you want to be working with the tools quickly, with lots of hands-on hours |
| Certification alongside work | industry certifications in networking, cloud platforms, security, often started while employed | you are already working somewhere with computers and want to move sideways into the technical side |
| Self-taught, portfolio-first | learning from documentation and projects, evidence in the form of things you built | you finish things without being asked; the portfolio does the talking, and it has to be real |

None of these is the serious one. They differ in cost, length, and how
soon you are hands-on, and people move between them their whole
careers — a college diploma that becomes a degree later, a self-taught
developer who takes a certification because a job needs it. Ask what a
particular path opens and what it costs, not which is best.

Some computing work also sits close to the trades: network cabling,
hardware service, industrial control and automation. Those roles reach
you through college programs, employer training, and apprenticeship-style
placements rather than a computer science degree, and they are real
technical careers.

## How to research it properly

Anybody can tell you the field is growing. Do better than that, because
it is easy to check:

- Read current job postings for a role that interests you — not the
  salary line, the *requirements* line. It tells you what employers are
  actually asking for this year.
- Compare three postsecondary programs on the things that differ:
  entrance requirements, length, cost, whether there is a co-op term,
  what graduates say they do now.
- Use Statistics Canada and provincial labour market information for
  trends rather than a headline, and notice whether a number describes
  Ontario, Canada, or somewhere else entirely.
- Look outside the obvious employers. Hospitals, farms, insurance
  companies, museums, city governments, and film studios all employ
  people who write software.
- Ask somebody doing the job. One twenty-minute conversation beats a
  week of reading, and most people say yes when a student asks.

Report what you find in whatever form communicates best — a short
presentation, a page, a video. Being able to explain a technical
pathway to somebody else is itself part of the course.

## Experience before a credential

Every path above is easier with evidence attached, and evidence is
available to you now:

- A **co-op** placement or a job-shadowing day puts you inside the work
  for long enough to learn whether you like it.
- **Career fairs** and open houses are worth attending with three
  prepared questions rather than a bag for the free pens.
- **Volunteer building** — the thing you did in [[The Community App]] —
  is genuine experience. A program a local organisation actually uses,
  with a person who will vouch for it, is a stronger portfolio piece
  than a tutorial project.
- Keep the artefacts as you go. Your [[Code Journal]] and
  [[Final Reflection]] are the raw material of a portfolio, and they
  are much harder to reconstruct a year later.

If a path feels closed to people like you, look for the
[[D3.4|groups that exist for exactly that reason]] — mentoring
programs, student chapters, and networks for women and other
under-represented people in computing, along with the professional and
industry associations attached to particular kinds of work. They exist
in Ontario, they are usually free to students, and their whole purpose
is to make the first door easier to open.

## The skills nobody lists first

The Ontario Skills Passport describes the Essential Skills and work
habits that employers actually name, and the computing versions are not
surprising once you have finished a project for somebody else: reading
technical text, writing clearly, numeracy, thinking and problem
solving, working with others, and continuous learning — alongside work
habits like reliability, organisation, initiative, and customer
service. Read that list again next to your project. The hardest parts
of the term were the specification, the client conversation, and the
handover, which is exactly what that list predicts.

Nothing on it is fixed at birth, and all of it is visible in how you
work this semester. That is the honest reason [[How Marks Work]]
rewards a finished, usable, documented program over a clever one
nobody asked for.
