Four people need a computer. Each of you gets one of them, the same
budget, and twenty minutes. Then you defend your choice to somebody who
picked differently.

## The four people

> [!example] Your client is one of these
> **Amara** edits video for the school's morning announcements. Long
> exports, big files, works in the studio and never moves the machine.
>
> **Devon** takes the bus, writes essays, keeps twenty tabs open, and
> is on video calls for a part-time job. Charges once a day if lucky.
>
> **Ms. Whitfield** runs the front office: three programs at once, a
> label printer, two monitors, and files that must never be lost.
>
> **Rowan** is starting this course next year, plus a photography
> class, on a budget that has to cover both a machine and a camera.

## What to do

1. **List what they do** — five to eight specific activities, in their
   words as you imagine them.
2. **Turn each activity into a component.** Which part does it lean
   on? Some activities lean on two; say which one gives out first.
3. **Rank the components.** Where does the money go first, second,
   third? Write the order down before you look at anything for sale.
4. **Spend the budget.** Write out the specifications you would buy —
   processor, memory, storage, graphics, display — plus one peripheral,
   in correct terminology.
5. **Name the trade-off.** What did your client not get, and on which
   day will they notice?

## The defence

Pair with somebody who had a different client. Two minutes each:
present the specifications, then defend one decision they challenge. A
good challenge sounds like "you spent on graphics — what activity of
theirs actually needs it?"

The strongest answers in this exploration are rarely the biggest
numbers. They are the ones where the person doing the talking clearly
knows which activity would break first.

## Afterwards

interview instead of an imagined client.
