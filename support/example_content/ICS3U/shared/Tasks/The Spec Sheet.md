> Solo · launched in Unit 1 and due six classes later · four working

## What you are making

A one-page recommendation for a **real person you know** who needs a
computer — a family member, a coach, a neighbour, someone in a club.
Not a hypothetical. The person has a budget and a set of things they
actually do, and your job is to turn those things into specifications
and then into one machine you would stake your name on.

## What must be in it

- **Who they are, in their words.** Three or four sentences from an
  actual conversation: what they do with a computer now, what annoys
  them about it, and what they wish it could do. If they say "it's
  slow", ask when — starting up, opening files, or with fifteen tabs
  open. The three answers point at three different parts.
- **The budget**, stated plainly, and where it came from.
- **A requirements table**: each thing they do, and the component it
  leans on.

  | What they do | Component it leans on | What that means concretely |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | Fifteen tabs and a video call | Memory | 16 GB, not 8 |
  | Photos from a good camera | Storage, then screen | 1 TB SSD; colour-accurate display |

- **The machine you recommend**, with its actual specifications
  written out in correct terminology — processor, memory, storage type
  and size, graphics, display, battery, ports.
- **Two peripherals** that matter for this person, with the numbers
  that made you choose them.
- **What you gave up.** Every budget forces a trade. Name it, and name
  who would have noticed. A recommendation with no trade-off in it is a
  recommendation nobody thought hard about.
- **One sentence on where the machine goes at the end of its life**,
  from [[Where to Take It, and Who to Ask]].

## How to work

1. Interview first, specify second. The interview technique from
   [[Interviewing Your Client]] applies exactly.
2. Turn each need into a component before you look at a single
   product — otherwise the product decides your requirements for you.
3. Use [[Reading a Spec Sheet]] to translate, and [[Inside the Machine]]
   when you need to explain *why* a part is the one that matters.
4. Write it for the person, not for me. If they cannot follow it, it
   has failed at its only job.

## How this is assessed

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Grounded in a person | Real needs, in their words, not a product review |
| Correct terminology | Memory, storage, processor used precisely |
| Requirements drive specs | Every number traces back to something they do |
| Peripherals justified | Chosen on numbers, not on brand |
| Honest about trade-offs | Says what was given up, and who would notice |
| Readable by the client | Plain language; no unexplained jargon |

## Reflect

A [[Code Journal]] entry: which of their answers changed your
recommendation most, and what would you have got wrong if you had
skipped the interview and gone straight to the specifications?
