If mathematics class only ever asked you to do things you already
knew how to do, it would be a photocopying service. The learning
happens in the stretch — standing at a whiteboard with your random
group, holding an optimization problem none of you can crack yet.
But not all struggle is equal. *Stuck-and-thinking* means you still
have a next move to try: build one specific box before the general
one, sketch the sign of the derivative on a number line, shrink the
three-dimensional question to its two-dimensional cousin. *Spinning*
means repeating the last move harder and hoping. The first builds
mathematicians; the second just builds frustration — and telling
them apart from the inside is a skill this course teaches on
purpose, with [[Getting Unstuck]] as its field guide. Within a year,
many of you will sit in a lecture hall where nobody hands you the
method before the problem; this room is where you practise not
needing them to.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Recall a problem that finally gave in after real struggle. What
   did the moment of giving-in feel like — and would being handed the
   answer have produced anything like it?
2. Optimization is strange struggle: you can often *feel* the answer
   before you can prove it — the best box looks nearly cubic, the
   shortest path looks straight — and the feeling is not evidence.
   Does sensing the destination make the struggle easier or crueller?
   Why?
3. Why does this class put you in *random* groups at *vertical*
   whiteboards? What do standing up and a shared marker do to
   struggle that a worksheet at a desk does not?
4. The group beside yours is stuck on a problem you have cracked.
   When does helping them actually take something from them — and
   what does good help look like instead?
5. Where is the line between productive struggle and genuinely being
   out of your depth? Is admitting the second a failure, or a move?

This is why every practice set here folds its answers away and why
nothing is ever timed: the callouts protect your struggle from your
own curiosity. Attempt first, peek second — the order is the whole
point.
