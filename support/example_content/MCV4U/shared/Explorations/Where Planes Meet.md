Your group gets three sheets of stiff cardboard — three planes, if
you squint — and permission to slice them through one another. In
three-space, what are *all* the ways three planes can sit?

## The task

Catalogue every configuration. Start with two sheets: parallel, or
meeting in a line — is there anything else? Then bring in the third,
and for each arrangement answer the only question that matters: what
do all three share — a point, a line, a whole plane, or nothing at
all? Draw each configuration, count them, and defend the claim that
your catalogue is complete. Then the bridge to algebra: a plane is
the solution set of one equation $Ax + By + Cz + D = 0$, so three
sheets are three equations. Match each configuration in your
catalogue to what solving the system would report — exactly one
solution, infinitely many, or none — and find the configurations the
count alone cannot tell apart.

> [!tip]- Facilitation notes — for the teacher
> Sheets with pre-cut slots beat imagination — hands find
> arrangements that sketches miss. The configuration groups
> reliably overlook is the triangular prism: three sheets meeting
> pairwise in three parallel lines, sharing nothing — leave straws
> or skewers out so groups can lay one along each intersection line
> and *see* the three lines refuse to meet. The fight worth having:
> "no common point" comes in flavours, and so does "infinitely
> many". Fast groups: which solution counts map to more than one
> picture, and what extra question separates them? Groups who met
> the cross product can chase the elegant test: dot one normal
> against the cross product of the other two, and ask what a zero
> is whispering.

## What mathematics tends to surface

The complete catalogue, argued rather than listed — coincident,
parallel, pencils of planes sharing a line, the prism, and the
single clean point. Then the correspondence that powers the rest of
the unit: intersection is *shared solutions*, and the count — none,
one, infinitely many — is geometry's shadow in the algebra.
[[Equations of Planes]] and [[Intersections of Lines and Planes]]
make both directions official.

## Where it leads

Solving three equations in three unknowns now comes with pictures
attached, and every answer earns a geometric interpretation. In
[[The Flight Path]], one line meets one plane in one point — and
that point is where your wheels touch.
