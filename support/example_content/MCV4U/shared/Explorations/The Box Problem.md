One square sheet of card, $20$ cm on a side. Cut equal squares from
the four corners, fold up the flaps, tape the seams: an open-top
box. Everyone in the room gets the same sheet. Whose box holds the
most?

## The task

Build first. Choose a cut size, make the box, and compute its volume
in $\text{cm}^3$. Post the class data — cut size against volume — and
stare at it: the numbers rise, peak, and fall. Now the real work:
could any cut beat the best box built in this room? Write the volume
as a function of the cut $x$, decide which values of $x$ even make
sense, and use this unit's tools to find the cut nobody can beat —
then *prove* nobody can. Then push: does your winning cut survive a
bigger sheet — $30$ cm, $40$ cm? Conjecture the rule for a square
sheet $s$ centimetres on a side and defend it. And what happens to
all that tidiness if the sheet is $20$ cm by $30$ cm instead?

> [!tip]- Facilitation notes — for the teacher
> The physical boxes matter: the class scatter plot never quite
> matches the model — tape, flap thickness, ragged cuts — and naming
> that gap honestly is better mathematics than hiding it. Expect a
> fight over the domain; a cut of $10$ cm leaves no box at all, and
> the group that says so out loud has found the constraint. The
> proof question separates "the biggest we found" from "the biggest
> there is" — the sign of the derivative on each side settles it.
> Fast groups: the square sheet's winning cut is always the same
> fraction of the side — let them find the fraction — and the
> $20 \times 30$ sheet resists tidy numbers on purpose.

## What mathematics tends to surface

The whole optimization playbook, in order and by necessity:
a constraint that turns two variables into one, a domain the story
itself imposes, a derivative whose zero locates the peak, and a sign
argument that turns "best so far" into "best, full stop". The class
data even supplies the misfit between model and cardboard.
[[Optimization]] names the playbook right after.
