A graph appears on the board with no numbers, no labels, no title —
just axes and a shape. Three questions, always in this order: what do
you notice? what do you wonder? what story could this graph be
telling? In this course the horizontal axis is usually time, and the
story is usually *motion* — which means the mathematics lives in the
slope: where the curve is steep, something is happening fast, and
where it flattens, something is coming to rest.

## How we play

1. Thirty seconds of silent looking. No hands yet.
2. Notice only what is *there*: a steep climb, a levelling-off, a
   moment where the curve turns around and comes back.
3. Pitch a story — then point to the feature of the graph that
   each part of your story explains.

> [!example]- Three stories for one curve that climbs, flattens, then climbs again
> - "Someone walking to school who stops at a friend's house. The
>   flat stretch is the doorstep conversation — position frozen,
>   clock still running."
> - "The height of water filling a vase with a bulge in the middle.
>   The flattening is the bulge: same tap, more vase to fill, so the
>   level rises more slowly."
> - "It is a cubic with a horizontal tangent." True to the shape,
>   but a story with no characters. We sent it back for some.

## One variation

Same rules, but the graph on the board is a *velocity*-time graph —
and now the story hides one layer down. A flat stretch no longer
means stopped; it means cruising. The curve dipping below the axis
does not mean going backward in time; it means the walker turned
around. Telling the position story from the velocity graph, or the
velocity story from the position graph, is the whole game of
[[Motion on a Line]] — and the group that plays it here first plays
it faster there.

> [!tip] Features have names
> Steepness, flat spots, turning points, the direction of bending —
> using the right name for the right feature is half of every story,
> and this course spends a semester making those names exact.
> "Steepness" is about to become [[The Derivative]], and the art of
> reading a whole story from slopes alone is [[Curve Sketching]].
