A scientific drawing is not art and it is not decoration. It is a
record, and it is judged on whether somebody else can read information
off it. This is good news if you think you cannot draw: none of what
earns marks requires talent.

## The rules that apply to all of them

- **Pencil, sharp, and a ruler for every straight line.** Ink cannot be
  corrected and freehand straight lines are never straight.
- **Single clean lines.** No sketching the same edge four times hoping
  one of them is right.
- **Big.** At least half a page. Small drawings cannot be labelled and
  cannot be read.
- **No shading and no colouring in.** Where you need to show a darker
  region, use **stippling** — dots, closer together where it is darker.
- **Labels printed horizontally**, outside the drawing, connected by
  ruled lines that touch the structure and **do not cross each other**.
  No arrowheads on label lines.
- **A title underneath**, saying what it is and how it is viewed.

## What changes between the three kinds

| Kind of drawing | The specific conventions |
| --- | --- |
| Biological, from a microscope | Draw only what you can actually see; state the total magnification; a scale bar if you have one |
| Apparatus | Two-dimensional cross-section, no perspective; liquid surfaces as straight lines; contents named |
| Ray diagram | Ruler for every ray; arrowheads showing direction; solid for real light, dashed for extensions and virtual rays |

### Biological drawings

The single most common mark lost: drawing what the textbook says is
there instead of what is down the eyepiece. If your cells are squashed,
torn, folded over, or full of an air bubble, draw them squashed, torn,
folded, or bubbled, and say so underneath. A drawing of an idealised
cell is a drawing of a diagram, not of a specimen.

Record the magnification as eyepiece × objective — a 10× eyepiece with
the 40× objective gives a total of 400× — and check the numbers engraved
on the lenses rather than assuming. See [[Using a Microscope]].

### Ray diagrams

- Draw the **principal axis** first, as a horizontal ruled line, and put
  the lens or mirror on it as a vertical line.
- Mark **F** and **2F** on both sides, measured with a ruler at equal
  distances. A ray diagram with the focal points guessed by eye is
  decoration.
- Draw the object as an upright arrow standing on the axis.
- **Arrowheads on every ray**, pointing the way the light travels.
- Where rays genuinely cross, you have a **real image**: solid lines.
  Where you have to extend rays backwards to find the crossing, the
  image is **virtual**: those extensions are dashed, and so is the
  image.
- The **normal** is dashed, drawn perpendicular to the surface at the
  point where the ray meets it. Every angle in optics is measured from
  the normal, never from the surface — writing an angle from the surface
  is the most common way a correct measurement becomes a wrong answer.

> [!tip] Draw the ray diagram before you touch the equipment
> In [[Finding the Focal Length]] and again in [[The Optics Design]],
> the diagram is your prediction. Drawing it first tells you where to
> put the screen and roughly what to expect, so a result that disagrees
> is immediately interesting instead of merely confusing.

## Check before you hand it in

- [ ] Title underneath, saying what and how viewed
- [ ] Magnification or scale stated, if it came from a microscope
- [ ] Ruled label lines, horizontal labels, nothing crossing
- [ ] No shading, no colour, no double lines
- [ ] For a ray diagram: arrowheads present, dashed lines only where
      the light does not really go, normals perpendicular and dashed
- [ ] Somebody who was not there could say what you were looking at

Every investigation write-up wants at least one of these. See
[[Writing a Lab Report]] for where the drawing sits in the report, and
[[Reflection]] and [[Lenses and Images]] for the ray conventions in
context.
