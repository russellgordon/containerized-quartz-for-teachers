This is the reference page — how to read a label, where the information
lives, and the handful of practices that prevent almost every accident
in a school lab. The agreement you sign before your first investigation
is [[Safety in the Lab]]; this page is what that agreement is built on.

## WHMIS in one paragraph

WHMIS — the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System — is
Canada's law-backed system for telling anyone who handles a hazardous
product what it can do to them and what to do about it. It is
harmonised with the international system used in most of the world, so
a label made in Germany and one made in Ontario carry the same symbols.
It has three parts: **labels** on containers, a **safety data sheet**
for each product, and **training** for the people using it. That third
part is this page, and the class it comes from.

## The pictograms, by what they are warning you about

The picture is a summary, not the instruction. It tells you what kind of
harm is possible so you know which precautions to read first.

| Pictogram | What is drawn | What it signals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flame | A flame | Flammable and combustible materials, including ones that ignite in air or release flammable gas on contact with water |
| Flame over circle | A flame above a circle | Oxidizing materials — they feed a fire, and can start one in things that would not otherwise burn |
| Gas cylinder | A gas cylinder | Gases under pressure, including refrigerated and dissolved gases |
| Corrosion | Liquid dropping onto a hand and onto a surface | Corrosive to skin and eyes; some are corrosive to metals |
| Exploding bomb | A bursting sphere | Explosives, and self-reactive materials and organic peroxides that can detonate |
| Skull and crossbones | A skull over crossed bones | Acute toxicity — serious harm or death from a small amount, quickly |
| Health hazard | A starburst inside a human torso | Serious, often delayed effects — cancer, reproductive and genetic harm, respiratory sensitisation, organ damage, aspiration hazard |
| Exclamation mark | An exclamation mark | Less severe effects — skin and eye irritation, skin sensitisation, drowsiness or dizziness |
| Biohazardous infectious materials | Three crescents around a circle | Micro-organisms and their toxins that cause disease in people or animals |

You will also see a **dead fish and tree** symbol on many labels,
marking hazards to the aquatic environment. It comes from the same
international system, though it is not one WHMIS itself requires in
Canada. Treat it as real information: it is telling you the product must
not go down the sink.

> [!important] Two symbols people mix up
> The **skull and crossbones** means a small amount can hurt you
> immediately. The **health hazard** starburst means the damage may take
> years to appear and may never be traceable to today. The second one
> looks less alarming and is often the more serious of the two, because
> nothing about handling it will feel dangerous at the time.

## Reading a label

A supplier label — the one printed by whoever made the product —
carries the product identifier, the supplier's name and contact
information, the pictograms, a **signal word**, hazard statements, and
precautionary statements. The signal word is doing real work:
**Danger** marks the more severe hazards, **Warning** the less severe.

When a chemical is decanted into a smaller container for a class, it
needs a **workplace label**: the product name exactly as it appears on
the safety data sheet, the precautions for handling it, and a pointer to
that sheet. An unlabelled beaker is not a mystery to solve — it goes to
me, and it gets disposed of.

## The safety data sheet

Every hazardous product has an SDS, in a standard order of sixteen
sections, so you can find the same information on any product from any
supplier. The four you will actually use:

| Section | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| 2 — Hazard identification | What can this do to me? |
| 4 — First-aid measures | What do we do in the first two minutes? |
| 7 — Handling and storage | How is it kept, and what must it not sit beside? |
| 8 — Exposure controls and personal protection | What do I need to wear? |

Sheets for everything in this room are kept where I will show you on
the first lab day. Read section 4 **before** the investigation, not
during the accident.

## The practices that prevent most of it

- **Eye protection goes on when you enter and comes off when the last
  person has cleaned up** — not when your own work is done. Somebody
  else's splash is still your eyes. Tell me if you wear contact lenses
  so we can make sure your eye protection is right for them.
- **Long hair tied back, loose sleeves secured, closed-toe shoes.**
  Spills and broken glass go down.
- **Never pipette by mouth.** Ever, with anything, including water. Use
  a bulb or pump.
- **Add acid to water, never water to acid.** Diluting concentrated acid
  releases a lot of heat. Pour acid slowly into a larger volume of water
  and the water carries the heat away; pour water onto acid and it can
  boil at the surface and throw acid out of the container.
- **Hot glassware looks exactly like cold glassware.** There is no
  colour change, no glow, no warning. Assume anything near a heat source
  is hot, and use tongs or a clamp.
- **Point the open end of a heated test tube away** from yourself and
  from everyone else, and never heat a sealed container.
- **Waft**, do not sniff. Cup a hand over the container and draw the air
  toward your face.
- **Never return unused chemical to the stock bottle.** Take what you
  need; the excess is disposed of as directed. One contaminated stock
  bottle spoils every class after yours, and occasionally does much
  worse than that.
- **Read the label twice** — reaching for it and again before pouring.
  The two bottles that look alike are always beside each other.
- **Nothing enters your mouth.** No eating, no drinking, no gum, no
  tasting, and wash your hands before you leave whether or not you think
  you touched anything.

## Before you start, know where these are

- [ ] Eyewash station — and that it runs for a full fifteen minutes
- [ ] Safety shower, and how to pull it
- [ ] Fire extinguisher and fire blanket
- [ ] First-aid kit and the spill kit
- [ ] The nearest exit, and the one you would use if that exit is the
      problem

> [!danger] Report everything, immediately
> Every spill, break, cut, burn, splash, and "I think some got on my
> hand". A small event handled in the first thirty seconds is nothing.
> The same event hidden for ten minutes becomes an incident report and
> sometimes an injury. **You will never be in trouble for reporting an
> accident** — the only thing that ends lab privileges here is not
> reporting one.
