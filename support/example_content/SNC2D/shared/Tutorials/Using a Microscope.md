A compound light microscope is the most expensive thing most of you
will touch this semester, and every way of damaging one comes from the
same mistake: moving the objective lens toward the slide while looking
through the eyepiece. Everything below is arranged to make that
impossible.

## Carrying and setting up

- Two hands, always. One under the base, one on the arm.
- Set it down away from the edge of the bench, with the cord where
  nobody will hook it.
- Turn the revolving nosepiece until the **lowest-power objective**
  clicks into place. It is the shortest one, and it is where every
  observation begins.

## Focusing, in the order that protects the slide

1. Put the slide on the stage, specimen over the hole in the stage, and
   secure it with the **stage clips**.
2. **Watching from the side, not through the eyepiece**, use the coarse
   adjustment to bring the objective and the slide as close together as
   they will go.
3. Now look through the eyepiece and turn the coarse adjustment so the
   objective moves **away** from the slide until the specimen comes into
   focus. Moving away can never crack anything.
4. Sharpen with the fine adjustment. Adjust the diaphragm until the
   contrast is best — brightest is not clearest.
5. **Centre what you want to see** before changing magnification. The
   field of view shrinks dramatically at higher power, and anything off
   centre is simply gone.
6. Rotate to the next objective. Focus with the **fine adjustment
   only**, and open the diaphragm — higher power needs more light.

> [!danger] Coarse adjustment on high power is how slides break
> On the high-power objective the gap between lens and cover slip is
> less than a millimetre. A coarse-adjustment knob crosses that in a
> fraction of a turn, and the result is a broken slide, glass under your
> hands, and a scratched objective that costs more than the slide by a
> wide margin. High power gets the fine adjustment and nothing else.

## Magnification, and what you lose to get it

| Objective | With a 10× eyepiece | Field of view | Depth of field |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low (typically 4×) | 40× | Widest | Deepest |
| Medium (typically 10×) | 100× | Narrower | Shallower |
| High (typically 40×) | 400× | Narrowest | Very shallow |

Total magnification is the eyepiece multiplied by the objective — read
the numbers engraved on each, since not every set is the same. Both of
the right-hand columns get worse as you go up, which is why you find
things on low power and only then magnify them.

Two things surprise people the first time:

- The image is **inverted and reversed**. Push the slide right and the
  image travels left. Your hands learn this in about five minutes.
- At high power only a thin slice of a thick specimen is in focus at
  once. Rolling the fine adjustment slowly back and forth is how you see
  the whole depth of it — that motion is part of observing, not a sign
  that you have lost focus.

## Wet mounts

Drop the specimen in a small drop of water, rest one edge of the cover
slip against the slide beside the drop, and lower it slowly with a
dissecting needle or probe. Going slowly pushes the air out ahead of the
glass instead of trapping it. Bubbles look like beautiful thick-walled
cells and have fooled everybody at least once — they are perfectly
round, very dark at the edge, and identical to each other, which real
cells never are.

## Putting it away

- [ ] Lowest-power objective clicked back into place
- [ ] Slide removed, and cleaned and dried if it is being reused
- [ ] Stage lowered, lamp off, cord wrapped loosely
- [ ] Dust cover on, carried back with two hands

> [!warning] Lens paper only
> Clean the eyepiece and objectives with lens paper and nothing else.
> Paper towel, tissue, and a shirt sleeve all carry grit that scratches
> the coating on the front element, and the scratch is there for every
> class after yours. If a lens is dirtier than lens paper can fix, tell
> me rather than working on it.
