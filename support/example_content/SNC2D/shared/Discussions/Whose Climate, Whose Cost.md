The physics in [[The Greenhouse Effect]] does not care who you are. The
consequences do. This discussion lives in the gap between those two
sentences.

## The question

The costs of a changing climate and the costs of responding to it land
on different people. Who should carry each, and who gets to decide?

## Two ledgers, not one

Almost every argument about climate policy goes badly because the two
sides are adding up different columns.

- **The cost of the change.** Thawing ground under roads and buildings
  in the North, and travel and harvesting conditions that no longer
  match the calendar. Coastal erosion and storm surge. Growing seasons
  that shift under farms already planted. Heat that is dangerous if your
  work is outdoors or your building has no cooling. Insurance that
  becomes expensive, then unavailable.
- **The cost of the response.** Jobs and tax bases in communities built
  around emissions-intensive industry. Up-front prices for a heat pump
  or a vehicle, which are a far bigger share of a small income than a
  large one. New mines, transmission lines, and processing plants, which
  land somewhere specific and on somebody specific.

Neither column is imaginary and neither is optional.

## Principles people actually argue from

| Principle | The argument | The objection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Polluter pays | Whoever emits should pay for the harm | Tracing a specific harm to a specific emitter is hard |
| Ability to pay | Those with resources can absorb the cost | Separates the bill from the behaviour entirely |
| Historical responsibility | Cumulative emissions caused today's warming | Charges people for choices made before they were born |
| Equal per person | The atmosphere is shared, so the share is equal | Ignores wildly different climates and needs |
| Beneficiary pays | Whoever gained from the emissions pays | Almost everyone gained something |

Pick one and defend it. You will be arguing against people holding the
others, and each of those has a real answer to your objection.

## How to prepare

Bring **one documented impact**, on one group of people, from a source
of the right kind — a national climate assessment or monitoring report
from Environment and Climate Change Canada, an IPCC assessment report,
or a peer-reviewed study the article you read is actually citing. Name
the source and the year of the report. Do not bring a number you cannot
point at, and do not round a number you did not measure.

> [!question]- The complication I will raise if nobody else does
> The people who bear the sharpest costs of a changing climate are
> often the people with the least say in the decisions that set
> emissions — including people not yet old enough to vote, and people
> living in places whose historical emissions are small. Does a decision
> made fairly by the people in the room count as fair if the people it
> falls on are not in the room? And if you say no, who do you propose
> puts them there?

## Ground rules

- Attack arguments, never people.
- Finish the sentence: "I disagree because…"
- Steelman. Argue against the best version of the other case. If you
  find yourself thinking "they would never have thought of that", assume
  they did.

Afterwards, write the paragraph you actually believe — you are free to
disagree with the principle you were assigned. That paragraph is useful
groundwork for [[The Climate Brief]]. Background:
[[Natural and Human Influences on Climate]] and
[[Reading a Graph Honestly]].
