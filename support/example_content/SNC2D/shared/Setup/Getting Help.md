## In order of speed

1. **Ask in class, at the time.** If you are confused, so are three
   other people who have not said anything yet.
2. **Check the concept page.** Most "I don't get it" moments are
   answered there, and the backlinks show you which class we did it in.
3. **Work through [[Getting Unstuck]].** It is a twenty-minute routine,
   and it resolves a good share of what looks unresolvable at first.
4. **Come to a help session.** Times are on [[Help Sessions]]. Drop in;
   no appointment, no warning needed.
5. **Email me.** Say what you tried. It makes the answer far more
   useful and it usually saves an entire round trip.

## Bring the specific thing

One equation that will not balance beats "chemistry". One step of a ray
diagram where you lose the thread beats "optics". The narrower the
question, the faster and better the answer — and narrowing it yourself
often solves it before you get to me.

## Help with a result, not just a method

The most useful conversation of the week is often somebody arriving
with data that did not do what they expected. That is not a failure to
be fixed quietly before anyone sees it; it is the interesting case, and
it is exactly what [[Mistakes Are Data]] is about. Bring the numbers
and the notebook, not a summary.

> [!note] Ask early
> Science builds, and this course builds harder than last year's did. A
> gap in what "controlled variable" means in the first unit turns into a
> wall when you are designing your own investigation in the second.
> Nobody has ever irritated me by asking a question too early.

If the difficulty is not the science — a week when things are hard for
other reasons — say so, in whatever words you like. We will sort out
the work; that part is always solvable.
