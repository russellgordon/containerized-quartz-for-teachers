When you looked at plant tissue in [[Looking at Tissue]], the first thing you
noticed was the wall around every cell, and the second was that some cells
were empty tubes. Both are clues. A plant faces the same problems an animal
faces — get materials in, move them where they are needed, keep the structure
standing up — and solves every one of them without muscle, without a pump, and
without nerves.

That is worth taking seriously rather than treating as a deficiency. Plants
are not simplified animals. They are a different set of answers to the same
questions, and the answers are mostly structural and physical rather than
behavioural.

## Two systems, and the tissues that build them

A plant is organised into a **root system** below ground and a **shoot
system** — stem, leaves, and any flowers — above it. Each is built from the
same three tissue types, arranged differently.

**Dermal tissue** covers. On a leaf it secretes a waxy cuticle that stops
water escaping, and it contains **stomata**, adjustable pores flanked by guard
cells. On a root it forms **root hairs**: single cells drawn out into long
projections, each one buying more absorbing surface, exactly as villi do in
your intestine.

**Ground tissue** fills, stores, and — in the leaf — photosynthesises. The
loosely packed cells in the middle of a leaf have air spaces between them so
that carbon dioxide can reach every cell's surface.

**Vascular tissue** transports, and it comes in two kinds that are easy to
confuse and important to keep apart. **Xylem** carries water and dissolved
minerals upward only. Its cells die at maturity and what is left is a stack of
hollow, reinforced tubes — the plumbing is made of corpses, which is also what
makes wood. **Phloem** carries dissolved sugars away from wherever they are
made or stored, upward or downward as needed, through living cells whose end
walls are perforated like a sieve.

## How water climbs a tree with no pump

This is the mechanism worth being able to state, because it is entirely
physical and it happens in a stationary organism that spends no energy on it.

Water evaporates from the wet cell surfaces inside a leaf and diffuses out
through the open stomata. That loss is **transpiration**. Because water
molecules attract one another strongly, the departing molecules do not leave
quietly — they pull on the ones behind them, and the pull is transmitted down
an unbroken column of water all the way to the roots. Adhesion of water to the
narrow xylem walls keeps the column from breaking. The tree is not pushing
water up; the atmosphere is pulling it, and the plant's job is to maintain a
continuous column for the pull to act through.[^1]

Water enters the root in the first place because the soil solution is more
dilute than the inside of a root hair cell, so water moves in by osmosis. From
there it crosses the root to the xylem and joins the column.

## The trade-off every land plant lives with

Stomata are the problem and the solution at once. Open them and carbon dioxide
diffuses in for photosynthesis — but water vapour diffuses out through the
same pores, and far faster. Close them and the water is saved, but the supply
of carbon dioxide stops and so does sugar production.

Guard cells manage this compromise minute by minute: they take up water and
swell, and because their walls are thicker on the inner side they bow apart
and open the pore; lose water and they sag closed. Plants in dry places close
their stomata during the heat of the day and hold them open at night; a plant
that wilts on a hot afternoon and recovers by morning is showing you this
system at its limit.

Look for the same logic anywhere in a plant. A structure that increases
surface area is almost always about exchange, and something that restricts it
is almost always about water.

Where systems break down and what we do about it: [[Systems and Health]]. To
practise arguing from structure to function across both plants and animals,
use [[Cells and Systems Practice]].

[^1]: This is called the cohesion–tension model, and it is a model in the
    proper sense — it explains the observations and it makes predictions you
    can test, such as the fact that the trunk of a tree is very slightly
    narrower on a hot afternoon than at dawn. Root pressure from below can push
    water a short way up a small plant, but it is nowhere near enough to
    account for a tall tree, which is why the pull from the leaves has to be
    doing the work.
