In [[Evidence of a Chemical Change]] you argued from observations — a colour
change, a gas, a temperature shift, a precipitate — that something new had
been made. An equation is how you say *what* was made, in a form that another
chemist can check. It is a sentence with a strict grammar, and every part of
it is a claim about the world.

## From words to symbols, without skipping the middle

There are three stages, and writing all three is not busywork. Each one can be
wrong in a different way, so keeping them separate is how you find out where
you went wrong.

A **word equation** names the substances and commits you to what reacted and
what was produced. It is where you settle the chemistry.

A **skeleton equation** replaces the names with formulas. It is where the work
from [[Chemical Formulas and Names]] gets used, and where a wrong formula
becomes a wrong equation.

A **balanced equation** adjusts the coefficients so the atoms account for one
another, which is the subject of [[Balancing Equations]].

> [!example] Magnesium burning, all three stages
> **Words:** magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide
>
> **Skeleton:** $\ce{Mg(s) + O2(g) -> MgO(s)}$
>
> **Balanced:** $\ce{2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)}$
>
> Notice that the observation — a brilliant white flame and a white powder
> left behind — only tells you the first line. Getting from there to the third
> line is chemistry you do on paper, and the powder weighing *more* than the
> ribbon did is the evidence that the equation's oxygen is real.

## What each symbol is claiming

The arrow is not an equals sign. Read it as **"produces"** or **"yields"**: it
points from what you started with to what you ended with, and it records that
the change ran in that direction under those conditions.

Everything to the left of the arrow is a **reactant**; everything to the right
is a **product**. Substances on the same side are separated by plus signs,
which read as "and", not as addition.

The letter in brackets after each formula is the **state**: $\text{(s)}$
solid, $\text{(l)}$ liquid, $\text{(g)}$ gas, $\text{(aq)}$ aqueous, meaning
dissolved in water. States are not optional garnish. In

$$\ce{AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) -> AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)}$$

the entire observable event is in the states: two clear solutions were mixed
and a solid appeared. Strip the states out and the equation no longer says
that anything visible happened.

Conditions that the reaction needs — heat, light, a catalyst — are written
above the arrow rather than as reactants, because they are not consumed.

## Why models make this concrete

Build the magnesium reaction, or the burning of methane, out of physical
model pieces and something becomes obvious that a page of symbols can hide:
**you never get a new piece.** You take the same atoms apart and reassemble
them. If your equation needs an atom you were not given, the model simply
cannot be built, and no amount of confident writing will produce it.

That is the whole content of the law of conservation of mass, arrived at with
your hands. It is also why a diagram of a model is worth drawing — it shows
the *rearrangement*, which is the mechanism, where the symbolic equation shows
only the bookkeeping. When you draw one, keep each atom the same colour and
size on both sides of the arrow; a reader should be able to point at an atom
in the products and find the same atom in the reactants.

Then make the counting reliable in [[Balancing Equations]], and start sorting
reactions by what they do in [[Types of Chemical Reactions]].
