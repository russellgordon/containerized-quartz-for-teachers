In [[Evidence of a Chemical Change]] you had to write down what happened
using phrases like "the white powder" and "the blue solution", because you did
not yet have anything better. That was deliberate. A description is not a
name, and until the substances have names you cannot say what reacted with
what.

This page is the vocabulary, and it comes before the grammar. Everything in
the rest of the unit — equations, balancing, reaction types — assumes you can
look at a formula and say what it is, and hear a name and write the formula
back.

## Two kinds of compound, and how to tell which you have

Look at where the elements sit on the periodic table. That one check decides
everything that follows.

A **metal with a non-metal** forms an **ionic compound**. The metal atom loses
one or more electrons and the non-metal gains them, producing oppositely
charged ions that attract each other in a rigid three-dimensional lattice. The
formula is not a molecule — it is the simplest whole-number ratio of ions in
that lattice.

**Two non-metals** form a **molecular compound**. Neither atom takes electrons
from the other, so they share, and the result is a discrete molecule. The
formula counts the atoms in one molecule.

That difference in mechanism is the reason the two naming systems differ, and
this is the part worth understanding rather than memorising:

- Ionic names use **no prefixes**, because the charges already fix the ratio.
  Sodium is always $\ce{Na+}$ and chlorine always $\ce{Cl-}$, so
  "sodium chloride" can only mean $\ce{NaCl}$. There is nothing to specify.
- Molecular names **need prefixes**, because the same two elements can share
  electrons in more than one arrangement. $\ce{CO}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ are
  both carbon and oxygen; one is a fuel that kills silently and the other is
  what you exhale. "Carbon oxide" would be useless, so we say carbon
  **mon**oxide and carbon **di**oxide.

Some metals form more than one stable ion, and those need a Roman numeral to
say which: iron(II) chloride is $\ce{FeCl2}$ and iron(III) chloride is
$\ce{FeCl3}$. The numeral is the **charge on the metal ion**, not the
number of anything.

## Building the formula from the charges

For an ionic compound, find the charge on each ion and combine them in
whatever ratio makes the total charge zero. The compound as a whole is
neutral; that is the only rule, and the crossing-over trick is just a fast way
of applying it.

Magnesium is $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and chloride is $\ce{Cl-}$, so it takes two
chlorides to cancel one magnesium: $\ce{MgCl2}$. Aluminium
$\ce{Al^3+}$ with oxide $\ce{O^2-}$ needs two aluminium and three
oxide to reach zero: $\ce{Al2O3}$. Always reduce to the lowest
whole-number ratio.

**Polyatomic ions** are groups of atoms bonded together that carry an overall
charge and travel through reactions as a unit. Learn these; they appear
constantly.

| Ion | Formula | Turns up in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydroxide | $\ce{OH-}$ | Bases such as $\ce{NaOH}$ |
| Nitrate | $\ce{NO3-}$ | Fertilisers, $\ce{KNO3}$ |
| Hydrogen carbonate | $\ce{HCO3-}$ | Baking soda, $\ce{NaHCO3}$ |
| Carbonate | $\ce{CO3^2-}$ | Limestone, $\ce{CaCO3}$ |
| Sulfate | $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | Copper(II) sulfate, $\ce{CuSO4}$ |
| Phosphate | $\ce{PO4^3-}$ | Fertilisers, bone mineral |
| Ammonium | $\ce{NH4+}$ | The one common positive one |

When you need more than one polyatomic ion, bracket it before adding the
subscript: calcium nitrate is $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$, meaning two whole
nitrate ions. Writing $\ce{CaNO6}$ says something entirely different and
untrue.

> [!warning] The ending is not decoration
> Sulf**ide** is $\ce{S^2-}$ — a single sulfur atom. Sulf**ate** is
> $\ce{SO4^2-}$ — one sulfur and four oxygens. The two differ by four
> oxygen atoms and by everything about their chemistry, and the only warning
> you get is three letters at the end of a word. An **-ide** ending means a
> simple ion of one element; **-ate** and **-ite** endings mean a polyatomic
> ion containing oxygen, with **-ite** having one fewer oxygen than **-ate**.

## Acids, briefly, because they look like a third category

An **acid** in this course is written as hydrogen combined with something
else, with the state $\text{(aq)}$ attached to show it is dissolved in water:
$\ce{HCl(aq)}$ is hydrochloric acid, $\ce{H2SO4(aq)}$ is
sulfuric acid, $\ce{HNO3(aq)}$ is nitric acid.

The $\text{(aq)}$ is doing real work. Pure $\ce{HCl}$ is a gas and behaves
like a molecular compound; dissolved in water it releases $\ce{H+}$ into
solution, and that is what makes it an acid. A formula without its state is
missing part of its meaning, which is why you will be asked for states from
here on. What those released ions actually do is the subject of
[[Acids and Bases]].

Drill the naming until it is automatic in [[Formulas and Names Practice]],
then start using it to describe reactions in [[Chemical Equations]].
