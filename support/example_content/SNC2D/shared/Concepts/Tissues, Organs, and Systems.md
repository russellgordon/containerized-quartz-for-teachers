The slide you drew in [[Looking at Tissue]] was not a picture of cells. It was
a picture of cells *of one kind, sitting together, doing one job*. That is
what a tissue is, and it is the first rung on a ladder that runs all the way
up to you.

The ladder matters for a reason that is easy to miss: at every rung, something
becomes possible that was impossible one rung down. No single cardiac muscle
cell pumps blood. Put millions of them together, electrically coupled so they
contract as one, wrap them in connective tissue and line them with epithelium,
and the assembly pumps. The pumping belongs to the organ, not to any cell in
it. Biologists call that emergence, and it is why "the body is just cells" is
a true sentence that explains nothing.

## The four rungs, said precisely

A **tissue** is a group of similar specialised cells that work together at one
task. Animals have four broad kinds, and you can name any of them from what it
does: **epithelial** tissue covers and lines surfaces, so it is packed tightly
with almost no gaps; **connective** tissue supports and joins, so its cells sit
scattered in a matrix they secrete themselves — bone, cartilage, blood and fat
are all connective; **muscle** tissue shortens; **nervous** tissue signals.

An **organ** is two or more tissue types built into one structure with one
overall function. The heart uses all four: cardiac muscle to contract,
nervous tissue to time the contraction, connective tissue for the valves and
the framework that stops it tearing itself apart, and epithelium lining the
chambers so blood slides past without clotting.

An **organ system** is a set of organs whose functions combine into something
larger. The stomach digests; the digestive *system* takes in food at one end
and delivers absorbed molecules to the blood.

An **organism** is the whole thing, and — this is the point of the next page —
no system in it works alone.

## The animal systems and what each is actually for

Learn these as *functions*, not as lists of organs. The organs follow from the
function every time.

| System | Its primary function | The problem it solves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Digestive | Break food into molecules small enough to absorb | Food is made of polymers your cells cannot use |
| Respiratory | Exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide with the air | Diffusion alone cannot reach a body this thick |
| Circulatory | Transport materials to and from every cell | Nothing diffuses far enough on its own |
| Excretory | Filter waste and regulate water and ions | Metabolism makes toxins continuously |
| Nervous | Sense, decide, and signal quickly | Chemical messages are too slow for a reflex |
| Musculoskeletal | Support the body and move it | A soft body cannot apply force |

Two of those entries say almost the same thing, and that is not an accident.
Diffusion is fast over the width of a cell and hopeless over the width of a
body. Almost every transport system in a large organism exists because
diffusion ran out of reach — including, as you will see, the systems that
plants build instead.

## Surface area is the recurring answer

Once you start looking for it, one design solution appears in system after
system. The lungs branch into hundreds of millions of tiny sacs. The small
intestine is folded, and the folds carry villi, and the villi carry microvilli.
The kidney contains about a million filtering units. A root divides and
divides and grows hairs.

Every one of those is the same move: **exchange happens at surfaces, so
maximise the surface without making the organism any bigger.** When you meet a
structure you have never seen before and it looks absurdly folded, branched,
or frilled, exchange is a very good first guess at what it does.
