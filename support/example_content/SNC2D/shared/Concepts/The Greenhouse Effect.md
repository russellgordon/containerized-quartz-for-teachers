In [[Modelling the Greenhouse Effect]] your group put two containers under the
same lamp, changed one thing on purpose, and wrote down a prediction before
switching anything on. The container with more carbon dioxide warmed faster.
This page is the mechanism behind that result — and, at the end, an honest
account of what your model did *not* show.

## Energy arrives as one kind of light and leaves as another

Start with the Sun. It is extremely hot, and hot objects radiate mostly
short-wavelength light — for the Sun, mainly visible light with some
ultraviolet and near-infrared. The atmosphere is largely transparent to that,
so most of it reaches the ground, which absorbs it and warms.

Earth's surface radiates too, but it is far cooler than the Sun, and cooler
objects radiate at longer wavelengths. What leaves the ground is **infrared**.

That difference in wavelength is the entire mechanism, because the atmosphere
is not equally transparent to both. Certain gas molecules absorb infrared
strongly while letting visible light straight through. Energy therefore comes
in easily and leaves with difficulty, and a surface that gains energy faster
than it loses it warms until the two rates match again — at a higher
temperature than it would otherwise have had.

Why only *certain* gases? Nitrogen and oxygen make up about 99% of the
atmosphere and are almost useless as greenhouse gases. Both are symmetric
molecules of two identical atoms, and their vibrations do not shift electric
charge within the molecule, so infrared radiation has nothing to grip. Carbon
dioxide, methane, water vapour and nitrous oxide have vibrations that *do*
shift charge around — bending, stretching asymmetrically — and those
vibrations absorb infrared at particular wavelengths. The molecule then
re-radiates, in every direction, so a fraction of the energy that was on its
way out comes back down.

## The natural effect keeps us alive; the enhancement is the problem

This distinction is not a technicality, and getting it backwards makes the
whole topic incoherent.

The **natural greenhouse effect** has been running since long before people
existed. Without it, Earth's average surface temperature would sit near
−18 °C — cold enough that surface water would be ice almost everywhere. The
observed average is roughly +15 °C. That difference of about 33 degrees is the
work of naturally occurring water vapour, carbon dioxide, and methane, and it
is the reason there is liquid water, and therefore life, at the surface. The
natural greenhouse effect is not pollution. It is a precondition for the
planet being habitable.

The **anthropogenic greenhouse effect** is the *enhancement* of that same
mechanism by gases added through human activity — chiefly carbon dioxide from
burning fossil fuels, along with methane, nitrous oxide, and industrial
halocarbons. Nothing new is happening physically. There is simply more of the
absorbing gas in the path, so a larger fraction of the outgoing infrared is
intercepted and the surface settles at a higher temperature.

So the sentence to be able to write is: the greenhouse effect is natural and
necessary; the problem is that we have strengthened it, quickly, by changing
the composition of the atmosphere.

## Sources and sinks, which is how the amount is set

The concentration of a gas in the atmosphere is a balance between what puts it
there — a **source** — and what removes it — a **sink**. Change either side
and the amount changes.

| Gas | Main sources | Main sinks | Roughly how long it lasts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon dioxide | Burning fossil fuels, deforestation, cement making, respiration, volcanoes | Dissolving into the oceans, photosynthesis on land, slow weathering of rock | Centuries, with a long tail |
| Methane | Wetlands, ruminant livestock, landfills, leaks from oil and gas, thawing permafrost | Chemical breakdown in the atmosphere | About a decade |
| Nitrous oxide | Soils, especially where nitrogen fertiliser is used; oceans; some industry | Destroyed in the stratosphere | Around a century |
| Halocarbons | Entirely industrial — refrigerants, propellants, solvents | Slow breakdown high in the atmosphere | Decades to millennia |
| Water vapour | Evaporation from oceans and land | Precipitation | Days |

Two rows deserve a second look.

**Water vapour** is the most abundant greenhouse gas, and yet it is not what
we argue about, because its residence time is days. Add water vapour to the
air and it rains out. What sets how much the air holds is the temperature — so
water vapour responds to warming rather than causing it, which makes it a
feedback rather than a driver. That distinction is developed in
[[Feedback Loops in Climate]].

**Carbon dioxide** is the opposite case. It is weaker per molecule than
methane or nitrous oxide, but it is emitted in enormous quantity and a
substantial fraction of what is released stays in circulation for centuries.
Long life is what makes a modest absorber into the dominant problem, and it is
why emissions accumulate: a sink that removes carbon dioxide over centuries
cannot keep pace with a source that runs every day.

## What the jar under the lamp did not show

Your model warmed, and the mechanism above is real — but a good scientist
names the limits of the model before someone else does.

A garden greenhouse stays warm mostly because the glass **stops warm air from
rising away**. That is convection, not radiation, and your sealed container
did some of the same thing. The atmosphere has no lid: air circulates freely,
and the planetary effect really is radiative. The name "greenhouse effect" is
therefore a slightly misleading historical label, and the model shares its
flaw.

Your containers also had no oceans, no clouds, no ice, and no day and night.
Those are not small omissions — they are most of what makes the real system
hard. The model is worth doing because it isolates one variable and shows the
mechanism cleanly. It is not evidence about how much the Earth will warm, and
a report that claims it is has overreached; see [[Writing About Science]].
