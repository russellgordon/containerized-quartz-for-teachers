In [[Modelling Digestion]] you watched something large fail to cross a
membrane and something small cross it easily. That single result is the reason
the digestive system exists at all. The molecules in food — starch, protein,
fat — are far too large to pass through the lining of your intestine and into
your blood. Digestion is not about making food disappear. It is about
**cutting big molecules into small ones**, and everything else in this system
is in service of that.

## Mechanical and chemical, and why both are needed

**Mechanical** digestion changes the size of pieces without changing what they
are. Chewing, the churning of the stomach wall, and the emulsifying action of
bile all do this. It sounds like the crude half of the job, but it is doing
something precise: enzymes can only act at a surface, so breaking one lump
into a thousand droplets multiplies the surface the enzymes can work on and
multiplies the rate of the chemical stage.

**Chemical** digestion changes what the molecules are. Enzymes split the bonds
holding a polymer together, adding water across each break — starch into
sugars, proteins into amino acids, fats into fatty acids and glycerol. Each
enzyme fits one kind of bond, which is why there is a different one for each
kind of food molecule, and why each works best at the pH of the place it is
secreted into.

| Station | Mechanical | Chemical | The mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mouth | Teeth cut and grind | Salivary amylase | Surface area up; starch begins breaking into sugars |
| Stomach | Muscular churning | Hydrochloric acid and pepsin | Acid unfolds proteins and activates pepsin, which cuts them into shorter chains |
| Small intestine | Bile emulsifies fat into droplets | Pancreatic amylase, proteases, lipase | Digestion finishes; small molecules are absorbed through the villi |
| Large intestine | — | Bacterial action | Water and ions are reclaimed; what is left is waste |

Two details in that table are worth stating out loud. Bile is made in the
liver, stored in the gall bladder, and contains **no enzymes** — it is
mechanical, breaking fat into droplets so lipase has surface to work on. And
the stomach's acid is not there to burn food; it denatures protein, unfolding
it so the cutting enzyme can reach the chain, and it provides the low pH that
pepsin needs in order to function at all.

## Absorption is where the system actually pays off

Everything upstream was preparation. In the small intestine the lining is
folded, the folds are covered in finger-like **villi**, and each villus cell
carries thousands of **microvilli**. The result is an absorbing surface out of
all proportion to the length of the tube — the same surface-area answer you
met in the lungs and the roots.

Inside each villus sits a capillary network and a lymph vessel. Sugars and
amino acids cross into the capillaries and are carried away in the blood; most
digested fat enters the lymph vessel instead and joins the bloodstream later.
The absorbed molecules go first to the liver, which adjusts what is in the
blood before the rest of the body sees it.

Note what has happened. The food is now *outside* the digestive tract and
*inside* the body, and it is no longer the digestive system's problem. It has
been handed off.

## No system finishes its own job

That handoff is the general rule, and it is the thing to be able to explain.

The digestive system delivers glucose and amino acids to the **circulatory**
system, which distributes them. The **respiratory** system delivers oxygen to
that same blood. Both arrive at a cell together because they have to: cellular
respiration combines glucose and oxygen to release usable energy, and a cell
supplied with only one of them gets nothing. The waste carbon dioxide leaves
the same way in reverse, and the nitrogen-containing waste from breaking down
amino acids is removed by the **excretory** system, because the digestive
system has no way to get rid of it.

Meanwhile the **nervous** system times the whole thing — you salivate before
the food arrives — and **muscle** tissue in the gut wall moves food along in
waves whether you think about it or not.

So when you are asked why systems must interact, the honest answer is not
"they help each other". It is that every system's output is unusable until
another system takes delivery of it, and an organism is the arrangement that
makes all of those deliveries happen at once.
