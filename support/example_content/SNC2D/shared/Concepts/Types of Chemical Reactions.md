In [[Reaction Types Lab]] you were given a set of reactions and asked to sort
them without being told the categories first. Groups arrived at different
sorting schemes — by colour change, by whether a gas came off, by whether
anything got hot. All of those are real patterns. The scheme chemists settled
on sorts by something else: **what happens to the arrangement of the atoms**,
which is the thing that lets you predict a reaction you have never seen.

## The five patterns

| Type | General form | What is happening | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Synthesis | $\text{A} + \text{B} \rightarrow \text{AB}$ | Two substances combine into one | $2\text{Mg(s)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{MgO(s)}$ |
| Decomposition | $\text{AB} \rightarrow \text{A} + \text{B}$ | One substance splits into two | $2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\text{(aq)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)}$ |
| Single displacement | $\text{A} + \text{BC} \rightarrow \text{AC} + \text{B}$ | One element takes another's place | $\text{Zn(s)} + \text{CuSO}_4\text{(aq)} \rightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4\text{(aq)} + \text{Cu(s)}$ |
| Double displacement | $\text{AB} + \text{CD} \rightarrow \text{AD} + \text{CB}$ | Two compounds swap partners | $\text{AgNO}_3\text{(aq)} + \text{NaCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{AgCl(s)} + \text{NaNO}_3\text{(aq)}$ |
| Combustion | fuel $+\ \text{O}_2 \rightarrow$ oxides | A fuel burns in oxygen, releasing energy | $\text{CH}_4\text{(g)} + 2\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(g)}$ |

Two honest complications, because you will meet both and they confuse people
who were taught the table as if it were airtight.

First, the categories **overlap**. Magnesium burning is a synthesis reaction
and a combustion reaction at the same time; it is not a trick question, and
both answers describe it correctly. Second, this scheme does not cover every
reaction in chemistry — it covers the ones you meet in Grade 10, which is a
different and more modest claim.

## Displacement is about reactivity, not luck

The two displacement rows are where prediction becomes possible, and where
there is a mechanism to explain rather than a pattern to recite.

Put a strip of zinc into blue copper(II) sulfate solution and the strip goes
copper-coloured while the blue fades. Put copper into zinc sulfate solution and
nothing at all happens. That asymmetry is not a quirk of the two metals you
happened to choose — it is the reason the **activity series** exists. Zinc
holds its outer electrons less tightly than copper does, so zinc gives them up
to the copper ions in solution: the zinc dissolves as $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ and the
copper ions become copper metal. Run it the other way and there is no reason
for copper to give electrons to zinc ions, so nothing does.

The rule that follows is worth stating exactly: **a more reactive element can
displace a less reactive one from its compound; a less reactive element
cannot.** A single displacement reaction that "should" have happened and did
not is usually telling you your two elements are the wrong way round in the
series.

**Double displacement** has a different driver. Mix two solutions of ions and
nothing needs to happen — the ions are already dissolved and can simply drift
past each other. A reaction occurs only when the swap **removes ions from the
solution**, and there are three ways to do that:

- an insoluble product forms and falls out as a **precipitate**, as silver
  chloride does above;
- a **gas** is produced and leaves;
- water is formed, which is what happens in the neutralisation of an acid by
  a base.

When you can name which of those three is happening, you have explained the
reaction rather than labelled it.

## Combustion, complete and incomplete

Burning a hydrocarbon with plenty of oxygen gives carbon dioxide and water,
and that is **complete combustion**. Starve it of oxygen and you get
**incomplete combustion** instead: some of the carbon comes off as soot, and
some as carbon monoxide, $\text{CO}$, which is colourless, has no smell, and
binds to haemoglobin far more tightly than oxygen does. That is the whole
reason fuel-burning appliances need ventilation and why a yellow, sooty flame
on a gas burner is a fault rather than a setting.

It is also where this unit meets the next one. Every carbon atom in a fuel
leaves the flame attached to oxygen, and the balanced equation tells you
exactly how much carbon dioxide a given mass of fuel must produce — see
[[The Greenhouse Effect]].

what acids and bases do when they swap partners in [[Acids and Bases]].
