Answer on paper first. The reasoning in each answer is the part to
compare against yours.

**1.** Put the stages of mitosis in order, and say what single
observation would let you identify **anaphase** in a field of cells
without seeing any of the other stages.

> [!success]- Answer 1
> Prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase — with interphase before and
> after, which is not part of mitosis but is where the cell spends most
> of its life.
>
> The identifying observation for anaphase is **two separate groups of
> chromosomes moving apart toward opposite ends of the cell**. Metaphase
> has them lined up in a single plane across the middle; telophase has
> two groups that have arrived and are being enclosed by re-forming
> nuclear membranes. Anaphase is the only stage where you can see a
> clear gap opening between two travelling groups.

**2.** A student counts every cell in one field of an onion root tip
and records: 96 not dividing, 12 prophase, 5 metaphase, 3 anaphase,
4 telophase. Assume the complete cell cycle takes 20 hours. Estimate
how long anaphase lasts, and state the assumption the estimate depends
on.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> Total cells counted: $96 + 12 + 5 + 3 + 4 = 120$.
>
> Fraction in anaphase: $\frac{3}{120} = 0.025$.
>
> Time in anaphase: $0.025 \times 20\ \text{h} = 0.5\ \text{h}$, which
> is about **30 minutes**.
>
> The reasoning is that a fixed slide freezes many cells at one instant,
> so the fraction caught in a stage is the fraction of the cycle that
> stage occupies. The assumption is that **the cells are dividing
> continuously and independently of one another** — not synchronised,
> and not all triggered by the same event. If they were synchronised,
> the count would tell you where they all are in the cycle rather than
> how long each stage takes.
>
> Worth noticing: $\frac{96}{120} = 0.80$, so 16 of the 20 hours are
> interphase. Division is the exception, not the routine.

**3.** A mature human red blood cell has no nucleus and no
mitochondria. Explain how each absence serves what the cell has to do,
and name the cost.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> **No nucleus:** the space is given over to haemoglobin instead, so
> the cell carries more oxygen per cell, and losing the nucleus lets the
> cell take up a flexible biconcave shape that squeezes through
> capillaries narrower than the cell is wide.
>
> **No mitochondria:** the cell does not consume the oxygen it is
> carrying. A delivery vehicle that burned its own cargo would be a
> poor design.
>
> **The cost:** with no nucleus the cell cannot make new proteins,
> cannot repair itself, and cannot divide. It has a limited working
> life and must be continuously replaced. Extreme specialisation buys
> performance at one job and gives up everything else, and that trade
> is the general pattern, not a quirk of this cell.

**4.** Trace an oxygen molecule from the air outside you to a working
muscle cell. At each step, name whether you are describing a cell, a
tissue, an organ, or a system.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> - Air enters through the airways and reaches the alveoli of the lung
>   — the **lung** is an organ, part of the respiratory **system**.
> - Oxygen diffuses across the thin alveolar wall — that wall is a
>   **tissue**, one cell thick precisely so the diffusion distance is
>   short.
> - It is picked up by haemoglobin in a red blood **cell**, carried in
>   blood, which is itself a **tissue**.
> - The **heart** — an organ built of muscle and nerve **tissue** —
>   pumps that blood through the circulatory **system**.
> - At a capillary, oxygen diffuses out into a muscle **cell**, part of
>   muscle **tissue**, part of an organ, part of the muscular
>   **system**.
>
> The point of the exercise: two systems are involved and neither works
> alone. Delivery is a hand-off, and the hand-offs are where the
> interesting biology is.

**5.** Xylem and phloem are both transport tissues in a plant. Give one
structural difference and connect it to a functional difference.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> **Structure:** mature xylem is made of cells that are dead and hollow
> at maturity, with thickened walls, forming continuous open tubes.
> Phloem is made of living cells joined end to end with perforated
> plates between them, and with companion cells alongside.
>
> **Function:** xylem carries water and dissolved minerals **upward
> only**, and needs no living machinery to do it — the pull comes from
> evaporation at the leaves. Phloem carries dissolved sugars from where
> they are made to wherever they are needed, which can be up or down,
> and moving a substance to where it is scarce takes living cells doing
> work.
>
> A one-way pipe can be dead. A two-way distribution network cannot.

**6.** A student writes: *"Mitosis is what makes the cells in your body
different from each other — that's how you end up with muscle cells and
nerve cells."* What is wrong with this, and what is the right account?

> [!success]- Answer 6
> It is backwards. **Mitosis produces two cells with identical genetic
> information** — that is the whole point of it, and it is why mitosis
> can repair a tissue and grow an organism without changing what the
> tissue is.
>
> The process that makes cells different is **differentiation**: cells
> with the same genetic information switch on different parts of it, so
> one becomes a muscle cell and another a nerve cell. Nothing about the
> information differs; what differs is which parts are being used.
>
> The student has spotted a real fact — you start as one cell and end
> up with many kinds — and attached it to the wrong process. That is
> the most common way a biology answer goes wrong: the observation is
> right and the mechanism is borrowed from somewhere else.

**7.** A cube-shaped organism 1 cm on a side grows into a cube 3 cm on
a side. Calculate the surface-area-to-volume ratio in each case, and
explain what the change means for an organism that relies on diffusion
across its surface.

> [!success]- Answer 7
> **1 cm cube:** surface area $= 6 \times (1)^2 = 6\ \text{cm}^2$,
> volume $= (1)^3 = 1\ \text{cm}^3$, ratio $= 6$ per cm.
>
> **3 cm cube:** surface area $= 6 \times (3)^2 = 54\ \text{cm}^2$,
> volume $= (3)^3 = 27\ \text{cm}^3$, ratio
> $= \frac{54}{27} = 2$ per cm.
>
> Volume grew 27 times while surface area grew only 9 times, so the
> ratio fell by a factor of 3. The **demand** for oxygen and nutrients
> scales with volume; the **supply** across the surface scales with
> area. Past a certain size, diffusion across the outside cannot keep
> the inside supplied.
>
> That is the reason large organisms have transport systems at all, and
> the reason exchange surfaces are folded, branched, or covered in
> projections — folding buys back the area that growth took away.

**8.** A gardening blog claims: *"Plants would grow faster if they just
kept their stomates open all the time — more carbon dioxide in means
more photosynthesis."* Say what is right about it and why the
conclusion does not follow.

> [!success]- Answer 8
> **What is right:** carbon dioxide does enter the leaf through open
> stomates, it does reach the mesophyll where photosynthesis happens,
> and its concentration can genuinely limit the rate.
>
> **Why the conclusion does not follow:** the same opening that lets
> carbon dioxide in lets water vapour out. A plant holding its stomates
> open through a hot, dry afternoon loses water faster than the roots
> and xylem can replace it, wilts, and then photosynthesises far less
> than a plant that closed up for a few hours. Closing stomates is not
> the plant failing to optimise; it is the plant managing a trade-off
> between two things it needs.
>
> The general lesson is worth keeping: an argument of the form "more of
> the input, therefore more of the output" is only sound if the input
> is the thing currently limiting the rate, and if nothing else is
> attached to it. Here, something else is very much attached to it.
