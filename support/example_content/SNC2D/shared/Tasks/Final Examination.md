> [!abstract] At a glance
> Individual · two and a half hours, in the examination period · written,
> with a periodic table and a calculator · all four units, weighted
> evenly, because they are worth the same

## What it is for

Most of what you hand in this year is done with a partner, at a bench,
with time to look things up. This is the one piece of evidence that is
unambiguously yours. In Grade 10 it also does something else: it is
practice at a kind of assessment that Grade 11 and 12 courses will use
constantly, met here where the stakes are lower.

## What is on it

| Part | Roughly | What it asks you to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Biology | 25% | The cell cycle and why mitosis matters; specialisation into tissues, organs, and systems; what goes wrong when a system fails |
| B. Chemistry | 25% | Names and formulas of binary and polyatomic compounds; balancing; reaction types; the evidence that a chemical change happened |
| C. Climate | 25% | The components of the climate system; the natural greenhouse effect and how it differs from the human-caused one; reading a real graph honestly |
| D. Optics | 25% | Reflection, refraction, and lenses; predicting an image; one calculation |

Each part has short answers, one diagram, and one longer explanation.
Two parts include data or a graph you have not seen before.

## What to expect, precisely

- **Explain, do not only name.** "Mitosis makes new cells" earns little;
  saying why identical copies matter for growth and repair earns full
  marks.
- **Diagrams are marked**: labelled, ruled where they should be, and
  drawn to show the thing rather than decorated.
  [[Drawing Scientific Diagrams]] is the standard.
- **The graph question** gives you a real record. You will describe what
  it shows, then say what it does NOT show — the habit from
  [[Reading a Graph Honestly]], which is where most of those marks are.
- **Balancing is expected to balance.** It is the cheapest mark on the
  paper and the most commonly dropped.
- **The optics calculation** uses the thin lens equation, given on the
  paper. Units and signs matter.

## How to prepare

1. **Redo, do not reread.** Five questions from each unit's practice set,
   cold: [[Cells and Systems Practice]], [[Formulas and Names Practice]],
   [[Balancing Practice]], [[Climate Data Practice]],
   [[Lenses Practice]].
2. **Redraw the diagrams from memory** — a cell, a system, a ray diagram,
   the greenhouse effect. If you cannot draw it, you cannot yet explain
   it.
3. **Re-read your own investigations.** Your [[Science Journal]] entries
   about what went wrong at the bench are the most efficient revision
   there is.
4. **Practise saying the explanations out loud.** Every long-answer mark
   on this paper is a sentence you could have said to somebody.
5. **Bring questions to the review classes.** This page is what is on
   the examination.

> [!tip] In the two and a half hours
> Do the part you find easiest first, to get moving, then the hardest
> while you are warm but not tired. If an explanation stalls, write what
> you DO know about it — partial explanations earn partial marks, and a
> blank space earns none.

## How this is assessed

Against the same expectations as everything else. Per [[How Marks Work]],
this examination is part of the final 30% of the course mark alongside
[[The Science Showcase]], so that neither one afternoon nor one
investigation decides your grade alone.
