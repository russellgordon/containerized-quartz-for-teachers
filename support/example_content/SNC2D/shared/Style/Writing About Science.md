Scientific writing is not fancy writing. It is writing that cannot be
misunderstood, by a reader who was not in the room and who is looking
for the weak spot.

## Say what you measured, not what you felt

> ❌ The reaction went really fast and got super hot.
>
> ✅ The temperature rose from 21 °C to 47 °C in 30 seconds.

Adjectives are the reader's job. Give them the number and let them
decide whether it was fast.

## Keep observation and interpretation in separate sentences

An observation is what an instrument or your senses recorded. An
interpretation is what you think it means. Mixing them is how a shaky
inference gets smuggled in as a fact.

> **Observation:** Bubbles formed on the magnesium and the tube became
> warm to the touch.
>
> **Interpretation:** A gas was produced and energy was released,
> which together suggest a chemical change rather than a physical one.

## Claim what your data supports, and no more

| Too strong | Honest |
| --- | --- |
| This proves light intensity controls the rate. | These results support the prediction that a higher intensity increases the rate. |
| The results were wrong. | The results did not match the prediction; the lamp also warmed the water, which was not controlled. |
| The unknown was an acid. | The unknown turned blue litmus red, which is consistent with an acid; a pH measurement would confirm it. |

> [!important] "Prove" is not a science word
> Evidence *supports*, *is consistent with*, or *fails to support*. One
> lab period proves nothing at all, and a reader who sees "proves" in
> your first paragraph trusts everything after it a little less. This
> is the single easiest mark in the course to keep.

## Explain the mechanism, not just the pattern

This is the Grade 10 move, and it is the difference between a level 3
report and a level 4 one.

> **Pattern:** The container with more carbon dioxide warmed faster
> under the lamp.
>
> **Mechanism:** Carbon dioxide absorbs infrared radiation emitted by
> the warmed surface and re-emits it in all directions, so some energy
> that would otherwise have left the container is returned to it,
> raising the rate at which its temperature climbs.

A mechanism can be checked, argued with, and shown to be wrong. A
pattern can only be repeated.

## Write chemistry so it can be read

Formulas need their subscripts and charges to mean anything.
$\ce{H2O}$ is water; H2O is a typing accident that happens to
be readable. Reactions get a proper arrow, states get their brackets,
and ions get their charges:

$$\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$$

$$\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$$

Sulfate is $\ce{SO4^2-}$, not SO4-2. The charge sits after the
subscript and the number comes before the sign. Getting this right is
not fussiness — $\ce{SO4^2-}$ and $\ce{SO3^2-}$ are different
substances and the difference is one character.

## Units, always

A number without a unit is not an answer. 4.2 what — seconds, grams,
centimetres, degrees? And a unit without a number is a category, not a
measurement.

## Passive voice is fine here

"The solution was heated to 40 °C" keeps the reader's attention on the
procedure rather than on who was holding the beaker. Scientific method
sections are one of the few kinds of writing where that is the right
choice, so ignore anybody who tells you to purge it.
