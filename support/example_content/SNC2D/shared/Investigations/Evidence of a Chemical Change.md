Six stations are set up around the room. At every one of them,
something happens. At only some of them is the stuff on the bench
afterwards **different stuff** from what was there before, and telling
those two situations apart is the whole of today.

## What you are trying to find out

"A chemical change makes a new substance." Fine — but you cannot see a
substance's identity. You see bubbles, colours, warmth, cloudiness. So
the real question is the awkward one underneath:

**Which observations actually distinguish a new substance from the same
substances rearranged, dissolved, or moved?**

By the end you should have a ranked list of kinds of evidence, and a
reason for the ranking that is better than "the textbook says so",
because no textbook has said anything yet.

## What you have to work with

Each station has a card naming exactly what to combine and how much.
Everything you need is at the station.

- Baking soda and vinegar
- Two clear solutions that turn cloudy when combined
- A clean iron nail and a blue copper(II) sulfate solution
- Steel wool, vinegar, and a thermometer, left to stand through the
  period
- A salt that warms the water as it dissolves
- A small dish of candle wax in a warm water bath

Two of those six are on the bench specifically to catch you. That is
not a trick; it is the point of the lesson.

Your design job, and it is the graded thinking here: **pick the station
whose classification you are least sure of, and design a follow-up
test** that would separate "a new substance was made" from "the same
substance is still there, in a different arrangement". Write the test,
name the result that would settle it each way, and have it checked
before you run anything.

## Safety

> [!danger] The rule that matters more than all the others today
> **Combine only what your station card names, in the amounts it names.
> Nothing else, ever, and nothing brought from home.**
>
> Substances that are each harmless on their own can produce a
> genuinely toxic gas when they are put together. There is no way to
> tell by looking, which is exactly why the rule is absolute rather
> than a matter of judgement. Leftovers are not combined, and no unused
> chemical goes back into a stock bottle.
>
> - Eye protection on from the first station to the last of the
>   cleanup. Hair tied back, sleeves secured, closed-toe shoes.
> - **Copper(II) sulfate solution is harmful if swallowed and irritates
>   skin and eyes.** Handle it over the tray, wash any splash off
>   immediately with plenty of water, and wash your hands before you
>   leave the room.
> - Vinegar is dilute acetic acid. It stings in an eye. Goggles stay
>   on.
> - **Waft** an odour toward your nose with your hand. Never put your
>   face over a container and inhale.
> - Nothing is tasted, eaten, or drunk. The wax is not a candle you
>   light, and there is no open flame in this room today.
> - Waste goes in the labelled container at the station, not down the
>   sink, unless the card says otherwise.
> - Tell me about every spill and splash the moment it happens, however
>   small it looks.

Label reading and hazard symbols: [[Lab Safety and WHMIS]].

## The prediction you write first

Before you start, and in writing:

1. For **each** station, one sentence: chemical change, not a chemical
   change, or genuinely unsure — and the observation you are betting
   on.
2. A single sentence naming the **kind of evidence you currently trust
   most**, and why.

You will hand these in unaltered. Changing a prediction after the fact
does not improve your mark and it destroys the only record of how your
thinking moved. See [[Mistakes Are Data]].

## What to collect

| Station | What you observed, in detail | Your call | The evidence you based it on | Confidence, 1–5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | | |

"Fizzed" is thin. "Fine bubbles rose steadily from the whole surface
for about a minute, then stopped, and the liquid stayed clear" is data
somebody else can reason with. Record temperature where a thermometer
is provided, with units, and record the starting value as well as the
final one.

For your chosen station, add: the follow-up test you designed, what
happened, and whether it moved your confidence.

> [!question]- Three observations that fool almost everybody
> **Bubbles.** A gas leaving a liquid is not proof of a reaction. Warm
> a soft drink and it fizzes; nothing new was made, dissolved gas
> simply became less soluble and left.
>
> **Colour.** Mixing a blue solution with a yellow one gives a green
> one, and every molecule that went in is still exactly what it was.
> Colour change is worth something only when nothing coloured was
> added.
>
> **Temperature.** Some salts warm the water noticeably as they
> dissolve and others cool it, and dissolving is not a chemical change.
> Energy moving does not by itself mean bonds were rearranged into new
> substances.
>
> Which leaves a hard question for the discussion: if each of the
> classic clues can be produced without a chemical change, what makes a
> *combination* of them convincing, and is "convincing" the same thing
> as "proven"?

## What to bring to the consolidation discussion

- Your ranked list of kinds of evidence, strongest to weakest, with the
  reason for the top one and the reason for the bottom one.
- The station you and your partner disagreed about, and what each of
  you was weighing.
- Your designed test, and whether it worked. A test that failed to
  separate the two possibilities is a genuinely useful contribution,
  and I will ask for one.

## What you should not claim

- **No single observation on this bench proves a chemical change.** At
  best you have a combination that is hard to explain any other way —
  which is the standard argued for in [[What Counts as Evidence]], and
  it is weaker than "proof" and stronger than "a guess".
- **You did not identify a single product.** A white solid is not
  "calcium carbonate" because it looks like chalk. You observed that a
  solid formed.
- **Reversibility is a clue, not a rule.** Some changes that are easy
  to reverse are chemical, and some that are hard to reverse are not.
- **You watched one trial at each station**, and at several of them you
  arrived partway through somebody else's.
