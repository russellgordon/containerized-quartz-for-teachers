Four reactions, and a tray of ordinary household liquids. Nobody is
going to tell you the names of the families today until after you have
proposed your own.

## What you are trying to find out

Chemists sort reactions into a small number of families, and the
families are not arbitrary — they describe **what happened to the
substances**, not what it looked like. Two questions:

1. Given four reactions and the evidence in front of you, can you sort
   them into groups and defend the boundaries you drew?
2. Separately: can you classify a set of everyday solutions as acidic,
   basic, or neutral, and say what "acidic" is a claim about?

The sorting is the assessment. A grouping you can defend from evidence
beats a grouping that happens to match the textbook for reasons you
cannot state.

## What you have to work with

**Reaction A** — a strip of magnesium ribbon burned in air. This one is
a **demonstration**, run by me, with the room at a distance. You record
the before and after.

**Reaction B** — the dilute hydrogen peroxide provided, with a yeast
suspension added, and a glowing splint held at the mouth of the tube.

**Reaction C** — a clean zinc strip or iron nail left standing in blue
copper(II) sulfate solution.

**Reaction D** — sodium carbonate solution added to calcium chloride
solution.

**The pH tray** — vinegar, lemon juice, milk, a baking soda solution, a
dilute soap solution, distilled water, and a soft drink. All in
labelled containers, all provided by the school.

Your design work, submitted and checked before you run anything:

- A **written safety plan** for the three reactions you run yourself:
  the hazard at each, the control you will use, and what you would do
  if it went wrong. This is the part I check first and the part I will
  send back.
- The **feature you will sort on**. Number of reactants and products?
  Whether an element appears alone on one side? What is exchanged?
  Decide before the data seduces you.
- For the pH tray: the **order** you will test in, and how you will
  avoid carrying one liquid into the next.

## Safety

> [!danger] Burning magnesium, oxygen, and a bench of acids
> - **Never look directly at burning magnesium.** It burns with an
>   intense white light that includes ultraviolet, and staring at it can
>   damage your eyes. Watch from your seat, from an angle, and take my
>   word for the colour of the flame. This one is a demonstration for
>   exactly this reason.
> - The splint at Reaction B involves a small flame at a station with a
>   heat-proof mat, lit **only when I am standing there**. Long hair
>   tied back, sleeves secured, nothing loose over the bench. **A tube
>   with more oxygen in it than air burns things faster than you
>   expect.**
> - Hot glassware looks exactly like cold glassware. Assume.
> - **Copper(II) sulfate is harmful if swallowed and irritates skin and
>   eyes.** Over the tray, wash splashes off at once, wash hands before
>   leaving.
> - Use only the dilute hydrogen peroxide provided. Concentrated
>   peroxide is a different substance in every way that matters and is
>   not in this room.
> - **Combine only what is listed above.** Not the leftovers, not two
>   pH-tray liquids, not "just to see". Household products that are
>   harmless separately can make a toxic gas together. Nothing comes
>   from home, and cleaning products — drain cleaner, oven cleaner,
>   bleach — are not on the tray and are not permitted anywhere near
>   this investigation.
> - Eye protection stays on until every station is cleared. Waft
>   odours; never inhale over a container. Never pipette by mouth.
> - Waste into the labelled containers. Report every incident straight
>   away, however small.

## The prediction you write first

1. For each of B, C, and D: what you expect to observe, and what you
   expect to be **present at the end that was not present at the
   start**.
2. Your proposed sorting feature, in one sentence, before any data.
3. For the pH tray: rank the seven liquids from most acidic to most
   basic **before testing**, and keep the ranking.

The third one is worth doing honestly. Nearly everybody puts one liquid
badly out of place, and which one you got wrong is more instructive
than the six you got right.

Read the strip against the chart in the **same light** each time, and
record the reading you actually matched, not the one you were hoping
for. Note the resolution of your strips — most classroom strips cannot
resolve better than about half a pH unit, and some are coarser.

> [!example]- One worked sort, so you can see the standard expected
> Suppose you had a fifth reaction: a clean iron nail in a solution of
> silver nitrate, with grey crystals growing on the nail and the
> solution slowly turning pale.
>
> A weak sort: "it changed colour, so it goes with C."
>
> A strong sort: "an element that started on its own — the iron — is
> now combined, and something that started combined is now sitting on
> the nail as a solid element. One element has taken the place of
> another. That is the same *move* as C, even though C is blue and this
> is not, and it is a different move from D, where two compounds
> swapped partners and neither element was ever alone."
>
> The second one names what happened to the substances. Colour was
> evidence that something happened; it was not the basis of the group.

## What to bring to the consolidation discussion

- Your groups, your boundaries, and the reaction that was hardest to
  place.
- A general pattern for each group, written with words rather than
  formulas: "an element plus an element gives one compound", and so on.
- One place your pH ranking was wrong, and your account of why you
  expected otherwise.

## What you should not claim

- **A coating on a nail is not "copper".** You observed a reddish solid
  deposited and a solution fading. Identifying that solid takes more
  than looking at it.
- **A precipitate is not identified by being white.** Plenty of
  substances are white and insoluble.
- **A glowing splint relighting is evidence for oxygen**, not proof of
  it, and it certainly does not tell you how much.
- **pH strips are coarse and the colour match is a judgement.** Two
  liquids half a unit apart cannot be ranked against each other by
  your data, and a group that reports them as different is claiming
  more than it measured.
- **Four reactions is a small sample to build a taxonomy on.** Your
  families are a proposal that survived four cases, which is how
  classification schemes start and not how they finish.
