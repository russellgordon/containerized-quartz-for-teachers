Between 1914 and 1929 Canada was a country of immigrants writing laws about
which immigrants it wanted. Read those laws together with the everyday
discrimination around them and you can see what the word "Canadian" was
actually doing: not describing everyone who lived here, but marking out who
would be treated as belonging.

## The law drew the line first

The *Chinese Immigration Act* of 1923 replaced the head tax with something
blunter — near-total exclusion. Chinese Canadians already here had to
register, and families were split for a generation. Communities marked the
day it came into force as Humiliation Day and refused to celebrate Dominion
Day for decades.

Immigration law more broadly let Cabinet bar entry by nationality,
occupation, or "unsuitability" to the climate — deliberately vague language
that allowed officials to exclude on grounds they never had to state
publicly. Britain and northern Europe were preferred; everyone else was
ranked below.

| Group | What the state did |
| --- | --- |
| Chinese Canadians | Head tax, then near-total exclusion from 1923 |
| Black Canadians | Segregated schooling; recruiters refusing enlistment |
| Jewish Canadians | University quotas; covenants barring property |
| South Asian Canadians | Rules designed to make lawful arrival impossible |
| Ukrainian Canadians | Registration, internment, loss of the vote in 1917 |

## And organized racism followed it

The Ku Klux Klan was active in Canada in the 1920s, most strongly in
Saskatchewan, where it recruited openly against Catholics, Jews and
immigrants from eastern Europe. It is a mistake to file this as an American
story that leaked over the border. It found Canadian members because it was
saying things Canadian audiences already believed.

The No. 2 Construction Battalion is the sharpest example of how the line was
drawn. Black men who volunteered were repeatedly turned away. When a unit was
finally authorized it was segregated and assigned labour rather than combat.
Canada accepted the service and withheld the equality.

## What to do with this

Do not read the discrimination as a background condition. It was made, by
named people, in identifiable laws, and it was resisted at every stage by the
people it targeted. Track how these rules move across the century in
[[Immigration Policy Over a Century]], and use [[Historical Significance]] to
decide which measures mattered most, and to whom.
