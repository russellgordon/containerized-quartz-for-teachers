An ideology is a connected set of beliefs about how society should be
organized and who should hold power. In the 1930s several of them promised to
end the economic disaster, and several of them located the cause of that
disaster in a group of people. It is the second move that turns an ideology
into a machine for producing violence.

## What was on offer

**Fascism** rejected democracy outright, held that a nation is a single
organism led by one man, and treated internal difference as a disease.
**Nazism** added a racial theory: that humanity is divided into ranked races,
that the German nation must be purified of others, and above all that Jews
were an enemy within. **Stalinism** claimed to build socialism and in
practice built a totalitarian state that starved, deported and shot its own
populations. Liberal democracy, in the same decade, was widely described as
exhausted and slow.

Underneath the political ideologies ran social ones. **Antisemitism** had
centuries of European history behind it and was reactivated with new
pseudoscientific language. **Eugenics** — the claim that human populations
should be improved by controlling who reproduces — was mainstream, taught in
universities, and written into law in Canada as well as Germany.

## In Canada, not only elsewhere

Adrien Arcand founded an openly antisemitic, Nazi-modelled party in Quebec
and published newspapers to promote it. "Swastika clubs" harassed Jews in
Toronto, and a baseball game at Christie Pits in 1933 turned into hours of
street fighting after a swastika banner was displayed. Restrictive covenants
kept Jews out of neighbourhoods, universities capped Jewish admissions, and
signs reading "No Jews" appeared in shop windows. Quebec's Padlock Act let
the government seal any premises it declared to be used for communist
purposes, without a trial, and it was used against a far wider range of
people than communists.

> [!warning] "Extreme" is not the same as "foreign"
> The temptation is to treat extreme ideology as something that happened in
> Europe and was reported here. It was not. Eugenic sterilization was
> Canadian law. Antisemitic exclusion was Canadian practice. Canada's own
> system of racial administration under the *Indian Act* was studied by
> officials in Nazi Germany. You can hold that fact and still refuse false
> equivalence — the point is not that Canada was Germany, but that the ideas
> were in the water everywhere, and Canadian institutions drank.

Three questions will get you a long way with any ideology. Who does it say
belongs, and who does it say does not? What does it promise to people who
feel they have lost something? And what does it authorize — what may now be
done to the group it has defined as the problem? Propaganda is the machinery
that carries those answers, and it works best on people who are frightened
and want an explanation.

Bring this to [[The Holocaust and Canada's Response]], and use
[[Historical Perspective]] to understand why these ideas persuaded people
without conceding that they were right.
