The hardest of the four concepts, because it asks you to hold two things at
once: that people in the past were working from beliefs that were not yours,
and that this does not make every decision they made excusable. The
curriculum sets both halves out. It "requires students to analyze past
actions, events, developments, and issues within the context of the time in
which they occurred," which means "understanding the social, cultural,
political, economic, and intellectual context, and the personal values and
beliefs, that shaped people's lives and actions."

Then the sentence students quote: "Students need to be conscious of not
imposing today's values and ethical standards on the past."

And the sentence they skip: "Students also learn that, in any given
historical period, people may have diverse perspectives on the same event,
development, or issue."

## Two errors, and the second is the common one

**Presentism** is judging 1923 by the standards of 2026 and stopping there.
It produces work that is confident and empty, because it never explains
anything — it only scores the past against a test the past never sat.

> [!warning] "Everyone thought that back then" is a claim about the past
> And it is a claim the record usually refutes. Peter Henderson Bryce
> published *The Story of a National Crime* in 1922 about deaths in
> residential schools. Henri Bourassa argued against Canada's imperial
> commitments while the recruiting posters were still up. The Canadian
> Jewish Congress and the member of Parliament A. A. Heaps pressed Mackenzie
> King to reopen immigration to refugees in the 1930s. If you write that
> nobody knew better, you owe your reader the search that failed to find
> them. Usually the search was never made.

The second error matters more than the first, because it launders a
decision into a climate. Decisions had authors, and authors had critics who
were alive at the time and left documents.

## Perspective is a method, not a mood

Reconstructing a perspective is research, not sympathy. Find out what the
writer could have known, who paid them, whom they were addressing, and what
their audience already took for granted. Then you can say what a document is
doing without either endorsing it or shouting at it.

**The question to put to a source:** *what did the writer assume the reader
already believed?* An editorial that does not bother to argue a point is
telling you that the point was safe. The unargued assumptions are the
period's actual values, and they are usually the most useful thing on the
page.

Take it into [[Ideologies in a Fearful Decade]], apply it under pressure in
[[Could Canada Have Done More?]], and treat it as a duty in
[[Oral History and Testimony]].
