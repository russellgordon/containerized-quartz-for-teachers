Rights in Canada were not granted from above once the country grew up. Every
one of them was demanded, usually for years, usually by people who paid for
the demand. This page is about the demanding.

## Anti-Black racism, and organized Black resistance to it

Segregation in Canada was real. Black Canadians were refused service in
restaurants and hotels, refused jobs and mortgages, and educated in separate
schools in parts of Ontario and Nova Scotia into the second half of the
twentieth century. It was rarely written into law in the American manner,
which made it harder to challenge, not easier — a business could simply claim
a right to choose its customers. Black communities challenged it anyway, and
the record of that work is specific.

- **Viola Desmond** refused to leave the whites-only section of a New Glasgow
  theatre in 1946 and was prosecuted, in the end, over a one-cent difference
  in ticket tax. The Nova Scotia Association for the Advancement of Coloured
  People took up her case; the province pardoned her posthumously in 2010,
  and she now appears on the ten-dollar note.
- **Hugh Burnett** and the National Unity Association of Chatham, Dresden and
  North Buxton campaigned through the late 1940s and 1950s against businesses
  that refused Black customers, documenting refusals until Ontario
  legislated. The province's fair employment and fair accommodation statutes
  came out of that pressure and became its Human Rights Code in 1962, the
  first in Canada.
- **Sleeping car porters**, long confined to the only railway job open to
  Black men, organized. Stanley Grizzle and the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car
  Porters won contract protections, then pushed against the immigration and
  employment rules that kept the job closed at the top.
- The **Negro Citizenship Association** took the discriminatory operation of
  immigration rules to Ottawa, arguing that Caribbean applicants were
  excluded by administrative practice rather than by any defensible law.
- Students at **Sir George Williams University** in Montreal occupied the
  computer centre in 1969 after complaints of racist grading went nowhere.
  The occupation ended in fire, damage and mass arrests, and forced Canadian
  universities to build complaint procedures they had not had.

> [!note] Do not narrate this as a series of firsts
> Leonard Braithwaite's election to the Ontario legislature in 1963 and
> Lincoln Alexander's election to Parliament in 1968 were genuinely
> significant. But a list of firsts quietly implies the barrier fell when the
> first person got through it. It did not. Ask instead what organization,
> what argument and what evidence made each opening possible — and what
> stayed closed.

Meanwhile Halifax expropriated and demolished **Africville** through the
1960s, moving residents out of a community denied water, sewers and paving
for a century, and calling it slum clearance. The city apologized in 2010.

The same decades carried the second-wave women's movement, which pressed a
royal commission on the status of women and built a national lobby from its
report; durable labour bargaining rights; new environmental organizations;
Quebec nationalism; and Indigenous political organizing. They did not always
agree, which is part of what makes the period worth studying.
