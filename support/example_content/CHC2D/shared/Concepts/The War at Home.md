The Second World War ended the Depression in Canada. Factories that had sat
idle ran three shifts, and anyone who wanted work could find it. That is the
cheerful half of the story, and it is true. The other half is that the same
emergency powers that organized the economy were used to imprison Canadian
citizens who had committed no offence.

## An economy directed from Ottawa

The federal government set prices, rationed sugar, butter, meat, coffee, tea
and gasoline, controlled wages, directed which factories made what, and
borrowed enormously from ordinary people through Victory Bond campaigns.
Women moved into industrial work in very large numbers — aircraft, munitions,
ships, vehicles — and the government opened day nurseries and adjusted tax
rules to make that possible. Both the nurseries and the tax changes were
withdrawn afterwards. Ask what that sequence says about what the opening had
been for.

Mackenzie King had promised no conscription for overseas service. As
casualties mounted he held a national plebiscite in 1942 asking to be
released from that promise. The country as a whole said yes; Quebec voted
heavily no. His formula — "not necessarily conscription, but conscription if
necessary" — is mocked as evasion, and it was also a fairly precise
description of a politician trying to hold a country together.

## Japanese Canadians

In 1942 the federal government used the *War Measures Act* to remove people
of Japanese descent from the British Columbia coast — Parks Canada puts the
number at 22,000, the Canadian Museum for Human Rights at some 23,000.
The majority were Canadian citizens, most born here. They were sent to
camps in the interior, to road work, or to sugar beet farms on the Prairies.
Their homes, businesses, farms and fishing boats were seized and sold, and
the proceeds were used to pay the costs of their own detention. After the war
they were pressured to move east of the Rockies or to "repatriate" to Japan,
a country many had never seen. Restrictions were not fully lifted until 1949.

> [!note] No one was ever charged
> The RCMP and the military both advised that Japanese Canadians posed no
> security threat. The removal went ahead anyway, driven by long-standing
> racism on the coast and by politicians who found it useful. In 1988 the
> federal government formally acknowledged the injustice, apologized and
> agreed to compensation — forty-six years later, when many of those interned
> as adults were already dead.

Italian and German Canadians were also interned in smaller numbers, some for
political association and some on no clear grounds at all. Jewish refugees
from Germany and Austria who reached Britain were shipped to Canada and held
in camps *alongside* actual Nazi prisoners — the same fences for the
persecuted and the persecutor. Meanwhile censorship shaped what Canadians
read, and propaganda shaped how they understood it.
