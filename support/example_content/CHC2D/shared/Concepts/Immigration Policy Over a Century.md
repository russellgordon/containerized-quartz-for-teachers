Immigration is the one policy area where you can watch Canada change its
mind in public, in law, and in numbers, across the whole span of this
course. It is also where the gap between what a government said and what it
did is easiest to measure, because the arrivals were counted every year.

The counting was done by the Dominion Bureau of Statistics and survives in
*Historical Statistics of Canada*, Series A350, still published free by
Statistics Canada as catalogue 11-516-X
([Section A](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11-516-x/sectiona/4147436-eng.htm),
accessed August 2026).

## Four eras, and what the door did

| Era | What the policy did | Arrivals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1919–1929 | Cabinet given power to bar entry by nationality, occupation, or unsuitability to the climate; the *Chinese Immigration Act* of 1923 made exclusion near-total | 164,993 in 1929 |
| 1930–1939 | Doors shut on economic grounds and kept shut on others; "none is too many"; fewer than 5,000 Jewish refugees admitted between 1933 and 1945 | 27,530 in 1931; 11,277 in 1935 |
| 1945–1966 | Displaced persons admitted, sponsorship widened, the domestic worker scheme recruited women from the Caribbean | 194,391 in 1951; 282,164 in 1957 |
| 1967 onward | A points system replaced national preference with scored criteria — schooling, skills, language, arranged work | 104,111 in 1960, before the change |

Hold the 1935 figure against the population: Canada had 10,376,786 people in
the 1931 census and admitted 11,277 newcomers four years later. That is not
a slowdown. That is a closed country, and the years in which it was closed
are the years in which people were trying hardest to leave Europe.

## What the points system did and did not fix

Removing named nationalities from the statute was a real change: after 1967
an applicant was scored rather than ranked by origin. But criteria carry
their own preferences. Schooling and language tests favour applicants from
places where the schooling and the language were already British or French,
and the domestic worker scheme had already shown what happens when a
country admits people for their labour while making their status
conditional.

Read the earliest era in [[Who Counted as Canadian]], the sharpest single
refusal in [[Could Canada Have Done More?]], and the question of who was
admitted and on what terms in [[Who Was Canada Willing to Admit?]].
