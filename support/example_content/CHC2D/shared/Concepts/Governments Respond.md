Faced with a collapse nobody had a theory for, Canadian governments tried
almost everything in sequence: doing very little, doing something loudly, and
building institutions that outlasted the crisis. Judging them means
separating what a policy was meant to do from what it did.

## The federal sequence

```mermaid
flowchart TD
  A[Relief left to provinces<br/>and municipalities] --> B[Federal relief camps]
  B --> C[On-to-Ottawa Trek, 1935<br/>halted at Regina]
  C --> D[Bennett's "New Deal"<br/>broadcast to the country]
  D --> E[Much struck down as<br/>outside federal power]
  E --> F[Bank of Canada<br/>a permanent institution]
```

Relief was constitutionally provincial and municipal, and municipalities went
broke providing it. When Ottawa acted, it built camps rather than programs.
Men from the camps rode freight trains east in the summer of 1935 to put
their case to the government; the trek was halted at Regina, and the
confrontation there ended in violence and arrests.

R. B. Bennett's late-term reform package — minimum wages, hours limits,
unemployment insurance — was announced in radio addresses. Courts then held
that much of it invaded provincial jurisdiction. Remember that: in Canada,
"the government should do something" runs immediately into the question of
*which* government may. The lasting change was institutional. The *Bank of
Canada Act* received royal assent on 3 July 1934 and the Bank opened in March
1935, giving the country a central bank for the first time. A national public
broadcaster followed.

The Depression also produced parties the older ones could not absorb. The
Co-operative Commonwealth Federation argued for public ownership and social
insurance; Social Credit, in Alberta, argued that the money supply itself was
the problem. Both were protest movements that became governments.

## Policy aimed at Indigenous peoples

The same decade produced a separate body of policy for First Nations, Métis
and Inuit peoples, and it ran in the opposite direction from relief.
Residential schools kept operating. Indian agents' powers were extended.
Federal franchise legislation continued to exclude status Indians. Alberta
and later British Columbia passed sexual sterilization statutes under which
Indigenous people were sterilized far out of proportion to their share of the
population; Alberta's ran for more than four decades before repeal in the
1970s. Alberta's Ewing Commission examined Métis conditions and led to the
first provincial legislation setting land aside for Métis settlements — which
Métis historians read as recognition and containment at once.

In the North, Ottawa began issuing numbered identification discs to Inuit
because officials would not learn Inuit naming practices. A state that could
not be bothered to record your name assigned you a number instead, and that
number followed you through the files for decades.

Judge these responses against each other in [[The Thirties Case]], and use
[[Cause and Consequence]] to separate intention from effect.
