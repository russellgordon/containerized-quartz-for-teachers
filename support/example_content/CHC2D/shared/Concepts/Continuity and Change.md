This is the concept students find easiest to name and hardest to use well,
because it looks like a description and is actually a comparison. The
curriculum says so directly: it "requires students to determine what has
stayed the same and what has changed over a period of time," and students
"make judgements about continuity and change by making comparisons between
some point in the past and the present, or between two points in the past."

A comparison needs two dates. If you cannot name both, you are not yet
making the judgement — you are describing one moment and calling it change.

## What the curriculum says you may compare

The list is broader than most students assume: "ways of life, political
policies, economic practices, relationship with the environment, social
values and beliefs, and so on." So a change in what a job paid counts, and
so does a change in what people believed a woman's work was worth.

| What you compare | The earlier point | The later point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The *Indian Act* | Enacted 1876, consolidating earlier colonial law | Never repealed; in force today as R.S.C. 1985, c. I-5 |
| Ceremony | Potlatch and Sun Dance prohibited from 1885 | Those prohibitions removed in the 1951 revisions |
| The federal vote | Denied to status First Nations people unless they gave up status | Available without that condition from the early 1960s |
| Equality in law | The Charter proclaimed 17 April 1982 | Section 15 equality rights in force only from 17 April 1985 |

Look at the first row and the last together. The *Indian Act* has been
amended repeatedly and has never gone away: that is continuity, and it is a
finding, not the absence of one. The Charter row is the opposite trap — a
famous date that is not the date the thing you care about took effect.

## Change has a direction, a speed, and winners

Once you have established that something changed, three questions remain.
**In which direction** — and for whom was that direction an improvement?
**How fast** — a shift over two years and a shift over forty are different
kinds of history. **Who noticed** — a change invisible to most of the
country can be the central fact of a decade for one community.

**The question to put to a source:** *compared with when, exactly?* Ask it
of any document that claims something is new, modern, unprecedented, or
back to normal. A 1946 advertisement announcing a new era for the Canadian
housewife is evidence of what somebody wanted believed in 1946; whether
anything changed is settled by putting it beside 1939.
