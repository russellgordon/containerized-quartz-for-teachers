Everything that happened is not equally worth studying, and deciding what is
— that is the whole of this concept. The Ontario curriculum states it
plainly: historical significance "requires students to determine the
importance of something (e.g., an issue, event, development, person, place,
interaction) in the past," and that importance "is determined generally by
the impact of something on a group of people and whether its effects are
long lasting."

Read that sentence twice. It carries two tests, and the paragraph it comes
from adds two more that students routinely skip.

## Four tests, in the Ministry's own words

**Impact on a group of people.** How many, and how deeply? A measure that
changed where a family could live, what they were paid, or whether they
could vote outweighs one that filled a week of newspapers and then stopped.

**Whether its effects are long lasting.** Did anything still differ ten
years later? Fifty? The *Chinese Immigration Act* of 1923 split families for
a generation. That is durability, and you can show it.

**Significant for whom.** The curriculum is blunt: "something that is
historically significant for one group may not be significant for another."
Vimy Ridge and Regulation 17 fall within three years of each other and are
not ranked the same way in every part of this country.

**Relevance now.** Significance "may also be determined by the relevance of
something from the past, including how it connects to a current issue or
event." That is not permission to judge the past by today's standards — see
[[Historical Perspective]] — but it is permission to say why a thing still
matters.

> [!question] The question to put to a source
> **Who was affected by this, how deeply, for how long — and who is saying
> so?** Ask it of one document at a time. A 1917 recruiting poster tells you
> whom the government thought worth persuading. A band council's letter of
> protest tells you whom a policy landed on. Neither answers the question by
> itself, and the distance between the two is usually where your argument
> lives.

## Significance is argued, not discovered

No source announces its own importance. You assign it, using criteria you
can state out loud, and a reader should be able to disagree with your
criteria rather than guess at them. Write them down before you rank
anything.

That is the move [[Building an Argument]] runs on, and the one
[[What Should Be Remembered Here|What Should Be Remembered Here?]] asks you to make about your own place.
