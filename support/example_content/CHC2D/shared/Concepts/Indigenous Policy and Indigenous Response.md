Between 1945 and 1982 Canadian policy toward First Nations, Métis and Inuit
peoples changed its language completely and its purpose very little. The word
"assimilation" left official use; the programs pursuing it did not. What did
change is that Indigenous nations built organizations able to stop a federal
policy — and did.

## What the state did

The *Indian Act* was overhauled in 1951. Some of the worst prohibitions went,
including the ban on hiring lawyers to pursue land claims. Others stayed: the
provision stripping status from a First Nations woman who married a man
without status stood for another three decades, taking her children's status
with hers. In the North, Ottawa moved Inuit families to the far High Arctic,
partly to assert sovereignty, and apologized for it in 2010.

Residential schools operated through this entire period. Alongside them, from
the late 1950s, child welfare authorities removed Indigenous children into
non-Indigenous foster and adoptive homes in numbers large enough that the
practice has its own name, the Sixties Scoop. The mechanism moved from the
school to the case file; the outcome did not.

> [!warning] The death toll: say exactly what each body says
> The Truth and Reconciliation Commission identified **3,201** deaths, and
> said in its own report that "it is probable that not all residential school
> deaths have been recorded." The National Centre for Truth and
> Reconciliation reported **4,141** verified deaths as of 2025. Those are two
> bodies at two dates; the second is higher because the work continued, not
> because the first was wrong. Do not round either figure, do not add them,
> and do not repeat larger numbers that come from remarks rather than
> findings. Where ground-penetrating radar has been used at school sites,
> what it detects are anomalies consistent with possible burials, not
> identified remains. Report the count as incomplete, because the records are.

The end date is contested too. The last federally run school is commonly said
to have closed in 1996, but Kivalliq Hall in Rankin Inlet was recognised as a
residential school only in 2019, making 1997 defensible. Give both, and say
why they differ.

## What Indigenous peoples did

Status Indians won the federal vote in 1960 without surrendering status.
Then, in 1969, the federal government proposed a White Paper that would have
abolished Indian status, dissolved the *Indian Act* and ended the treaty
relationship, presenting this as equality. The response was immediate and
organized: Harold Cardinal and the Indian Chiefs of Alberta answered with
*Citizens Plus*, arguing that treaties were not a disability to be removed
but rights to be honoured. The White Paper was withdrawn — a federal
government made to reverse itself, and the turning point of the period.

What followed built on it. The National Indian Brotherhood took over the
argument about education. The Calder case pushed the Supreme Court to
acknowledge that Aboriginal title had existed in Canadian law, opening modern
treaty negotiation. The James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement of 1975 came
out of Cree and Inuit litigation against a hydro project already being built.
And in 1982 section 35 recognised existing Aboriginal and treaty rights.

The National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation holds and publishes the
records of this history; start there rather than with a summary, read
[[Historical Perspective]] first, and take a case into [[The Rights Inquiry]].
