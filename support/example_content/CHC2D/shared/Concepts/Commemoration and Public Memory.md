A monument is not the past. It is a decision made later, by people with
budgets and meetings, about which part of the past a place will keep in
front of itself. Every plaque had an author, a committee, and a year — and
those are historical facts you can research the same way you research
anything else.

The curriculum asks you to "assess the significance of public
acknowledgements and/or commemoration in Canada of past human tragedies and
human rights violations, both domestic and international." Assess, not
admire.

## Four Canadian examples with dates attached

On **22 September 1988** Prime Minister Brian Mulroney rose in the House of
Commons to apologize for the wartime internment and dispossession of
Japanese Canadians. The agreement carried $21,000 to each surviving
individual, a $12 million community fund, pardons for those wrongfully
convicted, and citizenship for those wrongfully deported.

On **11 June 2008** Prime Minister Stephen Harper apologized to former
students of residential schools. On **2 June 2015** the Truth and
Reconciliation Commission released its 94 Calls to Action at its closing
event in Ottawa; numbers 79 to 83 concern commemoration directly, and 62 to
65 concern education — this course is partly an answer to 62.

Canada recognized the Holodomor as a genocide in **2008**, the first Western
country to do so, and marks Holodomor Memorial Day on the fourth Saturday in
November. The first monument in Canada had been raised in Edmonton in 1983,
twenty-five years earlier, by a community that was not waiting for the
state. Ontario and Canada have since designated **1 August** as Emancipation
Day.

Notice the pattern in the dates. In every case the community remembered
first and the government arrived afterwards, sometimes by decades.

> [!note]- What to look for where you live (click to expand)
> Cenotaphs and the names on them, including which conflicts were added
> later. Street and school names, and when they were changed or not changed.
> Plaques with a sponsoring organization at the bottom. A day marked
> locally. And absences — a community that lived here and has no marker at
> all is also a decision, made by someone, at some point.

Two questions carry the analysis. *Who asked for this, and how long did they
have to ask?* And *what does it require of anybody* — money, a curriculum
change, a return of land, or only a ceremony?
