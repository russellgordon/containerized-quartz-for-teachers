First Nations, Métis and Inuit people served again, in a war fought against a
racial state, on behalf of a country that still administered them by race.
Veterans Affairs Canada records that by 1945 over 3,000 First Nations members
had served, together with an unknown number of Inuit and Métis recruits, and
estimates that more than 12,000 First Nations, Métis and Inuit people served
across the great conflicts of the twentieth century.

## The same counting problem, and why it matters

Enlistment papers recorded status under the *Indian Act* inconsistently, and
recorded Métis and Inuit identity almost not at all. Those figures are floors.
This is not a footnote about record-keeping: a veteran whose identity was
never recorded is a veteran the benefits system could later fail to find, and
a community whose service was never counted is one whose contribution could
be denied afterwards.

Some communities supported the war effort strongly, holding fundraising
drives and sending large proportions of their young men. Others objected that
treaties with the Crown had not promised military service, and that people
denied the vote could not be compelled to fight. When conscription was
applied to status Indians, that argument was made formally and largely
brushed aside. Tommy Prince, of Brokenhead Ojibway Nation, became one of the
most decorated Indigenous soldiers of the war — and came home to the same
restrictions as everyone else.

## The home front took land

The Department of National Defence expropriated reserve land for training
camps and airfields. The Stoney Point reserve in southwestern Ontario was
taken in 1942 for a military camp under the *War Measures Act*, and its
people were moved. That land was not returned for more than half a century,
and the dispute over it later led to a death and a public inquiry — which is
one reason this unit and Unit 4 are not separate stories.

> [!success]- What "loss of status" actually meant (click to expand)
> Under the *Indian Act* — the version in force today is R.S.C. 1985, c. I-5,
> but the statute dates from 1876 — a status Indian could be enfranchised,
> meaning made a full citizen and removed from the register. It sounds like a
> promotion. It meant losing the right to live on reserve, to inherit reserve
> property, and to belong formally to your own nation, and it applied to a
> man's wife and children too. Enlisted men and veterans were among those who
> lost status this way, sometimes without understanding that they had.

The pattern from the First World War then repeated with the paperwork
updated. Veterans Affairs Canada records that most First Nations veterans
could not receive the pensions, grants and benefits available to others,
because the *Indian Act* classed them as wards of the Crown; where others got
a grant to buy land, most were given a certificate to use reserve land their
community already owned. Administration ran through the Indian agent rather
than the veterans' department, and much of it simply did not arrive.

The organizing that followed became the postwar Indigenous political
movement. Follow it into [[Indigenous Policy and Indigenous Response]], and
use [[Continuity and Change]] to say what was different in 1945 from 1918.
