In 1914 Canada was at war the moment Britain was. In 1939 it was not. Canada
declared war on Germany on **10 September 1939**, a week after Britain, and
that week is the whole point — Parliament met, debated and voted. The Statute
of Westminster had made Canada's foreign policy its own, and this was the
first time the country used that independence on a question that mattered.

## Why there was a second war

The Treaty of Versailles left Germany with reparations it could not pay,
territory it resented losing, and a grievance politicians could farm. The
Depression then destroyed the German economy and with it the credibility of
the Weimar republic. Hitler came to power promising to reverse both, and
proceeded to rearm, remilitarize the Rhineland, absorb Austria, dismember
Czechoslovakia and invade Poland — testing at each step whether Britain and
France would act. Italy invaded Ethiopia; Japan invaded Manchuria and then
China. The League of Nations, built to stop exactly this, had neither an army
nor the will of its members.

Whether the appeasement of the late 1930s was cowardice or a rational attempt
to buy time for rearmament is one of the genuinely open arguments in this
course. The people deciding were the generation that had lived through
1914–18.

## What Canada did

More than one million Canadians and Newfoundlanders served. Veterans Affairs
Canada records over 45,000 deaths and another 55,000 wounded.

| Where | When | The Canadian cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hong Kong | Dec 1941 | Of almost 2,000 sent, 550+ never came home |
| Dieppe | 19 Aug 1942 | 4,963 embarked; 916 died, 1,946 taken prisoner |
| Normandy | From 6 June 1944 | 340 died on D-Day; 5,021 in the campaign |
| Netherlands | 1944–45 | More than 7,600 Canadians died |

Beyond the battles, Canada trained aircrew from across the Commonwealth in
enormous numbers, and the merchant navy and the Royal Canadian Navy fought
the long convoy war of the Atlantic that kept Britain supplied. Canadian
troops also entered the concentration camps, which is where a great deal of
Canadian awareness of the Holocaust begins.

Dieppe is the raid historians still argue about: rehearsal for Normandy, or a
badly planned disaster with a justification built afterwards? Both cases can
be made from the evidence, which is what makes it worth arguing.
