A country at war reorganizes itself. Factories retool, prices move, laws that
would have been unthinkable in peacetime pass in an afternoon, and the
government acquires powers it does not always give back. The home front is
not the quiet part of the war. It is where the war rearranged Canada.

## The powers the government took

The *War Measures Act* passed within days of the declaration. It let Cabinet
govern by order-in-council — arrest, detain, censor, deport, seize property —
without returning to Parliament each time. The same statute was used in the
Second World War and again in 1970, so learn it early: this is what Canadian
governments reach for when they decide an emergency justifies suspending the
ordinary rules. Ottawa also introduced an income tax, described at the time
as temporary. It was not.

## Who was treated as a threat

People from countries Canada was fighting were declared "enemy aliens." Tens
of thousands had to register and live under restrictions. Sir William Otter,
who ran the operation, reported that **8,579** people were interned in camps
across the country, about 3,138 of them classed as prisoners of war and the
rest civilians. The majority were of Ukrainian origin — people recruited to
Canada from territory ruled by Austria-Hungary a decade earlier, then
imprisoned for where they had been born.

> [!note] "Enemy alien" was a legal category, not a description
> Almost none of the interned had done anything. The category was defined by
> birthplace and citizenship and applied to farmers, labourers and their
> families. Notice how fast a wartime label turned neighbours into suspects —
> and that the label came from the state, not from a court.

## What changed for people who stayed

Tens of thousands of women moved into paid work in munitions plants, offices
and on farms, in jobs that had been closed to them. Much of that opening
closed again after 1918, which makes it a good test case for continuity and
change. Prices rose faster than wages, and the resentment built through the
war years fed directly into the labour unrest of 1919, in which returned
veterans were prominent on both sides.

The war reached home most literally when a collision in Halifax harbour in
December 1917 detonated a ship loaded with munitions and flattened much of
the city's north end. It killed more people than any other single event on
Canadian soil during the war. The exact toll has never been settled, because
whole households died at once and the records of who lived there burned with
the neighbourhood.
