A primary source was made by someone who was there. The curriculum's own
list is longer than most students expect — "artefacts, art works, census
data, cookbooks, diaries, letters, legislation, legislative acts, oral
histories, photographs, speeches, treaties, and some maps." A cookbook is
evidence. So is a form.

None of that makes a primary source true. Being there guarantees only that
somebody had a view of it, and views are partial by definition.

## Five questions, asked in this order

**Who made it, and what were they?** A clerk, a soldier, an agent of the
Department of Indian Affairs, a mother writing to her son. Position shapes
what a person can see and what they are permitted to write down.

**For whom?** A diary and a report to a superior are two different
performances by the same person. Audience changes everything.

**When — the date of the event, and the date of the writing.** A letter
composed the same night is not a memoir written in 1962. Both are useful,
differently.

**Why does it exist at all?** Most documents exist because a rule required
them. Knowing the rule tells you what the document had to contain and what
it was free to omit.

**What survived, and why this?** Archives keep what institutions kept.
Government paper survives at a rate that private letters never do, which
quietly tilts the record towards the people who ran things.

> [!example]- One document, worked through (click to expand)
> Library and Archives Canada has digitized the roughly 640,000 service
> files of the Canadian Expeditionary Force, free and without an account,
> reachable from
> [canada.ca/en/library-archives](https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives.html)
> (accessed August 2026). Search a surname from your own town and open the
> attestation paper — the enlistment form.
>
> It gives an age, a birthplace, a trade, a next of kin, a physical
> description, and a signature or a mark. That is a great deal. Now apply
> the five questions. The age is what the man **said**, to a recruiter under
> pressure to enlist people. The trade is a self-description. The form has
> no space at all for why he went, so the file cannot answer that, and a
> student who claims it does has stopped reading the document and started
> filling it in.

The last move is the one that separates a good answer from a fluent one:
say what your source **cannot** tell you, and go find the source that can.
The habit is built in [[Using Evidence]] and it is what
[[The Document Examination]] actually marks.

Then find more of them through [[Where the Records Live]].
