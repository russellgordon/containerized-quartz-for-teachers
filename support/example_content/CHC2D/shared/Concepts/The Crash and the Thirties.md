The New York stock market crashed in October 1929. That is the date everyone
learns, and on its own it explains very little. A stock market can fall
without wrecking a country. What turned a crash into a decade was the
structure underneath it — and Canada's structure was unusually easy to wreck.

## Why Canada fell so hard

Canada in 1929 sold a narrow range of things to the rest of the world: wheat,
timber, pulp and paper, minerals, fish. When world demand collapsed, export
income collapsed with it, and there was no large domestic market to fall back
on. Countries then raised tariff walls against each other, shrinking trade
further — a response that made every country's problem worse while each
government believed it was protecting its own.

Two habits from the 1920s made the fall steeper. Households owed money on
goods bought on instalment credit that they could no longer afford. Investors
had bought shares on margin, so a falling market forced them to sell into the
fall. Then the weather turned, and drought and wind stripped the topsoil off
large areas of the southern Prairies.

> [!tip] Be careful with the unemployment statistics
> The official figures counted men who were wage earners looking for wage
> work. They did not capture farm families whose income had gone to nothing,
> women pushed out of paid work and back into households, or First Nations,
> Métis and Inuit communities largely outside the wage economy altogether.
> Whatever rate a source gives you, ask what it was measuring — the recorded
> numbers almost certainly understate the hardship.

## Not everyone equally

Regional differences were extreme. Saskatchewan, dependent on wheat and hit
by drought at the same time, suffered worst; British Columbia and central
Canada, more diversified, suffered less. If you held a secure job you may
have done *better* through the decade, because prices fell while your wage
did not.

First Nations, Métis and Inuit communities entered the Depression already
impoverished by policy and left it that way. Relief, where it existed, was
administered separately and at lower rates. In the North the collapse of fur
prices removed the one exchange economy many Inuit families had, at the same
moment government and company posts were making them dependent on purchased
goods.

Single unemployed men were moved into federal relief camps, paid twenty cents
a day, and kept far from cities, which was the point. Applying for municipal
relief usually meant proving your destitution to an inspector who could enter
your home. The shame attached to that experience is documented in enormous
quantity in letters written to the prime minister, and those letters are
among the best primary sources this course has.
