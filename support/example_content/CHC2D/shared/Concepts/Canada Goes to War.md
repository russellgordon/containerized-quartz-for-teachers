When Britain declared war on Germany in August 1914, Canada was at war too.
Not by a vote, not after a debate — by law. Canada ran its own domestic
affairs but not its foreign policy, so a declaration made in London bound the
whole Empire. The only question Canada's Parliament could answer was how much
to send, and for how long.

## Why there was a war at all

Causes are almost never single events. Europe's great powers had tied
themselves into two alliance systems, so a local quarrel could drag in states
with no stake in it. They had spent two decades building armies and navies
against one another. Empires competed for territory and markets. And
nationalist movements inside the Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires were
pulling those empires apart from within. Sarajevo lit the fuse; it did not
build the bomb.

Canada's own reasons were different again. Many English Canadians felt a real
loyalty to Britain and thought of themselves as British subjects first. Many
French Canadians did not, and said so publicly. Both positions were sincere;
neither was the whole country.

## What it cost

Veterans Affairs Canada records that more than 650,000 Canadians and
Newfoundlanders served, that more than 66,000 died, and that more than
172,000 were wounded. Newfoundland was a separate British dominion then, not
part of Canada, which is why its losses sit alongside Canada's.

| Battle | When | The Canadian cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Second Ypres | Apr–May 1915 | 6,035 casualties in 48 hours; 2,000+ died |
| Beaumont-Hamel | 1 July 1916 | 800 Newfoundlanders in; 68 at roll call |
| Vimy Ridge | 9–12 Apr 1917 | 10,600+ casualties, nearly 3,600 fatal |
| Passchendaele | July–Nov 1917 | 4,000+ dead, almost 12,000 wounded |

Second Ypres was the first mass use of poison gas on the Western Front:
German troops released chlorine from cylinders dug into their own forward
trenches, and waited for the wind.

## The argument about what it meant

You will hear that Canada "became a nation" at Vimy Ridge. Treat that as a
claim to be tested. It is a *later* interpretation, it grew after the war,
and it grew loudest in English Canada. What is not in dispute is the
constitutional change: Canada signed the Treaty of Versailles under its own
name and took its own seat at the League of Nations. A country that was given
a war in 1914 was, by 1919, signing the peace for itself.

Separate the change from the story told about it using
[[Cause and Consequence]], and argue it in [[The War Question]].
