Veterans Affairs Canada records that more than 4,000 Indigenous people served
in uniform during the First World War. They enlisted from communities whose
members could not vote federally without giving up their status, whose
children were being taken to residential schools, and whose movement off
reserve could be controlled by an Indian agent. Understanding why they went
is the historical question this page exists for.

## The reasons were not one reason

Some enlisted out of a warrior tradition much older than Canada. Some went
because a treaty relationship was understood as an alliance with the Crown,
and an ally at war is answered. Some needed the pay. Some were pushed by
recruiters or by residential school staff. Some believed service would force
Canada to treat their nation differently afterwards. Those motives coexisted,
sometimes in one person, and none cancels the others.

> [!warning] The number is a floor, not a count
> Veterans Affairs Canada says plainly that "exact statistics are difficult
> to determine." Enlistment papers identified some men as "Indian" and not
> others, and Métis and Inuit service was rarely recorded as such at all.
> When a source gives you a precise total, ask who was counted, by whom, and
> under what definition — the undercount is itself evidence about how the
> state saw these soldiers.

Francis Pegahmagabow of what is now Wasauksing First Nation became one of the
most decorated soldiers in the Canadian Expeditionary Force and later led his
community as chief. His reputation is often retold with sniper tallies
attached; those come from accounts of uneven reliability, and the honours he
was actually awarded are firmer ground. Note too how easily one celebrated
soldier is used to imply the army was fair. It was not.

## Coming home

Veterans Affairs Canada records that most First Nations veterans could not
receive the pensions, grants and benefits other veterans received, because
the *Indian Act* treated them as wards of the Crown rather than as citizens.
Where others received a grant to buy land, most were handed a certificate to
use reserve land that already belonged to their own community.

Worse, the federal soldier settlement program took reserve land and gave it to
non-Indigenous veterans. Men returned from fighting for Canada to find their
nation's territory reduced by the same war effort they had joined. Some,
including Pegahmagabow, turned straight to political organizing: the League
of Indians of Canada, founded by the veteran F. O. Loft, is a consequence of
this war as surely as any monument is.
