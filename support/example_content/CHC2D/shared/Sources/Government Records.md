Most of what survives from the twentieth century survives because a
government kept it. Statutes, orders in council, census returns,
departmental correspondence, military service files, royal commission
reports, agents' reports, the minutes of a township council — this is the
biggest single body of evidence in Canadian history, and it is the state
talking to itself.

That last point is the one to hold on to. These documents were not written
for you, and they were almost never written by the people they are about.

## How to read one

**Find the rule that made it exist.** Almost every government document was
required. An Indian agent filed a report because he was obliged to; a
recruit signed an attestation paper because the form demanded it. The rule
tells you what the document had to contain — and therefore what its silences
mean, which is usually nothing at all.

**Read the authority line.** Who signed, under what power, and who could
have refused. A policy without a named author is a policy nobody has looked
up yet.

**Translate the vocabulary, and then keep the original.** Administrative
language exists to make decisions sound procedural. "Removal," "relocation,"
"enfranchisement," "protection," "unsuitability to the climate" — each hides
an action. Say what happened, and quote the word the file used, because the
word is evidence too.

## What it can and cannot tell you

It can tell you what was decided, when, by whom, on whose authority, and in
what words the state was willing to put in writing. That is a great deal,
and it is checkable.

It cannot tell you whether the policy worked, what it felt like, or what the
people subject to it thought. For that you need community publications, the
record of protest, and [[Oral History and Testimony]].

> [!info] Where these records live, and what they will call people
> Statutes currently in force are at the federal Justice Laws Website; the
> *Indian Act*, first enacted in 1876 and never repealed, is there today as
> R.S.C. 1985, c. I-5. Digitized government publications and archival
> microfilm are free at [Canadiana](https://www.canadiana.ca/) and
> [Héritage](https://heritage.canadiana.ca/), run by the Canadian Research
> Knowledge Network (accessed August 2026). Both carry warnings that their
> records use the racist terminology of the period, and Héritage's
> Indigenous holdings are explicitly the government's administrative file —
> not Indigenous-authored material. Read them as such, and say so in your
> work.
