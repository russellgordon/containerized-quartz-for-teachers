A census is not a photograph of a country. It is an operation: enumerators
walking streets and concessions, asking questions somebody else wrote, and
writing answers into boxes somebody else designed. Every number that comes
out the far end carries the marks of that process, and reading statistics
historically means reading the process as well as the total.

## Where the historical numbers actually are

Statistics Canada's live census portal reaches back only to 1996. For
anything earlier, the route is *Historical Statistics of Canada*, catalogue
11-516-X — about 1,088 tables running from 1867 to the mid-1970s, free, with
a CSV beside every series, at
[Section A: Population and Migration](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11-516-x/sectiona/4147436-eng.htm)
(accessed August 2026).

Two examples of what that gets you. Series A2-14 gives Canada's population
at each census: 8,787,949 in 1921, 10,376,786 in 1931, 14,009,429 in 1951.
Series A350 gives immigrant arrivals every year from 1852: 164,993 in 1929,
11,277 in 1935, 282,164 in 1957. Two columns, and the Depression and the
postwar boom are both visible without a word of commentary.

Library and Archives Canada offers something different and equally useful —
a **name search** of the census returns themselves, which finds one real
household rather than a national total. Use it for a family; do not use it
for a trend.

## Counts, rates, and the thing being counted

A count answers "how many"; a rate answers "how many out of how many," and
a hundred years of population growth makes almost every raw count rise. Any
comparison across decades needs a denominator. And check that the category
itself held still: "farm," "employed," "urban" and "immigrant" have all been
redefined inside the span of this course.

> [!failure] A count is only as honest as its categories
> The curriculum asks you directly: "From the perspective of First Nations,
> Métis, and Inuit, why is the 1921 census flawed?" and "Why did many Métis
> people choose not to publicly identify as Métis during this period?"
> Those two questions answer each other. Enumeration on reserves was
> irregular, the North was barely covered, the categories were the
> government's own, and people who had watched what identification led to
> had excellent reasons not to be identified. The undercount is not noise in
> the data. It is a consequence of policy, and it is evidence about the
> policy.

So a table can be the finding, and a table can be the thing you interrogate.
Say which you are doing. Then check the source that made it with
[[Government Records]] and put the number to work in [[Using Evidence]].
