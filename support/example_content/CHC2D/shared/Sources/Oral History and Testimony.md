The curriculum lists oral histories and oral traditional knowledge among the
primary sources of this course, alongside legislation and census data. That
placement is deliberate. A spoken account is evidence, held to the same
standards as any other document — and to one more, because a document has no
family and a testimony does.

## What a testimony is evidence of

It is evidence of an experience, and it is evidence of how that experience
is remembered and told now. Those are two different things and a good answer
separates them. A veteran describing an assault in 1944 is a witness to that
morning; the shape of the telling, sixty years later, also records what the
country has since made it possible to say out loud.

So date a testimony twice — when the events happened, and when the account
was recorded. Then ask who was in the room, who asked the questions, and
whether the speaker chose the setting.

## Provenance and custodianship

The people who hold these recordings hold obligations, not only files.

**The Memory Project**, run by Historica Canada, has gathered more than
3,000 testimonies from veterans and their families. The archive is moving
onto The Canadian Encyclopedia and is not complete there yet, so a name you
cannot find may simply not have been migrated. Every article carries
ready-made citations, and the speakers bureau will send a veteran to a class
free of charge — Remembrance-season requests close on 3 October.

**The National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation** at the University of
Manitoba holds the records of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission under
a statute, governed by a Governing Circle and advised by a Survivors Circle.
Its Grade 9–10 teaching set is public at
[archives.nctr.ca](https://archives.nctr.ca/index.php/G9-G10)
(accessed August 2026).

> [!quote] What the NCTR asks of anyone who comes to its archive
> "NCTR's spirit name – *bezhig miigwan*, meaning 'one feather'. Bezhig
> miigwan calls upon us to see each Survivor coming to the NCTR as a single
> eagle feather and to show those Survivors the same respect and attention
> an eagle feather deserves."

Survivor statements are **not** an open collection. Access is tiered and
consent-based, and the Centre prioritises Survivors, their families and
their communities. You are a reader and a witness of this archive, never an
extractor: do not hunt for an individual's records and do not write to the
NCTR for records as a class exercise, because that competes with the people
it exists to serve. The National Residential School Crisis Line runs 24
hours at 1-866-925-4419, and it is for anyone in this class.

Take that care into [[Indigenous Peoples and the Second World War]] and
[[What Did the War Change at Home?]].
