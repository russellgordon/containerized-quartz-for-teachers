Rights in Canada did not arrive. They were extended, piece by piece, to
different people at different times, usually after somebody had spent years
demanding them and often after somebody had gone to court. The sequence is
the evidence, and this inquiry is about reading it.

## The question

**Who got which rights, when, and what did they have to do to get them?**

Two traps sit in that question. The first is treating a statute's date as
the date something changed for real people. The second is treating
extension as inevitable — a story of steady progress in which nobody had to
fight, which is not what the record shows.

## The evidence set

| The measure | The date that matters | Whom it reached |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canadian Bill of Rights | Assented 10 August 1960, S.C. 1960, c. 44 | Federal law only; an ordinary statute, and still in force |
| The federal franchise for status First Nations people | Early 1960s — the NCTR's timeline gives 1961 | Voting at last without surrendering Indian status |
| Royal Commission on the Status of Women | Reported at the end of the 1960s | Recommendations, not law; the follow-through is a separate question |
| *Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms* | Proclaimed 17 April 1982 | Most of it — but not all |
| Charter section 15, equality rights | In force 17 April 1985 | Deliberately delayed three years so governments could amend their laws |
| Bill C-31 | 1985 | Amended the *Indian Act* on status, after litigation and campaigning |

Alongside the table, the class works with the cases and campaigns that
produced these dates: the Murdoch case on the value of a wife's unpaid work,
Jeannette Corbiere Lavell's challenge to the loss of status on marriage,
Mary Two-Axe Earley's decades of organizing, and the expropriation of
Africville read from the perspectives of residents, city officials and the
press.

## The reasoning the class does

For each row, separate **the right on paper** from **the right you can
use**. Ask who had to litigate, who had to organize, and who simply had the
right already. Then look at the three-year gap between 1982 and 1985 and say
plainly what it was for and whom it disadvantaged.

Finish with the "and when" half of the title. Put the dates on one timeline
and describe the pattern in a sentence someone could disagree with.

Read the movements behind the dates in
[[Rights, Movements, and Resistance]], the constitutional frame in
[[Patriation, the Charter, and After]], and take it into
[[The Rights Inquiry]].
