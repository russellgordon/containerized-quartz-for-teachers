In 1917 the Borden government made military service compulsory. Nobody
disputes that the decision split the country. The investigation is whether
it had to be made — and "necessary" is a word that falls apart the moment
you press on it, which is exactly why this is the first inquiry of the
course.

## The question, taken apart

Necessary **for what**? Three different claims hide inside the one word, and
they need separate evidence.

*Militarily* — were there not enough reinforcements to keep the Canadian
Corps in the field, and would nothing else have supplied them?

*Politically* — had the government committed itself so publicly that retreat
was impossible? A promise is a fact about politics, not about manpower.

*Morally* — if the war was worth fighting, was compulsion the fair way to
share it? People argued this at the time, on both sides, and their reasoning
is on the record.

## The evidence set

Your class works with six kinds of material, none of it reproduced here
because finding it is part of the task.

**Enlistment and casualty returns for 1915 to 1917**, which is where the
military claim stands or falls. **Statements by ministers** before and after
the decision, including what the government had already pledged. **The
Military Voters Act and the Wartime Elections Act**, which changed who could
vote in the election that followed — read them for who gained the franchise
and who lost it. **The 1917 election results by region**, mapped. **Editorial
and cartoon coverage** from at least one English-language and one
French-language paper. And **the returns showing how many conscripted men
actually reached the front before the Armistice**, which is the single
number most arguments about 1917 never mention.

> [!success]- The turn most answers get wrong (click to expand)
> Writing "conscription was necessary because Canada needed soldiers" and
> stopping. That is a restatement, not a finding. The claim only becomes an
> argument when you show the shortfall in numbers, name an alternative that
> was actually available in 1917, and explain why it was rejected. If your
> evidence cannot do all three, say so — a limited conclusion, honestly
> bounded, marks better than a confident one built on nothing.

## What the class produces

A written judgement with its criteria stated first, using
[[Cause and Consequence]] to separate what was intended from what followed.
Expect the room to divide. Read the aftermath in
[[Conscription and the Country It Split]], then carry the argument into
[[The War Question]].
