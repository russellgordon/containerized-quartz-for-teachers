Every community has already answered this question, whether or not anybody
noticed it being asked. There is a cenotaph or there isn't. A street carries
a name. A school was renamed, or a proposal to rename it failed. A day is
marked with an assembly, or it passes. This inquiry treats those as
decisions with authors and dates, and goes looking for them.

## The question

**What does this place commemorate, who decided, and what is missing?**

The last clause is the hard one, because an absence leaves no plaque. You
find it by working out who lived here — from census returns, church and
community records, and local newspapers — and then noticing which of those
communities the landscape does not mention.

> [!todo] The search, in the order that works
> Walk or map the commemorations you can see: monuments, plaques, street and
> park and school names, murals, memorial trees, halls.
>
> Photograph the small print. Plaques name a sponsoring organization and a
> year, and that is your lead.
>
> Find the unveiling in the local paper. Who spoke, who paid, who was not
> invited.
>
> Look for the council or board minutes. A commemoration is usually a motion
> before it is an object.
>
> Then ask the community itself. Historical societies, cultural
> associations, and the nations whose territory this is have kept records
> the municipality never did.

## The reasoning the class does

Apply the criteria from [[Historical Significance]] honestly: impact on a
group, durability, significance for whom, relevance now. Then apply them a
second way, to the commemoration rather than the event — because a monument
raised in 1922 is evidence about 1922, and a renaming argued about in 2019
is evidence about 2019.

Culture counts here too. A song, a mural, a film or a museum exhibition can
carry a community's memory further than any granite, and it is often what a
community made when the official record would not hold it.

Finish with a recommendation you could actually put to somebody — a council,
a board, a museum, a community organization — and say what it would cost and
whom it would answer. That is the standard [[The Commemoration Inquiry]]
marks against.

Set your local finding beside the national record in
[[Commemoration and Public Memory]], and argue the ethics of it in
[[Who Gets to Tell the Story?]].
