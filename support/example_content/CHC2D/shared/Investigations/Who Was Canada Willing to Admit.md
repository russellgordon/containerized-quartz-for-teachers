Between 1914 and 1929 Canada advertised abroad for settlers and legislated
at home to keep particular people out. Both things are true at once, and the
investigation is to work out the rule that reconciles them — because there
was one, and officials of the day could have told you what it was.

## The question

**Whom did Canada want, on what terms, and how did the state say so when it
preferred not to say so out loud?**

That last clause is the historian's part. Some exclusions were written into
a statute with a group named in the title. Others were built out of
neutral-sounding requirements that only certain applicants could ever meet.
Both are policy. Only one leaves an obvious paper trail.

## The evidence set

**The *Immigration Act* of 1919**, and specifically the powers it gave
Cabinet to bar entry by nationality, occupation, or unsuitability to the
climate — language broad enough that officials never had to state a reason
in public.

**The *Chinese Immigration Act* of 1923**, which replaced the head tax with
near-total exclusion and required Chinese Canadians already here to
register. Set it beside the community's response: Humiliation Day, and the
refusal to mark Dominion Day for decades afterwards.

**Continuous-journey regulations**, which excluded South Asian migrants by
demanding a voyage that could not be bought.

**Recruitment advertising** aimed at Britain and northern Europe, which
tells you the preference in the government's own promotional voice.

**Annual arrival figures** from *Historical Statistics of Canada*, to test
whether the policy produced the population it promised.

**Local newspapers** on the arrival of a specific group in a specific town —
where the national policy meets an actual street.

> [!abstract] The distinction the whole inquiry rests on
> A **named exclusion** tells you what a government was willing to defend in
> public. An **engineered exclusion** — a rule written so that only some
> people can satisfy it — tells you what it wanted without wanting to be
> quoted. Sort your evidence into those two piles before you write anything.
> The relative size of the piles is itself a finding about the period.

## What the class produces

A short evidence-based account of the rule Canada was actually operating,
with at least one named exclusion and one engineered one. Then follow the
same distinction forward through [[Immigration Policy Over a Century]], and
set it against the discrimination it licensed in [[Who Counted as Canadian]].
