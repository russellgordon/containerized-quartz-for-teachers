An archive is not a library. A library holds published things, arranged
so you can find a subject. An archive holds what an organization or a
family accumulated while doing something else, kept in the order it was
made in — so you find material by tracing who created it, not by topic.

## The ladder

Archival material is described from the top down: **fonds** — everything
from one creator — then **series**, then **file**, then **item**. A
description of a whole series may be all that exists, because most
archives have never described their holdings item by item.

A **finding aid** is the map of a fonds: who made the records, when, how
much there is, what the series are. A **catalogue record** is the entry
for one described unit. Read both — the record says what the material
is, the finding aid says what sits beside it, which is usually where the
good evidence turns out to be.

> [!example]- What a catalogue record is really telling you
> Take the Archives of Ontario's First World War poster collection,
> **C 233**. `C` marks a private collection; `233` is that collection.
> The record gives a creator, a date range, an extent, and access
> conditions — but not whether the material is digitised, which for
> these posters it is. Copy the reference number exactly: it is how
> anyone, including you next month, finds the same item again.

Most archival description is online; most archival material is not. A
database search ends in a reference number and a reading-room visit, so
prefer a digitised collection when the deadline is a week away.

## Where we will actually work

The **Archives of Ontario** has online exhibits and free lesson plans
built on real records — wartime posters, the 1918 influenza, letters
from the Depression, women's wartime work. **The Memory Project** holds
over 3,000 testimonies from veterans and family members, each with a
ready-made citation — good practice for [[Citing Historical Sources]].

> [!warning] The NCTR is not a collection to be mined
> Survivor statements and individual residential school records are
> access-controlled by statute, and the National Centre for Truth and
> Reconciliation prioritises Survivors and families searching for their
> own people. Use its public exhibits, its published reports, and the
> material it prepared for Grade 9 and 10; do not submit records requests
> as a class exercise. The National Residential School Crisis Line is
> 1-866-925-4419.

Copy the repository, reference number, title, and date the moment you
open an item — [[Where the Records Live]] has the full list, and
[[Reading a Primary Source]] is what to do with it.
