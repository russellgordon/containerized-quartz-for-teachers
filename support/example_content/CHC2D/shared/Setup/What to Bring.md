Short list. The last item on it is the one that decides how the period
goes.

| Bring | Why |
| --- | --- |
| A notebook you keep all term | This is [[Your Inquiry Notebook]] — questions, sources, and how your thinking changed. Paper is fine; a single digital file is fine; five apps is not |
| Something to write with | You will annotate printed documents in most weeks, and marking up a source is how you read one |
| A device you can research and write on | The school has loaners. Everything we use runs in a browser |
| The reading, already done | Periods start from a source you were meant to have met |
| The current task page, read | [[The Source Study]] and every task after it state what earns the marks before you begin |

## About the reading

It is rarely long, and it is never optional. A period built on a document
does not work if half the room is meeting it for the first time — the
discussion turns into a summary of what it says instead of an argument
about what it shows.

If you could not do the reading, come anyway and tell me at the start.
That is a smaller problem than it feels like, and much smaller than
pretending.

## Handling documents in class

Some of what we work with is a photocopy of something fragile, and a few
things are borrowed. Do not fold, eat over, or take them home unless the
page says you may. Annotating your own printed copy is encouraged —
underline the claim, circle the date, and put a question mark where the
source is telling you what it wants you to think.

## Software

Nothing to install. A browser, a word processor, and access to this site
covers the course. Citations are written by hand rather than generated,
because the point of [[Citing Historical Sources]] is knowing which
details a reader needs to find your source again.

## One thing not to bring

Certainty. The seminars are built on questions where the evidence
genuinely divides — [[Was It Worth It?]] is the first — and arriving with
a position you are unwilling to test wastes the period, yours and
everyone else's.
