**Due: the third week of Unit 1.** Individual. About 600 words.

One primary source from the period 1914–1929, read properly. This is the
task that teaches the habit everything else in the course depends on.

## Choosing a source

A recruiting poster, a letter home, a newspaper front page, a government
order, a photograph, a page of census returns, a diary entry, a union
notice. It must be a **primary** source — made at the time, by someone
who was there — and you must be able to say where it is held.

## What you produce

1. **The source, described.** What it physically is, who made it, when,
   for whom, and why. If you cannot establish one of those, say so.
2. **What it says**, including what it takes for granted.
3. **What it does not say**, and who is missing from it.
4. **What it is evidence of.** This is the heart of the task: a
   recruiting poster is weak evidence about how many men enlisted and
   excellent evidence about how the state wanted enlistment understood.
5. **How you would check it** — what second source would confirm or
   complicate it.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Provenance | Maker, date, audience, and purpose established or honestly flagged |
| Reading | You notice what the source assumes as well as what it states |
| Evidence | You distinguish what it proves from what it merely suggests |
| Silence | You identify who is absent and why that matters |
| Citation | Properly referenced, per [[Citing Historical Sources]] |

> [!warning] A source is not a witness you cross-examine for the truth
> It is a trace left by someone with reasons. "Is this reliable?" is a
> weaker question than "reliable for what?" — see
> [[Reading a Primary Source]].

Where such sources live, and which archives you can actually reach, is
on [[Where the Records Live]].
