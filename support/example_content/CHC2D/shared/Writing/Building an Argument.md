A history essay is not a report on a topic. It is a claim that somebody
could reasonably deny, defended with evidence, in an order the reader can
follow. The curriculum asks you to "evaluate and synthesize" your findings
into "conclusions and/or informed judgements" — evaluate, then synthesize.
Those are two jobs and most weak work has skipped the first.

## A thesis is a claim, not a subject

Test yours by writing its opposite. If the opposite is absurd, you have a
topic sentence pretending to be a thesis.

> Conscription in 1917 was a controversial policy that divided Canadians.
>
> Conscription in 1917 delivered fewer reinforcements than its supporters
> promised while costing the government its authority in Quebec, so it
> should be judged a political decision that failed on its own military
> terms.

The first cannot be denied by anyone. The second can — and a reader who
disagrees now knows exactly where to attack, which is the courtesy an
argument owes.

## Say your criteria before you judge

Almost every question in this course is a judgement: most significant,
justified, a turning point, could more have been done. A judgement without
stated criteria is a preference. So put the criteria in the introduction:
*I am treating significance as depth of impact, durability, and reach beyond
the group directly involved.* Now the reader can dispute your standard
rather than your taste.

## Structure that survives contact with evidence

Open with the claim and the criteria. Give each body paragraph one reason,
not one source — sources are what you spend inside a paragraph, not what
organizes the essay. Deal with the strongest evidence against you in the
body, where it can be answered, never in a final apologetic sentence. Close
by saying what your conclusion is bounded by; a limit stated is a strength.

## Then choose the form deliberately

The curriculum expects you to "communicate ideas, arguments, and
conclusions using various formats and styles, as appropriate for the
audience and purpose" — a seminar, a debate, a presentation, a
recommendation to a council. The argument does not change; the ordering, the
tone and the amount of context do. Ask who is receiving this and what they
already know before you decide how to open.

Get the evidence right in [[Using Evidence]], the vocabulary right in
[[The Words Historians Use]], and the acknowledgements right in
[[Citing Historical Sources]]. Then write one for real in
[[The Source Study]].
