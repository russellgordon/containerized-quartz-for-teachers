Evidence is not decoration and it is not proof. It is the material you
reason with, and the reasoning has to be visible on the page. The curriculum
asks you to "interpret and analyse evidence," which means the sentence after
the quotation matters more than the quotation.

## Three ways in, chosen on purpose

**Quote** when the exact words are the evidence — a phrase in a statute, a
euphemism in a departmental memo, a line a witness chose. **Paraphrase**
when the content matters and the wording does not. **Summarize** when you
need the shape of a long source in one sentence. Quoting everything is not
rigour; it is a refusal to decide what matters.

## The three moves, every time

Introduce the source so the reader knows who is speaking and when. Present
the evidence. Then interpret it — say what it shows, and say what it cannot
show. That third move is the one that gets skipped, and it is the one being
assessed.

> [!example] What the three moves look like together
> Reporting to the Department of Indian Affairs in 1921, the agent recorded
> the year's attendance without noting a single absence by name. The silence
> is not evidence that no child was missing; the form had no column for it.
> What the report does establish is the shape of the record itself — a
> system that counted children as attendance figures and preserved no
> instrument for asking where a particular child had gone. To find that
> out, the inquiry has to leave the departmental file.

Notice what the paragraph does not do. It does not claim the document proves
more than it can. It states the limit, then names the next source. That is
an argument in good faith, and it reads as confidence rather than hedging.

## Evidence that disagrees with you

Find it deliberately. Two sources that conflict is not a problem in your
research; it is usually the finding. Ask which source was better placed to
know, who each was written for, and whether both could be true of different
people. Then say which you weigh more, and why.

## The habits that keep it honest

One claim, one piece of evidence, one interpretation — then the next claim.
Never let a quotation open or close a paragraph on its own. Attribute inside
the sentence, so a reader can weigh the source without leaving the line. And
record where every item came from at the moment you find it, because
reconstructing a reference the night before is how good work loses marks in
[[Citing Historical Sources]].

Test what you have found with [[Reading a Primary Source]], then build it
into a case in [[Building an Argument]].
