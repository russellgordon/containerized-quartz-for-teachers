Precise vocabulary is not decoration on a finished argument. It is how the
argument gets made, because most mistakes in student history writing are a
word doing work its author never authorized. The curriculum asks for
"appropriate terminology," including the vocabulary of the four concepts of
historical thinking. Here is what the load-bearing words actually mean.

## Words about sources

A **primary source** was made by someone present at the time. A **secondary
source** interprets primary sources afterwards. A **tertiary source** — an
encyclopedia, a textbook — summarizes the secondary literature. Calling one
by another's name misstates how close your evidence sits to the event.

## Words about government

A **statute** is a law passed by a legislature. A **regulation** is made
under the authority of a statute. An **order in council** is a decision of
Cabinet given legal force without debate. A **policy** may be none of these
— an internal practice with no statutory basis, which is why some of the
heaviest decisions in this course left so little law behind them.

## Words about people

Say **Indigenous peoples** for the collective, and name **First Nations**,
**Métis** or **Inuit** whenever your evidence is specific — and name the
nation itself when you know it. **Aboriginal** is section 35's term in the
*Constitution Act, 1982*, so it belongs where you discuss that section.
**Indian**, **Eskimo** and **enemy alien** run through the record because
governments used them as legal categories: quote them, as evidence of the
system that produced them, and never adopt them as your own descriptions.

Be equally careful with **residential school**, **day school** and
**industrial school** — different institutions, differently recognized in law.

## Words that carry a verdict

Some words decide the argument before you make it. **Genocide** is one — a
term with legal and historical content, applied in this course to the
Holodomor, which Canada recognized as a genocide in 2008. Use it where your
sources support it, attribute the recognition, never as emphasis.

> [!warning]- Five words to strike from a draft (click to expand)
> **Inevitable** — nothing was; you are hiding a decision.
> **Obviously** and **of course** — they signal a step you did not take.
> **Everyone** — see [[Historical Perspective]]; somebody objected.
> **Modern** and **backward** — these rank periods rather than describe them.
>
> Delete each and read the sentence again. If it now says less, the word was
> carrying an argument you had not proved.

Take the vocabulary into [[Building an Argument]] and match it to your
evidence in [[Using Evidence]].
