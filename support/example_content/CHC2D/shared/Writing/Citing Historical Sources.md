A citation is not a penalty for using someone else's work. It is the
instruction that lets a reader go and check you — and in history, where two
scholars can read one file and disagree, it is the whole basis on which
anyone believes you.

The curriculum asks for "accepted forms of documentation" — footnotes,
endnotes, author-date citations, reference lists — acknowledging "archival
sources, articles, art works, blogs, books, films or videos, oral evidence,
websites." Your teacher names the style; below is what every style needs.

## What a historical citation has to carry

Published work needs author, title, publisher and year. Archival material
needs more, because the point is to send someone to the same piece of paper:
the **repository**, the **collection or fonds**, the **reference number**,
the **item and its date**, and the date you looked at it. Copy the reference
number the archive prints — a search cannot reconstruct it. Testimony needs
the speaker, the project that recorded it, the custodian holding it now, the
date recorded and the date accessed — two dates, for the reasons set out in
[[Oral History and Testimony]].

```
Archival document, note form:
  1. Attestation paper, [name], Canadian Expeditionary Force
     personnel files, [reference number as printed by the archive],
     Library and Archives Canada, accessed [date you looked].

Oral testimony:
  2. [Speaker], testimony, The Memory Project Archive, Historica
     Canada, recorded [date], accessed [date you looked].

Statistical series, reference list:
  Statistics Canada. "Series A350, immigrant arrivals, 1852 to
     1977." Historical Statistics of Canada, 11-516-X. Accessed
     [date you looked].
```

Two conveniences. The Memory Project's testimonies on The Canadian
Encyclopedia print a ready-made citation in four styles; and Statistics
Canada's open licence **requires** attribution, so citing it is a condition.

## Two things that are not sources

An encyclopedia entry is a fine place to check a date and the wrong thing to
call a primary source. Cite it as what it is, then go to the archive.

Text produced by generative artificial intelligence has no author, no date
of observation, and no obligation to have checked anything. It can point you
towards a source; it can never be one. If you cannot trace a claim to
something published with a name and a year on it, you do not have it yet.
Record every reference as you collect it, and use it in
[[Building an Argument]].
