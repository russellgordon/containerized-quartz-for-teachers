Who tells a story determines what can be known, what can be hidden, and
how much a reader is asked to do.

## The decisions a writer makes

| Decision | Options | What it costs and buys |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Person | First, second, third | First buys intimacy and pays in reliability |
| Distance | Inside the head, at the shoulder, far above | Close narration makes judgement harder — deliberately |
| Time | Told as it happens, or recalled | Retrospect adds a second, older voice |
| Knowledge | What the narrator knows and when they learned it | Withholding is a structure, not a trick |

## Retrospective narration

[[Monkey Beach]] is told by Lisamarie, looking back. That means two
voices are present at once: the girl living it and the woman
reconstructing it. When the narration seems to know too much, ask which
of the two is speaking — the doubling is the novel's method, not a slip.

## Free indirect style

Third person that slides into a character's own words without quotation
marks: *He would show them. He would show all of them.* The judgement in
that sentence belongs to the character, and the reader is left to decide
what to do with it. Watch for it in the short fiction unit; it is
everywhere in Munro.

## Unreliability, at this level

Not just liars. A narrator can be unreliable through youth, grief,
loyalty, or a worldview the text is quietly examining. Ask what the text
knows that its narrator does not, and how you can tell.
