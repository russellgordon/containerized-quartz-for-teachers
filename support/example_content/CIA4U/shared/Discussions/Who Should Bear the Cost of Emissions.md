**The question:** greenhouse gas emissions impose costs on people who did
not choose them. Who should pay, and how much?

[[Externalities]] gives you the economics: an unpriced cost is
over-produced, and pricing it corrects the quantity. It does not tell you
whose bill it is, and that is the entire argument.

## Four answers people actually give

**The emitter.** Whoever burns the fuel pays, because they made the
choice. Simple, and it lands hardest on households and industries with no
practical alternative — the person heating a house in Timmins has fewer
options than the person heating one in Vancouver.

**The consumer.** The cost belongs in the price of the goods, so that
demand adjusts. This is what a carbon price does. It is also regressive
before rebates, because energy is a bigger share of a small budget.

**The shareholder.** Firms absorb it from profit rather than passing it
on. Whether they can depends entirely on elasticity, which is why
[[Elasticity]] is not an optional prerequisite for this seminar.

**Everybody, through general revenue.** Fund the transition from taxes and
leave prices alone. Removes the incentive effect, which is the point of
pricing in the first place.

## What Canada actually chose, twice

The consumer fuel charge was set to zero on 1 April 2025 and repealed in
March 2026; the industrial Output-Based Pricing System continues, at \$95 a
tonne in 2026 under the benchmark effective 15 May 2026. So Canada shifted
the answer from "consumers and emitters" to "large emitters only", and
Alberta has frozen its own industrial price for 2026. The full sequence is
in [[The Price on Carbon That Was Removed]].

> [!note] Carbon leakage is the honest objection, not a talking point
> If a price applies here and not elsewhere, emissions-intensive
> trade-exposed production can move rather than clean up, and global
> emissions do not fall. That is why the federal system prices only
> emissions above a benchmark rather than every tonne. Whether that
> protection is calibrated correctly is a genuine empirical question, and
> the answer is different for cement than for oil.

## The discussion

1. Argue the position you least agree with, for four minutes, using
   evidence. This is not a debating trick — it is the fastest way to find
   the strongest version of what you are up against.
2. Distinguish the positive question (does pricing reduce emissions, and by
   how much) from the normative one (is that reduction worth this cost to
   this household). Say which you are arguing.
3. D2.2's question: which forms of influence actually moved this policy —
   voting, lobbying, protest, litigation, or an election?
4. Land it: design a price you would defend to a household in a small
   northern community and to a steel plant competing with an unpriced
   importer. Name what you gave up.
