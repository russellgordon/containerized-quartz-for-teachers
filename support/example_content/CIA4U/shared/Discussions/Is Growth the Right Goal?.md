**The question:** Canadian economic policy is organised around raising
gross domestic product. Should it be?

Notice that this is two questions wearing one coat. Is growth good? And is
GDP a good measure of it? You can answer yes to the first and no to the
second, and most economists do.

## The case for growth

Growth is what has historically financed everything else — pensions, health
care, cleaner technology, shorter working lives. Productivity growth is the
only way real wages rise without inflation, which is
[[Growth and Productivity]]'s argument. And a stagnant economy makes
distribution harder, not easier: every gain has to be taken from somebody.

## The case against, in its serious form

Not "growth is bad" but three specific objections. GDP counts production
and not depletion — a forest cut down adds to it and the forest's absence
does not subtract. It counts paid work and not unpaid: childcare bought is
in, childcare done at home is out, which changes the number without
changing the childcare. And it says nothing about distribution, so an
economy can grow while the median household does not.

The alternatives the curriculum names — the Human Development Index, the
Genuine Progress Indicator, happiness measures, low-growth models — each
fix one of those and give something up. That trade is the discussion.

> [!success]- What GDP is actually for
> It was built during the Second World War to measure productive capacity,
> and it does that well. The complaint is not that GDP is badly
> constructed; it is that a good measure of one thing was promoted to a
> measure of everything. That distinction is worth holding on to, because
> "GDP is a bad measure" is easy to say and usually means "GDP is a measure
> of something other than what I care about".

## The discussion

1. Canada's real GDP was flat in the first quarter of 2026 and fell 0.2%
   in the last quarter of 2025 (table 36-10-0104-01, released 29 May 2026).
   Name three things you would want to know before calling that bad.
2. Pick one alternative measure and defend it against the objection that
   it embeds somebody's values. Then admit that GDP does too, and say why
   that is or is not different.
3. If growth continues and the poverty rate does not move — it was 11.0%
   in 2024, table 11-10-0135-01, released 29 April 2026 — has policy
   succeeded?
4. Land it: what would you measure, if you were setting the target?
