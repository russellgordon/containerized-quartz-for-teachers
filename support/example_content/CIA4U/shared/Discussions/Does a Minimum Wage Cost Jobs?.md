**The question:** when the legal wage floor rises, does employment fall?

This is the best seminar in the course, because the disagreement is real,
it is about evidence rather than values, and the people on both sides are
serious economists who have read each other.

## What the simple model says, and why that is not the end

A price floor above the market-clearing wage produces surplus labour —
more people willing to work at that wage than employers willing to hire.
[[Supply and Demand]] gives you that in one diagram. But the diagram
assumes a competitive labour market with many small employers. Where a few
employers dominate hiring in a town or an occupation, the same theory
predicts that a higher floor can raise employment. So the model does not
settle it; the data has to.

## Four studies, and what each takes as evidence

| Study | Method | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Card and Krueger, 1994 | Fast food on both sides of a state border, before and after | No employment loss |
| Neumark and Wascher | Same episode, payroll records rather than survey | Employment loss |
| Brochu and Green, 2013 | Canadian data, worker flows rather than stocks | Hiring rates fell; layoff risk for existing workers fell |
| Later bunching studies | Count jobs at each wage level across many increases | Jobs move up the wage distribution more than they disappear |

Notice what is being argued about. Not whether the wage rose, and not
whether employment changed — but which comparison is credible, and what
counts as the effect. Brochu and Green's result is the most interesting for
this seminar because it says the burden falls on people trying to get hired
rather than on people already employed, which no single employment number
would show.

## The discussion

1. Ontario's general minimum wage is \$17.60 an hour and rises to \$17.95
   on 1 October 2026, announced on 1 April 2026 as a 1.9% adjustment
   tracking the Ontario CPI. Is indexing to prices the same policy as
   raising the floor? Argue it.
2. Take one study from the table and attack its comparison group. Then
   defend it.
3. Who bears the cost if employment does fall — and who bears the cost if
   the floor stays where it is? Both answers are distributional.
4. Land it: what evidence would change your mind? If none would, you are
   having a normative argument, and [[Judging an Economic Claim]] says to
   name it as one.
