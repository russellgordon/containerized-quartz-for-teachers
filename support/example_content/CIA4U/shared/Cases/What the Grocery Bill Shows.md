The grocery bill is where macroeconomics stops being abstract. It is also
a case in which two separate arguments — about inflation and about market
structure — are constantly mistaken for each other, and separating them is
most of the work.

## Two facts, from two different places

The first is a price fact. In June 2026 the Consumer Price Index rose 2.8%
year over year, but food purchased from stores rose 3.9%, decelerating
from 4.3% in May, while shelter rose 1.5% (Statistics Canada, table
18-10-0004-01, released 20 July 2026). Groceries and rent moved at
different speeds, so the inflation a household experiences depends on what
it buys — which is why the headline rate can feel wrong to someone whose
budget is mostly food.

The second is a structure fact. The Competition Bureau's *Canada Needs More
Grocery Competition*, published 27 June 2023, reported that Loblaw, Sobeys
and Metro together recorded more than \$100 billion in sales and more than
\$3.6 billion in profit in 2022, and concluded that Canada needs more
competition in grocery retail.

## The two arguments people run together

| Claim | What would count as evidence | What would not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food prices rose because of general inflation | The CPI, energy and input costs, wage data | Grocer profits alone |
| Food prices rose because three firms set them | Margins over time, entry and exit, regional price gaps | The size of total profit |

Total profit is the number quoted most often and the weakest of the set:
a very large firm with a thin margin can report a very large profit. The
argument turns on margin and on whether prices are higher where the choice
is narrower — and the Bureau's own study is a better source for that than
any headline about billions.

## Who feels it, and by how much

Groceries are a much larger share of a low-income household's budget, so a
given food-price increase is a larger real income shock for them. The
Market Basket Measure put 11.0% of Canadians below the poverty line in
2024, and 11.5% of children — 29.7% of children in one-parent families
headed by women, against 8.4% in two-parent families (table 11-10-0135-01,
released 29 April 2026).[^mbm]

That distribution is not a footnote to the inflation story. It is why
[[Inequality]] and [[Inflation]] belong on the same page, and why
[[Market Structures]] is the tool for the second half of this case.

[^mbm]: The Market Basket Measure prices a specific basket for a
reference family and compares it to disposable income. The figures above
use the 2023 base; see [[Index Numbers and Real Versus Nominal]] for why
the base matters.
