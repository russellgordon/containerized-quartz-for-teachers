This is the case that will have changed by the time you read it, and that
is the point. Canada's trading relationship with the United States is
being renegotiated in public, in stages, and your job is to work with a
moving file rather than wait for it to settle.

## Where it stood in the summer of 2026

The Canada–United States–Mexico Agreement built in a joint review of its
own terms. That review reached its 1 July 2026 deadline without the three
parties agreeing to extend the agreement's term. Goods that comply with
CUSMA's rules of origin remain exempt from the general tariff; goods that
do not have faced 10%. Separately, and under different American law,
tariffs imposed under Section 232 remain on steel, aluminum, copper, some
automotive parts, and lumber. Further American measures were reported as
taking effect in August 2026, and by the time you argue this case the list
will have moved again.

## What the data said while that was happening

| Measure | June 2026 | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goods exports | \$77.5 billion, up 0.4% from May | Statistics Canada, released 4 August 2026 |
| Exports to the United States | Up 0.3%, a fifth consecutive rise | Same release |
| Surplus with the United States | \$10.0 billion, down from \$11.1 billion in May | Same release |
| Overall merchandise trade surplus | \$3.9 billion | Same release |

That table is the interesting part. Exports rose to a new high during a
tariff dispute, led by metal and mineral products including unwrought
gold. A student who expected exports to collapse has to explain this, and
the explanation is where the economics lives: composition matters, some
goods are exempt, some are priced in a global market, and an aggregate
hides the industries that were hit hardest.

## The reasoning to do

Use [[Comparative Advantage]] to say what the model predicts a tariff
does — and then say what it leaves out, because the model has no
geopolitics in it and no adjustment costs for the worker whose plant
closed. Use [[Elasticity]] to work out who actually pays a tariff: the
answer depends on how easily buyers can switch, not on who writes the
cheque at the border.

Then the harder question, which is E2.3's: what should Canada do? Retaliate
in kind, diversify markets, subsidise affected industries, or absorb it and
keep negotiating — each has a constituency, a cost, and a record. Judge
them by criteria you state in advance, as [[The Trade Question]] requires.

**Retrieve before you write.** Statistics Canada's merchandise trade
release and the federal government's own tariff notices are the two
primary sources; anything second-hand about this file is likely out of
date within weeks.
