Productivity is output per hour worked. It is not effort, and it is not
how hard anybody is trying. Almost every economic argument about Canada's
long-run living standards eventually becomes an argument about this one
measure, so it is worth understanding what it does and does not say.

## The facts nobody disputes

Business sector labour productivity fell 0.5% in the first quarter of 2026,
after edging down 0.3% in the previous quarter — goods-producing
businesses down 1.7%, services up 0.3%. Hourly compensation rose 0.9%, so
unit labour costs rose 1.4%, a fourth consecutive quarterly increase.
Statistics Canada released those figures on 3 June 2026; the series is
table 36-10-0206-01.

The longer view is starker. Statistics Canada's *Economic and Social
Reports* article "Measuring Canada's economic performance relative to the
United States", published in March 2026, found the Canada–United States
labour productivity gap has widened by about 26% since 2000.

## The facts that are argued about

The same Statistics Canada work points at firm size: in 2019 small firms
produced 56% of Canada's nominal GDP against 45% in the United States, and
Canadian small firms operated at 70% of their US counterparts' labour
productivity while Canadian large firms reached 87%. That is a
composition explanation — Canada has more of its economy in the less
productive kind of firm.

Other economists put the weight elsewhere: on business investment in
machinery and intellectual property per worker, on competition policy and
protected sectors, on interprovincial trade barriers, or on measurement
itself, since output in services is genuinely hard to count. These are not
mutually exclusive, and the policy each one implies is different — which
is exactly why the diagnosis matters more than the diagnosis-sounding
phrase.

> [!success]- Why "work harder" is not an answer
> Because productivity is a ratio with hours in the denominator. Adding
> hours raises output and hours together and moves the ratio very little.
> What moves it is capital per worker, better technology, and reallocating
> work towards firms and industries that produce more per hour. Every
> serious remedy on offer is one of those three, and each has losers, which
> is why none of them is easy.

## The question to argue

Does a productivity gap matter to a person who is not an economist? Make
the case both ways. Productivity growth is what lets real wages rise
without inflation, which is [[Growth and Productivity]]'s claim. Against
that: output per hour says nothing about who receives the output, which is
[[Inequality]]'s claim, and nothing about the environmental cost of
producing it, which is [[Sustainability and the Economy]]'s.
