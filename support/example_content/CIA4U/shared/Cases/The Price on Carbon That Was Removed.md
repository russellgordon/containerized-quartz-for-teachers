Canada ran a consumer carbon price for six years and then stopped. The
industrial one continues. This is the clearest case in the course of an
economic instrument meeting politics, and of how easily the two halves of
a policy get discussed as though they were one thing.

## What happened, in order

The federal fuel charge — the part households paid at the pump and on the
gas bill — was set to zero on 1 April 2025 and formally repealed by
legislation in March 2026. What remains as the federal backstop is the
Output-Based Pricing System, which covers large industrial emitters.
Environment and Climate Change Canada updated the benchmark effective
15 May 2026: \$95 a tonne in 2026, \$100 in 2027, \$115 in 2030, \$130 in
2035 and \$140 in 2040. Alberta has frozen its own industrial carbon price
for 2026, which is a live test of how a federal benchmark works when a
province declines to follow it.

## The evidence fight, and what it was actually about

The Parliamentary Budget Officer published distributional analyses of the
fuel charge, and in April 2024 acknowledged that its 2022 and 2023 versions
had inadvertently included the industrial system in the modelling when they
were meant to cover only the consumer levy. Its October 2024 update found
that most households received more in rebates than they paid in fuel charge
and the GST on it — but that most income quintiles came out behind once
modelled economy-wide effects on output and investment income were
included.

That is one dataset producing two headlines, and both were used. The real
disagreement is about what "cost" means: the cash transfer alone, or the
transfer plus the modelled effect on the economy — and, on the other side
of the ledger, whether to count the emissions avoided at all.

> [!warning] Read the two prices as two policies
> A great deal of published commentary on "the carbon tax" does not say
> which price it means. Before you quote any claim about cost, incidence or
> emissions, establish whether it concerns the consumer fuel charge that
> ended in 2025 or the industrial system that continues at \$95 a tonne in
> 2026. A claim about one is not evidence about the other.

## What to argue

Take the position of a household, a heavy emitter, a province, and a person
who will be alive in 2070, and set out what each one gained and lost on
1 April 2025. Then answer the harder question that [[Externalities]] sets
up: if a carbon price is the efficient instrument, and it was removed for
reasons that were not about efficiency, what does that tell you about how
economic policy is actually made?
