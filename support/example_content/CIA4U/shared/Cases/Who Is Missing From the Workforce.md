For most of the past decade Canadian employers described a labour
shortage and Canadian policy responded by raising immigration. Both of
those have now changed direction, and the case is to work out what the
numbers actually support.

## Two sets of figures, moving at once

On the labour side: the unemployment rate was 6.4% in July 2026, down 0.1
percentage points and a third consecutive monthly decline, the lowest
since July 2024, with employment up 75,000 (Statistics Canada, table
14-10-0287-01, released 7 August 2026). Job vacancies rose to 506,700 in
the first quarter of 2026, up 2.4% — the first quarterly increase since
the second quarter of 2022 (released 16 June 2026).

On the population side: Canada's population was 41,417,056 on 1 April
2026, down 55,025 over the quarter. Non-permanent residents fell by
117,879 to 2,558,562, and 83,149 permanent immigrants were admitted in the
quarter, 20.2% fewer than in the same quarter of 2025 (released 17 June
2026). The Immigration Levels Plan 2026–2028 sets 380,000 permanent
resident admissions in each of 2026, 2027 and 2028, with about 64% in the
economic class, and commits to bringing non-permanent residents below 5%
of the population by the end of 2027.

So: unemployment falling, vacancies rising for the first time in nearly
four years, and the population shrinking. Those three facts are consistent
with each other, and saying how is the case.

> [!question] Is a shortage a shortage, or a price?
> An economist's first response to "we cannot find workers" is "at what
> wage?" A genuine shortage means quantity demanded exceeds quantity
> supplied at the going wage — which a market usually resolves by raising
> the wage. Ask, for any industry claiming a shortage: has its offered wage
> risen faster than the average? If not, what stops it? Sometimes the
> answer is a regulated price, sometimes a thin margin, sometimes an
> employer who would rather wait.

## The stakeholders, and what each one counts

Employers count vacancies. Unions count wages and working conditions, and
argue that easy access to temporary workers weakens both. Newcomers count
the pathway to permanent residence and the recognition of credentials
earned elsewhere. Municipalities count housing, schools and clinics —
which is why this case and [[The Cost of a Place to Live]] are the same
case seen from two sides. Nobody in that list is being unreasonable, which
is what makes it a real economic problem rather than a moral one.

Bring [[Supply and Demand]] for the wage question and [[Unemployment]] for
what the 6.4% does and does not include — a person who stops looking for
work leaves the labour force and stops being counted as unemployed.
