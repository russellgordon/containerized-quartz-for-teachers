A federal budget is a very long document with a small number of load-
bearing pages. Most of it is announcement; the part that binds is the
fiscal tables at the back. Learn to go there first.

Four numbers carry the argument, and they are not interchangeable.

| Number | What it is | The mistake people make |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue | What the government takes in this year | Treating a tax rate cut as a revenue cut of the same size |
| Programme spending | What it spends, excluding interest | Confusing it with total spending |
| Deficit | Revenue minus total spending, one year | Calling it the debt |
| Debt-to-GDP | Accumulated debt against the size of the economy | Ignoring the denominator, which also moves |

A deficit is a flow — one year's shortfall. Debt is the stock those flows
accumulate into. A government can run a deficit and still see its
debt-to-GDP ratio fall, if the economy grows faster than the borrowing.
That is not a trick; it is arithmetic, and it is the centre of most
Canadian fiscal argument.

## A worked case, with the dates attached

Budget 2025 was tabled on 4 November 2025 by Finance Minister
François-Philippe Champagne and projected a deficit of \$78.3 billion for
2025–26. The Spring Economic Update of 28 April 2026 reported that the
year had actually closed at about \$67 billion, while leaving the forward
track roughly unchanged, near \$50 billion — about 1.4% of GDP — by the end
of the projection period. The Parliamentary Budget Officer has publicly
questioned whether those forward targets will be met.

Three things worth noticing in that paragraph. The outcome beat the
projection, which happens often enough that a projection should never be
reported as a fact. The forward track did not improve, which tells you the
better outcome was treated as one-off. And an independent officer disputes
the numbers, which means the disagreement is public and you can read both
sides rather than choosing an authority.

> [!note] Provincial budgets are where school and hospital money lives
> Health care, education and most social assistance are provincial
> responsibilities, funded partly by federal transfers. If your question is
> about a hospital, a classroom or a shelter bed, the federal budget is the
> wrong document — start with Ontario's, and follow the transfer.

Budgets change behaviour before they change anything else, which is why
[[Incentives]] belongs beside this page, and why [[The Policy Brief]] asks
you to say what a measure will make people do.
