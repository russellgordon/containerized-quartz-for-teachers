This model puts a whole economy on one pair of axes: real output along the
bottom, the price level up the side. It looks like the market diagram and
it is not one, and treating it as one is the commonest error in this unit.

In a single market, demand slopes down partly because buyers switch to
something else. Here there is nothing to switch to — the price level is
the price of everything at once. Aggregate demand slopes down for three
other reasons: a higher price level leaves money already held worth less;
it pushes interest rates up, so investment and durables fall; and it makes
Canadian goods dearer against foreign ones, so net exports fall.

Short-run aggregate supply slopes up because some wages and prices are
fixed by contract while others move, so a higher price level widens
margins and draws out more output. Long-run aggregate supply is vertical:
once everything has adjusted, output is set by resources, skills and
productivity, and not by prices at all.

## Two kinds of shock

A demand shock — a collapse in confidence, a sharp fiscal contraction —
moves aggregate demand left, and output and the price level fall together.
That is painful but tractable, because one lever pushes both back. A
supply shock is the hard case: a tariff or an energy price jump moves
short-run supply left, output falls while prices rise, and a single
instrument cannot chase two variables moving in opposite directions.

> [!success]- Worked answer — a supply shock, curve by curve
> Short-run aggregate supply shifts left. Output falls below potential and
> the price level rises, which is stagflation in one sentence. Cut the
> policy rate and you support output while adding to inflation; raise it
> and you hold inflation down while output falls further. No setting fixes
> both. What is left is a judgement about which risk is the worse one.

That bind is what a central bank is describing when it holds. The Bank of
Canada kept its target for the overnight rate at 2.25 per cent on 15 July
2026, and its July 2026 projection has growth of 0.7 per cent in 2026 and
1.8 per cent in 2027, with inflation back near 2 per cent by early 2027.
Where an economy sits in the cycle is [[Business Cycles]]; what the price
level itself is doing is [[Inflation]].
