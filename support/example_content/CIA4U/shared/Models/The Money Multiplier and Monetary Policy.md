Banks do not simply store money. The act of lending it creates more of it,
and the money multiplier is the model that shows how. It is also a good
lesson in how far to trust a model when the institution it describes has
moved on.

## From one deposit to many

Deposit \$1,000 in a bank that keeps a tenth of its deposits as reserves.
It holds \$100 and lends \$900. Whoever borrows that spends it, and the
person paid banks it somewhere; that bank holds \$90 and lends \$810. Each
round is smaller than the last, and the deposits created by the whole
sequence add to \$10,000 — ten times the original deposit, from a reserve
ratio of one tenth.

The relationship has a name. Write r for the fraction of deposits a bank
holds back as reserves rather than lending on; the simple deposit
multiplier is its reciprocal.

$$m = \frac{1}{r}$$

Now the caution. The formula assumes every bank lends everything it is not
holding back, that every dollar lent returns as a deposit, and that
someone creditworthy always wants to borrow. Banks hold excess reserves
when lending looks risky, cash drains out into wallets, and credit demand
falls in a downturn — so the real figure is always below the formula's.

## The instrument that actually moves

The Bank of Canada does not conduct policy by setting a reserve ratio. It
sets a target for the overnight rate — what financial institutions charge
each other to borrow overnight — and the rest of the country's borrowing
prices off it. The target was held at 2.25 per cent on 15 July 2026, a
sixth consecutive hold, with the Bank Rate at 2.5 per cent and the deposit
rate at 2.20 per cent; the next fixed announcement date is 2 September
2026. Do not quote those figures from this page in a year. The live series
is Bank of Canada Valet `V39079`, "Target for the overnight rate", and it
will tell you today's. What the rate is aimed at is [[Inflation]]; where
it enters the economy is the saving-to-investment join in
[[Circular Flow]].
