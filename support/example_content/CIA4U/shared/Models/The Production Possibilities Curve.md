A production possibilities curve is the scarcity argument drawn once. Two
goods, every resource employed, technology fixed, one period of time —
strip an economy down that far and the trade-off you keep hearing
described becomes a boundary you can point at.

## What the picture assumes

Those assumptions are not decoration. Two goods, because a page has two
axes and the argument survives the simplification. All resources employed,
because the curve is a boundary of what is possible, not a record of what
happened. Technology and the stock of resources fixed, because otherwise
the boundary would be moving while you argued about a point on it.

A point **on** the curve is efficient: you cannot get more of one good
without giving up some of the other, and what you gave up is the
opportunity cost of what you gained. A point **inside** is waste —
unemployed labour, idle plant, a skills mismatch. A point **outside** is
unattainable now, which is not the same as unattainable.

## Why it bows, and what moves it

It bows outward because resources are not equally good at both jobs. Shift
the workers and land best suited to the first good across to the second
and you lose a great deal for very little gain, so each further unit costs
more than the last. The whole boundary moves outward with more resources,
better-trained labour or better technology, and inward when those are lost.

> [!question] Where would you put each of these?
> Draw one curve — capital goods against consumer goods — and place three
> Canadian choices on it: a province deciding between a new hospital and a
> transit line; a student deciding between four years of university and
> four years of wages; a mill idled by a shortage of skilled labour. Two
> are choices between points on the curve. One is not. Which, and why?

What the model leaves out is who gets the output. An economy can sit
exactly on its frontier, entirely efficient, and hand the result to
anyone at all — the curve is silent about that, and a great deal of
political argument is about nothing else. The curve is a drawing of
[[Opportunity Cost]]; the argument underneath it is [[Scarcity and Choice]].
