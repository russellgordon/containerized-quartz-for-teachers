The same tax lands very differently on two different goods, and elasticity
is the reason. [[Supply and Demand]] tells you which way quantity moves
when price moves. Elasticity asks the question that actually decides a
policy: how much.

## Measuring the response

Price elasticity of demand is a ratio of two percentage changes — the
percentage change in quantity demanded over the percentage change in
price. Percentages are used so that the answer does not depend on whether
you counted in litres or barrels, or priced in cents or dollars.

$$E_d = \frac{\%\,\Delta Q_d}{\%\,\Delta P}$$

The result is negative, since the changes run in opposite directions, so
economists compare its size and set the sign aside. Larger than one is
**elastic** — quantity moves proportionally more than price; smaller than
one is **inelastic**; exactly one is **unit elastic**. If price rises
10 per cent and quantity falls 4 per cent, the answer is 0.4.

## What makes a good elastic

- **Substitutes.** The more of them, and the closer, the more elastic —
  one brand of coffee is elastic, coffee itself much less so.
- **Necessity.** Insulin is inelastic at almost any price; a holiday is not.
- **Share of income.** Nobody re-plans a month over the price of salt.
- **Time.** Demand grows more elastic the longer buyers have to rearrange.

## The policy consequence

Tax an inelastic good and you raise revenue and change little behaviour.
Tax an elastic good and you change behaviour and raise little revenue. No
tax gives a government both, so always ask which of the two a particular
tax was built for — that is an argument about [[Incentives]]. Note what
this model cannot hand you: an elasticity for any actual Canadian good.
Statistics Canada publishes the prices — gasoline rose 20.5 per cent year
over year in June 2026, Table 18-10-0004-01, released 20 July 2026 — but
the matching quantities sit elsewhere, and an elasticity is something you
have to compute, with both series and their dates named.
