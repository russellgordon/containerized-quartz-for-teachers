Your mark comes from what you can do with economics, not from how much of
it you can recite. Ontario splits the work into four achievement
categories, and every assessed piece in this course is marked against some
combination of them.

## The four categories, in economics

| Category | What it means here | Where you meet it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Knowledge and Understanding | The concepts, the models, and what each one is for | Every unit; heaviest in Unit 1 |
| Thinking | Choosing the right model, criticising the source, judging whether a claim survives its own evidence | [[The Firm Study]], [[The Policy Brief]], [[The Trade Question]] |
| Communication | An argument a reader can follow, with charts that are labelled and sources that are named | Discussions, and every written task |
| Application | Taking a model to a live Canadian question and getting a defensible answer out of it | [[The Intervention Argument]], [[The Economic Issue Report]] |

Thinking is where this course lives. Knowing what elasticity is belongs to
the first category; deciding that elasticity — rather than market structure
— is the right lens for the question in front of you, and then saying why
the estimate you found is or is not credible, belongs to the second. That
judgement is what separates a good paper here from a tidy one.

## Term work and the final evaluation

Term work is worth 70% of the course mark and the final evaluation 30%,
which is Ontario's standard split. Within the term, the eight assessed
tasks carry most of the weight, with [[The Economic Issue Report]] — the
culminating task — carrying the largest single share of it. Prepared
participation in discussions and the entries in your portfolio account for
the rest. The final evaluation is [[The Data Examination]], three hours,
with real tables supplied.

## What is worth marks, and what is not

Say this one out loud: **a correct number with no source is worth less
here than a well-sourced approximation.** A figure you cannot attribute
cannot be checked, cannot be updated, and cannot be argued with, so it
does no work in an argument no matter how right it happens to be. A
figure that names its table and its release date can be checked by anyone,
including by you in three weeks when it has changed.

The rubric for each piece of work is on that task's own page. Read it
before you start rather than after you finish.
