Gross domestic product is the value of final goods and services produced
in a country in a period. It can be built two ways, and Statistics Canada
publishes both: by expenditure — consumption, investment, government
spending, and exports less imports — and by industry, adding up value
added in each sector. Where the two diverge, the gap itself is telling.

## The indicators and where they live

| Indicator | What it answers | Statistics Canada table |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Real GDP, quarterly | Is total output growing, and by how much | 36-10-0104-01 |
| GDP by industry, monthly | Which sectors moved, and in which month | 36-10-0434-01 |
| Consumer Price Index | How fast the price of a fixed basket is rising | 18-10-0004-01 |
| Labour Force Survey | Who is working, looking, or out of the labour force | 14-10-0287-01 |
| Labour productivity | How much output an hour of work produces | 36-10-0206-01 |

Learn the table numbers rather than the values. Real GDP was 0.0% in the
first quarter of 2026, released 29 May 2026 from Table 36-10-0104-01 —
true on the day it was published and superseded on the next release date.
The number goes stale; the lookup does not.

## What GDP does not count

GDP counts what is transacted and measured. It leaves out unpaid
household work — care, cooking, driving a family member to an
appointment — and volunteering, both of which are production by any
ordinary meaning. It does not net off environmental depletion, so cutting
a forest adds to GDP and losing it subtracts nothing. It misses black and
grey market activity, by definition, since that activity is hidden. And
it is a total, so it says nothing about who received the output.

None of this makes GDP a bad measure. It makes it a measure of one thing.
The judgement you are asked for is not "GDP is flawed" — it is naming,
for a specific question, whether GDP is the right instrument or whether a
distributional or non-market measure answers it better.
