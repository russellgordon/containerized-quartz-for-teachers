Everything an economy produces comes out of four factors of production:
**land** (natural resources), **labour** (human effort and skill),
**capital** (tools, machines, buildings and software used to produce other
things), and **entrepreneurship** (combining the other three and carrying
the risk of being wrong). Growth means producing more from them, by two
routes only: use more of the factors, or get more from the same quantity.

The second route is productivity, and the definition must be exact. Labour
productivity is **output per hour worked** — not effort. A worker with
better equipment, a larger market, or a better-organised firm produces more
per hour without trying harder, which is why "Canadians need to work
harder" answers a question about capital with a claim about effort.

## What the measured record shows

Statistics Canada reported labour productivity down 0.5% in the first
quarter of 2026, after a 0.3% decline in the fourth quarter of 2025 —
goods-producing businesses fell 1.7%, services rose 0.3%. Released 3 June
2026, Table 36-10-0206-01. Quarterly moves are noisy; do not lean on one.
The longer record is harder to dismiss. In *Economic and Social Reports*,
March 2026, Statistics Canada found the Canada–United States productivity
gap has widened by roughly 26% since 2000. The same work reports that in
2019 small firms produced 56% of nominal GDP in Canada against 45% in the
United States, and that Canadian small firms ran at about 70% of their US
counterparts' labour productivity, large firms at 87%.

> [!question] Why is the gap there?
> The gap itself is not disputed. Its cause is. Statistics Canada's March
> 2026 work points at firm size and capital intensity; others argue that
> competition policy, interprovincial barriers, or the measurement of
> service output carry more weight. Each implies a different policy, so
> repeating the 26% settles nothing.

Growth is not automatically a gain. Higher output can arrive with
deforestation, emissions and depleted fisheries attached, and those costs
fall on people who were not party to the transaction. The Canadian case is
in [[The Productivity Question]]; what GDP omits, [[Measuring an Economy]].
