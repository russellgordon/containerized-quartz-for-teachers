An efficient outcome and a fair one are two different questions, and
economics can only settle one of them. Keeping them apart is most of what
separates careful economic writing from advocacy dressed up as analysis.

## Efficiency is a question about waste

An allocation is efficient when you cannot make anyone better off without
making someone worse off — when nothing more can be squeezed out of the
factors of production you have. That is a technical claim, arguable with
evidence. Moving manufacturing to where capital is used more intensively
raises measured efficiency; economies of scale lower unit costs and can
lower prices. The same moves displace workers in one place and load
environmental cost onto another. Efficiency says output per unit of input
rose — not that the trade was worth making.

## Equity is a question about distribution

Equity asks who ends up with what, and whether that pattern is
defensible. It is a values question, which does not make it soft — you
can measure the distribution precisely and then argue about it honestly.
Canada's headline measure is the Market Basket Measure poverty rate:
11.0% of Canadians in 2024, Statistics Canada Table 11-10-0135-01,
released 29 April 2026. It describes the outcome, not the target.

Governments act on the second question with tools that cost something on
the first: progressive income tax, child benefits, employment insurance,
social assistance. Each changes an incentive, and the size of that cost
is contested. Deregulating and privatising Crown corporations pushed back.

> [!note] An efficient outcome can be one nobody would choose
> Efficiency is silent about the starting point. An allocation in which
> one person holds everything, and no trade could improve anyone's
> position without harming another, is efficient. An argument that stops
> at "this is efficient" has not yet said the policy is good.

The measures behind the second question are in [[Inequality]]; the case
that growth is the wrong target is argued in [[Is Growth the Right Goal|Is Growth the Right Goal?]].
