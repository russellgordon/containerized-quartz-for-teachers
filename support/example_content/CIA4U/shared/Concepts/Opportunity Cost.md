The opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the next best alternative
you gave up in order to make it. Not "what it cost you" in the loose sense,
and not the sum of everything you did not do — one thing: the best of the
options you turned down. Getting that definition exactly right is what
separates an economic argument from a complaint about money.

## A decision you are actually making

Compare one year of full-time university against one year of full-time work
at Ontario's general minimum wage. The wage is \$17.60 an hour, rising to
\$17.95 on 1 October 2026 — the increase was announced on 1 April 2026 and
is a 1.9% adjustment based on the Ontario Consumer Price Index.

To do the comparison you need three quantities, and only one of them is on
this page:

- The wage, above.
- The hours you would actually be scheduled. Assume nothing here; a
  "full-time" retail schedule is often not 40 hours.
- Tuition, fees and the cost of living where you would study. Get these
  from the institution's own published fee schedule for the year you would
  enter, and check Statistics Canada's annual tuition fees release in *The
  Daily* for the national picture. Do not use a number you remember.

> [!success]- The worked answer — open it after you have made your own
> Take 40 hours a week for 50 weeks — 2,000 hours. At \$17.60 that is
> \$35,200 gross for the year, before any deduction; at \$17.95 it is
> \$35,900. That forgone income is the largest part of the opportunity
> cost of the university year, and it appears on no tuition invoice. Add
> the tuition and fees you looked up. Subtract nothing for the degree's
> later earnings — those belong to a longer comparison, and folding them
> in here quietly changes the question you asked.

A whole year against a whole year is not how most decisions arrive. When
the question is one more shift or one more course, use [[Marginal Thinking]]
instead. Record the decision, the alternative and your reasoning in
[[A Model of Your Own]], and check it later against what happened.
