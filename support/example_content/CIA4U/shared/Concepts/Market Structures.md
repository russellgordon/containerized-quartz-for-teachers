A market's structure is the answer to one question — how much does any
single firm's decision move the price? Everything else follows from that:
how much a firm can charge, how hard it must work to keep you, and how
much a regulator has to pay attention.

## The four structures

| Structure | Firms | Price-setting power | Who gains | Who loses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Perfect competition | Many, identical product | None — the firm takes the price | Consumers, through low prices | Firms, whose profit is competed away |
| Monopolistic competition | Many, differentiated product | Some, from brand and location | Consumers who value variety | Consumers paying for differences that are mostly packaging |
| Oligopoly | Few, watching each other | Considerable, and interdependent | Incumbents, and workers with bargaining power | Entrants, and consumers when rivalry goes soft |
| Monopoly | One | Set by the firm, bounded by demand and regulation | The firm — and the public, where the scale is genuinely efficient | Consumers, and anyone trying to enter |

Canada gives you real cases in the right-hand columns. Grocery is the
standard one. The Competition Bureau's retail grocery market study,
*Canada Needs More Grocery Competition*, published 27 June 2023, found
that Loblaw, Sobeys and Metro together reported more than \$100 billion
in sales and more than \$3.6 billion in profits in 2022. Concentration is
not by itself proof of wrongdoing — but it tells you how much one firm's
pricing decision matters. Telecommunications and domestic airlines sit in
the same column for a related reason: a national network is expensive to
build once and painful to build twice.

## What online marketplaces did to the structure

Technology changed structure, not only price. Search made comparison
nearly free, which pushes some markets toward the competitive end.
Reviews and published specifications reduced the information gap between
seller and buyer. At the same time, platforms concentrated the point of
sale: a marketplace that hosts thousands of sellers and also competes
with them holds a position no single seller does. Your digital footprint
became an input, so that the price you are shown can depend on what is
known about you. Ask of any online market which of these dominates —
they do not all point the same way.

The costs behind these structures are in [[Costs and the Firm]]; the
Canadian evidence is worked through in
