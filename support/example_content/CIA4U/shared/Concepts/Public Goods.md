"Public good" is a technical term, not a compliment. It does not mean a
good thing the public wants. It means a good with two specific
properties, and those properties predict that a market will under-supply
it however much people want it.

## The two properties, crossed

| | Rival | Non-rival |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Excludable** | Private good — a loaf of bread, a bus seat at capacity | Club good — a streaming subscription, a toll bridge below capacity |
| **Non-excludable** | Common resource — a fish stock, groundwater, a congested road | Public good — national defence, flood control, basic research, a lighthouse |

Rival means your use leaves less for me. Excludable means someone can
keep me out unless I pay. The bottom-right cell is the problem: if you
cannot keep me out and my use costs you nothing, I have every reason to
wait for someone else to pay, and everyone reasoning that way funds
nothing. A lighthouse keeps every ship off the rocks; flood control
protects the whole floodplain; a published result can be read by anyone.
Markets under-supply these not because firms are shortsighted, but
because no firm can collect.

## The cases students usually get wrong

Health care and roads are the two that trip people up. A hospital bed is
rival — one patient in it means one fewer available — and excludable,
since a payment could be demanded at the door. By the test above, it is a
private good. Canada provides it publicly anyway, for reasons that are
real but different: risk pooling that private insurance markets handle
badly, the fact that people cannot judge or postpone urgent care, and a
political commitment to access. Most roads are excludable in principle,
as every toll highway proves, and rival once congested.

That distinction matters when you argue about a budget. "It is a public
good" is a claim about market failure that you must show. "We provide it
publicly because access should not depend on income" is a claim about
values, and a respectable one — but it needs different evidence.
