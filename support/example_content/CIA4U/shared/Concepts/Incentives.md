People respond to what they are actually rewarded for, which is not always
what the person designing the reward had in mind. When you analyse a tax, a
subsidy, a wage floor or a rebate, the useful question is not "is this a
good idea" but "what does this pay people to do, and who can do it?"
Prices are the commonest incentive, and taxes and subsidies move them
deliberately: a sales tax raises the price a buyer faces and cuts quantity.

## A designed incentive

Canada's federal carbon price now reaches large industrial emitters alone,
through the Output-Based Pricing System. The Environment and Climate Change
Canada benchmark effective 15 May 2026 sets it at \$95 per tonne in 2026,
rising to \$100 in 2027 and \$115 in 2030. The intent is plain: make
emitting expensive enough that a firm's own cost calculation favours
abatement, without telling it how. Whether the incentive bites depends on
whether it is applied — Alberta has frozen its industrial carbon price for
2026, so the same tonne carries a different cost depending on province.

## An incentive nobody designed

Unreported work exists because both parties face a real reward: the payer
avoids tax and the worker keeps more of a small wage. Nobody intended it —
it is a by-product of taxing recorded transactions. The costs land
elsewhere: no employment standards coverage, no contribution record, no
recourse when a job goes wrong. For its scale, look up Statistics Canada's
underground economy estimates through *The Daily* rather than repeating a
figure you have heard; they are modelled, and the method matters.

> [!warning] Incentives can produce the opposite of the intention
> A rule that pays hospitals per procedure rewards procedures, not health.
> A fine for collecting children late from daycare can raise lateness, by
> converting an obligation into a price people will pay. State plainly what
> behaviour a policy pays for before you endorse it, and ask who is best
> placed to respond in a way nobody wanted.
