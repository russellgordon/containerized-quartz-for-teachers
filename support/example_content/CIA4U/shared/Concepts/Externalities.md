An externality is a cost or a benefit that falls on somebody who was not
party to the transaction. The buyer and the seller agree a price; a third
party gets the smoke, or the quieter street, or the immunity. Because that
third party is not at the table, the price does not carry their cost or
their benefit, and the market produces too much of the thing when the
effect is negative and too little when it is positive.

Negative and positive externalities are the same idea with opposite signs.
Neither is an accusation of bad behaviour. A firm emitting within the law
and a household driving to work are both producing effects the price does
not count, which is precisely why policy — rather than goodwill — is the
usual instrument.

## Third-party effects and the policies aimed at them

| The activity | Who bears the cost or receives the benefit | The policy that tries to price it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emissions from a large industrial facility | People downwind now, and everyone exposed to climate damage later | The federal Output-Based Pricing System, at \$95 per tonne in 2026 under the ECCC benchmark effective 15 May 2026 |
| Household and vehicle fuel use | The same third parties, in smaller individual amounts | The federal consumer fuel charge — set to zero on 1 April 2025 and repealed by legislation in March 2026, so no federal charge now applies |
| Driving into a city at rush hour | Every other driver, who loses time | Tolls and transit subsidies; Ontario's Highway 407 is the province's standing example |
| Immunisation | People who are never exposed because transmission is interrupted | Publicly funded immunisation programmes, which pay for a benefit the buyer alone would undervalue |
| Completing post-secondary education | Employers, future colleagues, and the tax base | Public operating grants, student aid and income-contingent repayment |

The bottom two rows are positive externalities, and they explain a policy
pattern worth noticing: governments tax what they want less of and
subsidise what they want more of, which is the same instrument pointed in
two directions.

Note also what the second row means. The removal of the consumer fuel
charge did not remove the externality — it removed the price attached to
it. Whether a household-level price should exist at all, and who should
carry the cost if it does not, is exactly the dispute in
[[Who Should Bear the Cost of Emissions?]]. Where a benefit is so widely
shared that no individual will pay for it, you have crossed into
[[Public Goods]].
