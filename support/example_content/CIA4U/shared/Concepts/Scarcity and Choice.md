Scarcity is not the same thing as poverty. Scarcity is the permanent
condition that the resources available to you — land, labour, capital,
entrepreneurship — are finite, while the uses you could put them to are
not. A wealthy country faces scarcity as surely as a poor one, because
every hour of skilled labour spent on one task is an hour not spent on
another.

Because resources are scarce, every economy has to answer three questions.
It answers them whether or not anybody sits down to decide.

- **What** will be produced, and in what quantities?
- **How** will it be produced — with which mix of labour, capital and land?
- **For whom** is it produced — who ends up holding the output?

What distinguishes one economic system from another is not whether it
answers those questions. It is what does the answering: prices, custom, or
a central authority.

## Four systems, and where you can look

| System | What answers the three questions | Where to look |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market | Prices and private ownership, with little state direction | Singapore, Hong Kong — cited as the closest cases, though no pure one exists |
| Mixed | Prices, plus a state that taxes, regulates, transfers, and produces some goods itself | Canada, Germany, Japan |
| Traditional | Custom, kinship and inherited role | Subsistence economies; elements persist in many rural regions |
| Command | Central planning and state ownership of the means of production | North Korea; Cuba, partially and unevenly |

Calling Canada "mixed" is not a hedge. It has content. Most prices here are
set by markets, but hospital and physician care is publicly funded and
publicly insured, schooling is compulsory and largely public, labour is
regulated down to a statutory floor — Ontario's general minimum wage is
\$17.60 an hour, rising to \$17.95 on 1 October 2026 — and a large share of
income is moved by taxes and transfers. So the live question in this course
is never "market or state". It is where, in one particular market, the line
currently sits, and what argument put it there.

Every answer to the three questions costs you the alternative you gave up;
that is [[Opportunity Cost]]. When you want the trade-off drawn rather than
described, use [[The Production Possibilities Curve]].
