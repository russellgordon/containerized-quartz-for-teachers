Before you ever took a drama course, you were fluent in drama. A wedding is
staged; a hockey team's pre-game ritual is choreographed; the story your
uncle tells at dinner has been revised, tightened, and performed a dozen
times; the videos you watch tonight will use character, timing, and
[[Tension|tension]] on you deliberately. Communities present and past have
used drama to entertain, to teach, to worship, to mourn, and to remember —
long before anyone built a theatre to hold it. This discussion is about
noticing the drama you already live inside.

## Questions worth arguing about

1. Name a ceremony you have attended — religious, athletic, family,
   school. What was rehearsed about it? Who was the audience, and what was
   the performance *for*?
2. A story told at a dinner table changes with retelling: details grow,
   timing sharpens. Is that performance dishonest, or is it something
   else?
3. Ancient Greek theatre grew out of religious festivals; many Indigenous
   traditions carry teachings through performed story. What do communities
   get from drama that a written explanation cannot deliver?
4. What could someone understand about a culture from the drama it makes —
   its rituals, its shows, its stories — that a textbook could not tell
   them? What might a stranger learn about us from ours?
5. Sports crowds chant in unison; ten thousand people fall silent
   together. Where else does ordinary life turn a crowd into something
   like a chorus — and why does it feel so powerful from the inside?

Keep this discussion in your pocket for the rest of the course. When a
convention like [[Choral Speaking]] feels artificial, remember that you
have already seen it in the world — drama class did not invent these
tools, it only names them. [[Drama Journal]] entries that connect our
classwork to drama you noticed outside school are some of the best entries
there are.
