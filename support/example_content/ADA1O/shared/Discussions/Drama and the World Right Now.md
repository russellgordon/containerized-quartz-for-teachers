Every play, film, and series is made by people living somewhere, at some
moment, worried about something. You can read the moment off the work —
what it takes for granted, who it laughs at, what it treats as an
obvious happy ending. Today we practise reading it.

## Before we talk

Bring one thing you have watched recently: a film, an episode, a play, a
sketch, a piece of theatre on a phone screen. It does not have to be
good. It has to be something you can describe.

## What we are discussing

1. **What does it assume?** Every story takes something for granted
   about how life works — who has money, who has authority, what a
   family looks like, what counts as success. Name one assumption yours
   makes without ever stating it.
2. **Who is the joke on?** Comedy dates faster than anything else,
   because it runs on who an audience finds ridiculous. Whose expense
   was your example's humour at, and would that have been different ten
   years ago?
3. **What is it worried about?** Stories cluster around a fear —
   loneliness, surveillance, climate, being left behind. What is yours
   afraid of?
4. **Which stereotype is doing the work?** A stock character is a
   shortcut: the audience gets the whole person in three seconds. That
   is efficient and it is also how a stereotype gets rehearsed in front
   of a thousand people. Which shortcut did your example use, and what
   did it cost?

## The long view

The forms in [[Where Drama Comes From]] all answered the moment they
came from. Greek tragedy argued about duty in a city that was inventing
citizenship; commedia's masters and servants were funny to audiences who
lived exactly that hierarchy. Contemporary work is doing the same thing,
and we simply cannot see our own moment as clearly — which is why this
conversation is worth having out loud rather than alone.

> [!note] Arguing well about work you love
> The point is not to catch a favourite show being bad. Work can be
> excellent and still carry its era's assumptions, and noticing that is
> a compliment to the work: it means you took it seriously enough to
> read it. [[Our Ensemble Agreement]] applies — we argue with ideas, not
> at the person who liked the thing.

Afterwards, in your [[Drama Journal]]: one assumption you found in
something you love, and one you suspect our own class pieces have been
making all semester.
