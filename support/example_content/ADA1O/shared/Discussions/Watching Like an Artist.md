You will spend more of this course watching than performing, and watching
is a skill — not a rest between turns. An artist's first move as an
audience member is to **describe before judging**: not "that was good" but
"the tallest figure was turned away from the group, and everyone else was
looking at her". Description sees choices — levels,
[[Focus and Emphasis|focus]], stillness, a shift in [[Status|status]] —
and choices can be discussed, borrowed, and made differently next time.
Judgement only sees talent, and talent is the least interesting thing on
any stage.

## Questions worth arguing about

1. "That was good" and "that was boring" both end conversations. What is
   the most useful thing an audience member has ever said to you about
   your work — and what made it useful?
2. Is it possible to describe a performance with no judgement hiding in
   the description? Try it with the last thing our class watched, and let
   the others hunt for the smuggled verdict.
3. We usually treat theatre etiquette as rules — sit still, phone away,
   applaud. What changes if you treat it instead as generosity toward
   someone taking a risk? Which rules survive that reframing, and which
   turn out to be only habits?
4. An audience can lift a scene or sink it without making a sound. What
   have you felt from an audience while performing — and what, exactly,
   were they doing?
5. When something goes wrong on stage, what does the *audience* owe the
   performer in that moment?

Every task in this course makes you an audience many more times than a
performer, so this skill gets more practice than any other. Use the
describe-first habit in class discussion, in your [[Drama Journal]], and
in anything you write for [[Writing About Drama]] — naming choices before
judging them is the whole trick of that page too.
