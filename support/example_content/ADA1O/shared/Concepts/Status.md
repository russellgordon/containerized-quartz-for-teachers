Status is not rank. A king can play low status — apologising, hesitating,
avoiding everyone's eyes — and a servant can play high. **Status is
behaviour**: the moment-to-moment signals that say *I matter more here* or
*you matter more here*.[^1] Once you see it, you cannot stop seeing it — in
scenes, in hallways, in every conversation you have this week. That noticing
is the point; see [[Drama in Everyday Life]].

## The signals are tiny

High status and low status are built from physical choices small enough to
seem accidental:

- **Eye contact.** High status holds it calmly, or withholds it on purpose.
  Low status glances, checks, looks away first.
- **Posture and stillness.** High status keeps the head still while speaking
  and takes up space without apology. Low status fidgets, shrinks, touches
  its own face.
- **Hesitation.** Low status starts sentences with *um*, *sorry*, *I just* —
  and lets itself be interrupted. High status pauses whenever it likes,
  because it knows nobody will fill the gap.

The remarkable thing is that these choices work from the outside in. Adopt
the posture and the feeling follows — which makes status the fastest
character-building tool you have. Decide a character's status and half your
[[Movement and Gesture]] and [[Voice]] choices make themselves.

## The status ladder game

Everyone draws a card, ace low, king high, and keeps it secret. The scene is
mundane — a bus stop, a staff room. Play your card's status, watch everyone
else's behaviour, and at the end the group lines up in the order they *read*
each other. Then reveal the cards.

The game teaches two things at once: how to *play* status precisely, and how
to *read* it — the accuracy of the final line-up is the real score. It is
also the best possible warm-up for [[Improvisation Showcase]], because
improvised scenes come alive the instant two performers start moving on the
ladder: watch what happens when the low-status character rises one rung at a
time.

Scenes about status only work in a room where nobody confuses the character's
status with the performer's worth — one more reason
[[Why Trust Comes First|trust comes first]].

[^1]: The idea of status as a moment-to-moment transaction comes from Keith
    Johnstone's *Impro: Improvisation and the Theatre* (1979), still the book
    working improvisers quote most.
