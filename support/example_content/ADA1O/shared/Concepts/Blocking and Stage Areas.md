**Blocking** is the plan for where performers stand and move on stage — and
the first thing to understand about it is that it is meaning, not traffic.
Deciding who crosses to whom, who turns away, who is left standing alone by
the door: these are statements about the characters, made in space. Two
people delivering the same lines from opposite corners of the stage are in a
different scene than the same two people nose to nose.

## The map is drawn from the actor's view

Stage directions describe the stage from the *performer's* point of view,
facing the audience. Stage right is the actor's right — the audience's
left. Upstage is away from the audience; downstage is towards it. (The
names are older than they sound: stages were once raked, tilted upward at
the back, so walking upstage really was walking uphill.)

Those two axes divide the stage into nine named areas:

| | Stage right | Centre | Stage left |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Upstage** (away from audience) | Up right | Up centre | Up left |
| **Centre** | Right | Centre stage | Left |
| **Downstage** (towards audience) | Down right | Down centre | Down left |

The areas are not equal. Downstage centre is the strongest position on the
stage — closest to the audience, hardest to ignore — and upstage corners
are the weakest. That imbalance is a tool: park a character up left for a
whole scene, then bring them down centre for one line, and the move itself
does half the acting.

## Blocking is focus made permanent

Everything [[Tableau]] taught you about a single frozen picture, blocking
applies to a scene in motion — it is [[Focus and Emphasis|focus]] planned
across time. Bodies point the audience's eye, distance shows relationship,
and levels show power, exactly as in a tableau; blocking just decides how
those pictures flow into one another.

> [!tip] Every cross needs a reason
> The classic beginner note is "wandering" — drifting around the stage
> because standing still feels strange. Flip the rule: stand still until
> the character has a reason to move, then move all the way. A cross made
> *on* a decisive line, for a reason the audience can feel, is blocking.
> A shuffle made from nerves is noise.

When you are watching other groups' work, blocking is one of the aesthetic
choices worth naming in your [[Drama Journal]] — see
[[Watching Like an Artist]] for how to describe what a staging choice is
*doing* rather than just whether you liked it.
