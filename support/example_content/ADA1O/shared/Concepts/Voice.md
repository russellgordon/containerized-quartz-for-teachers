Your voice is an instrument with six strings, and most beginners play only
one — volume. Learn to play the other five and you can reveal a character's
age, mood, background, and secret intentions without changing a single word
of the script. Often the *contrast* between the words and the voice is the
most interesting thing on stage: "I'm fine" said slowly, quietly, with a
pause in the middle, means anything but fine.

## The six choices

| Choice | What varying it can reveal |
| --- | --- |
| Volume | Confidence, intimacy, threat — a whisper can menace better than a shout |
| Pitch | Age, excitement, strain; a pitch that rises under pressure shows nerves |
| Pace | Thought speed — racing when cornered, deliberate when in control |
| Pause | Power and doubt; who can afford silence, and who rushes to fill it |
| Tone | The attitude under the words — warmth, contempt, irony, exhaustion |
| Accent | Where a character is from, and what they want people to assume |

Try one line six ways, changing a single string each time: *"I didn't know
you'd be here."* Delivered fast and high, it is panic. Slow, low, with a
pause before *you'd* — it is a threat. Same words, different character. This
is why voice work belongs to [[Role and Character|character building]], not
decoration, and why a character's [[Status]] is audible before it is
visible.

A word of caution on accent: it is the most powerful string and the easiest
to play badly. An accent should come from a specific, researched choice about
who the character is — never from a lazy impression of a group of people.
When in doubt, drop the accent and let tone and pace do the work.

## Projection is not shouting

Shouting happens in the throat; projection happens in the breath. A shout
raises pitch, strains the voice, and reads as anger whether you meant it or
not. Projection keeps the pitch and tone you chose and simply sends them
further — breath low in the body, mouth open, consonants crisp, aimed at the
back wall. The test: project a gentle line. If it still sounds gentle at the
back of the room, that was projection. If it sounds furious, that was
shouting.

The best place to train all six strings at once is [[Choral Speaking]], where
a group must make voice choices *together* — twenty people matching pace and
tone exactly, or splitting deliberately into echo and unison. You cannot
match what you have not learned to hear.
