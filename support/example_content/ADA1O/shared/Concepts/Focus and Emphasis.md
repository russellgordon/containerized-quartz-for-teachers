An audience can only truly watch one thing at a time. **Focus** is the craft
of deciding what that one thing is, moment by moment — and if you do not
decide, the audience will, and they will often choose wrong. The performer
scratching their nose upstage can erase the most important line of your
scene.

## The tools that steer the eye

Four contrasts do almost all the work:

- **Stage picture.** The arrangement of bodies is an arrow. Where people
  stand, face, and point tells the audience where to look before anyone
  speaks. This is exactly what [[Tableau]] trains — a tableau *is* a stage
  picture with the sound turned off.
- **Levels.** Higher usually reads as more powerful and pulls the eye — which
  is also why levels matter so much in [[Status]] work. One person standing
  while five sit owns the stage.
- **Stillness against movement.** In a still stage picture, the one moving
  person takes focus. In a busy scene, the one still person does. The rarer
  thing wins.
- **Sound against silence.** The same rule with your ears: one voice against
  silence commands attention; a pause after noise is louder than shouting.
  [[Choral Speaking]] plays this contrast constantly — one voice, then
  twenty, then one.

Notice the pattern: focus comes from *contrast*, not from effort. You take
focus by being different from everything around you, and — just as important
— you **give** focus by matching everything around you. Generously giving
focus is a skill; scenes die when everyone tries to take it at once.

## Before you show a stage picture

Run this check as a group before showing any tableau or staged moment:

- [ ] We can say in one sentence what the audience should look at first
- [ ] Every body on stage either points focus there or deliberately gives it
- [ ] At least two levels are in use
- [ ] Something is still enough for the moving thing to matter
- [ ] Anyone not in focus knows their job is to stay out of it

Focus is also how you build rapport with an audience rather than just
performing *at* them — a performer who controls where people look can then
choose to look right back at them. We explore that when we talk about
breaking the fourth wall in [[Improvisation Showcase]] rehearsals, and it is
what separates presenting from communicating.
