There is a difference between *taking on a role* and *performing a character*,
and knowing it will save you a lot of worry this year.

A **role** is a point of view you step into: the mayor at the town meeting,
the friend who saw what happened, the worker whose factory is closing. Roles
need no costume, no accent, no memorised lines — just a willingness to answer
questions *as that person* instead of as yourself. Most of our class work
happens in role, and most of it lasts two minutes at a time.

A **character** is a role you have lived with: a particular person with a
history, habits, and a way of standing that is not yours. Characters are what
you build for performance work like [[Scene Study from a Story]]. Every
character starts life as a role. Nobody skips the first step.

## Three ways into a role

When a role feels like nothing — when you are standing there feeling like
yourself in a hat — reach for these three questions in order:

1. **Given circumstances.** What is true for this person before the scene
   starts? Where were they an hour ago? Who is watching them now?
2. **Want.** What do they want *in this scene* — not in life, in the next two
   minutes? A person who wants something specific stops being a hat.
3. **Obstacle.** What is in the way? The want plus the obstacle is the scene.
   Without an obstacle there is nothing to act — see [[Tension]].

> [!tip] You do not have to feel it — you have to pursue it
> Beginners wait to *feel* like the character. Experienced performers pursue
> the want and let the feeling arrive on its own — or not. The audience reads
> pursuit, not feeling.

Two conventions exist precisely to deepen roles: [[Writing in Role]] slows a
character down enough to discover what they actually think, and
[[Hot Seating]] lets your classmates' questions find the gaps in a character
you thought you knew. Use both before any performance and the character will
hold up under pressure.

Stepping into a role is also the safest kind of risk this course asks of you —
which is why it depends on the classroom we build in
[[Why Trust Comes First]].
