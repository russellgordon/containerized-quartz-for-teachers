Nothing we do in this room was invented in this room. The tableau, the
chorus, the mask, the moment an actor turns and speaks straight to you —
each arrived from somewhere, carried by people who needed it for
something. Knowing where a form came from changes how you use it.

## Storytelling before theatres

The oldest dramatic forms are not buildings. They are people gathered to
tell a story that the community needs told — around a fire, at a
ceremony, at a market. Storytelling traditions of Indigenous peoples in
what is now Canada carry knowledge, law, humour, and history across
generations, and they are living traditions, not historical ones: the
stories belong to particular nations and particular tellers, and some
are told only in certain seasons or by certain people. That is worth
saying plainly here, because "everybody's stories are for everybody" is
a habit that theatre has often had and should not.

## Four forms, and what each one solved

| Form | Where and when | The problem it solved | What we borrow |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Greek theatre | Athens, from the 6th century BCE | A whole city watching one story together, outdoors, far from the stage | The **chorus** — a group who comment, warn, and speak as the community |
| Commedia dell'arte | Italy, 16th–18th centuries | Travelling players, no script, different town each week | **Stock characters** and improvisation on an agreed outline |
| Noh and Kabuki | Japan, 14th and 17th centuries onward | Distilling emotion to its most controlled form; then spectacle for a wider public | **Masks, stylised movement**, and the idea that restraint can be enormous |
| Melodrama | Europe and North America, 19th century | Big houses, gas light, audiences who talked back | Heightened **stakes and clear types** — and the sound cue |

Look at what our own warm-ups descend from. [[Choral Speaking]] is the
Greek chorus, four thousand years on. [[Mime]] and the exaggerated types
in an improvisation set are commedia. The stillness of a held
[[Tableau]] borrows from forms that trusted an audience to read a single
image for a long time.

## Why the origin matters to you

A convention carries its purpose with it. The chorus exists to let a
community speak — so using it for one character's private thought fights
the form. A mask exists to fix an expression and free the body — so
adding a facial performance underneath wastes it.

> [!note]- Borrowing carefully
> Theatre borrows constantly, and that is not itself a problem. The
> question worth asking is whose form it is, whether it is being used
> for what it was made for, and whether the people it came from would
> recognise it. "We saw it in a film once" is not research.
> [[Drama and the World Right Now]] takes this argument into the
> present.
