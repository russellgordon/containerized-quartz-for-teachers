Tension is the element that keeps an audience watching. It is not the same as
conflict — nobody needs to shout. Tension is simply something *unresolved*
that the audience cares about. The moment everything is settled, the scene is
over, whether you have stopped talking or not.

## Four kinds of tension

Drama teachers usually name four kinds. Most good scenes run at least two at
once.

- **Tension of the task.** Someone must do something difficult before
  something else happens. Defusing the bomb. Getting the ring to the volcano
  before the army arrives.
- **Tension of relationships.** Two people want incompatible things from each
  other, and neither can walk away. Almost every argument scene you have ever
  loved is this one.
- **Tension of surprise.** The audience knows something is coming but not
  when. The shark theme starts; the water stays calm.
- **Tension of mystery.** Something is withheld. Who sent the letter? What is
  behind the door? Why will nobody say the name?

> [!question]- Name the tension: three familiar stories (click to expand)
> *Cinderella at the ball as midnight approaches* — task (get out before the
> spell breaks) layered on relationship (she does not want to leave him).
> *The Three Little Pigs* — surprise: we know the wolf is coming; the pigs do
> not. *Any detective story* — mystery, by definition, usually with a task
> tension in the final chase.

## Manufacturing tension on purpose

Tension is not luck — conventions exist to build it deliberately.
[[Flashback and Flash-Forward]] is the clearest example: a flash-forward that
shows the audience where events are heading creates surprise tension in every
scene that follows, because now they are waiting. A [[Tableau]] held three
seconds longer than is comfortable creates tension out of pure stillness.
[[Thought Tracking]] can create relationship tension instantly, by letting
the audience hear a thought the other character never will.

When you build your [[Tableau Story Sequence]], choose the moments of highest
tension — the instant *before* things resolve, not after. An audience leans
toward an unresolved image and away from a finished one.

The quickest diagnosis for a flat scene is one question: *what is unresolved
here, and does the audience know it?* If the answer is nothing, no amount of
louder acting will help — go back and add an obstacle (see
[[Role and Character]]).
