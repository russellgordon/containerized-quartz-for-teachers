Every scene you will make this year — every tableau, improvisation, and
polished performance — is built from the same five materials. Learn their
names and you can talk about *why* a scene works instead of just saying it
was good. That vocabulary is half of what this course teaches.

## The five elements

Each element comes with a test question. Ask it about any scene — one you
made, one you watched, one from a film — and you are doing analysis.

| Element | The test question |
| --- | --- |
| Role and character | Who is this person, and what do they want right now? |
| Relationship | What do these people mean to each other — and is it changing? |
| Time and place | Where and when are we, and how do we know without being told? |
| Tension | What is unresolved? What is the audience waiting to find out? |
| Focus and emphasis | Where is the audience looking, and did someone choose that? |

None of these is optional. A scene with no [[Tension]] is a conversation. A
scene where the audience does not know where to look has performers, but no
[[Focus and Emphasis|focus]]. A scene where nobody wants anything has bodies
on stage, but no [[Role and Character|roles]].

## The elements combine

The elements are not five separate dials — turning one moves the others.
Change the *relationship* (these two are sisters, not strangers) and the
*tension* of the same argument doubles. Change *time and place* (this goodbye
is happening at a hospital, not a bus stop) and every gesture gains weight.
When we rehearse, "make it better" usually means "change one element and see
what happens to the rest."

You will use this vocabulary constantly: naming the elements in your own work
for your [[Drama Journal]], and in others' work when we practise
[[Watching Like an Artist|watching like an artist]]. The words are also the
backbone of [[Writing About Drama]] — an opinion becomes analysis the moment
it names the element doing the work.
