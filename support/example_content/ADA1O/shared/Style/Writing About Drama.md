Half this course happens on your feet; the other half happens when you put
what you saw and made into words. Writing about drama has one rule that does
most of the work:

> [!important] Describe before you judge
> "It was good" ends a conversation. "The tallest performer stayed at the
> lowest level the whole scene, so the power felt upside down" — that starts
> one. Say what you **saw**, then what it **did**, then what you **make of
> it**.

## The vocabulary is the toolkit

Use the course's own words — that is what they are for, and using them
correctly is itself a [[C1.2|curriculum expectation]]. The difference is
concrete:

| Instead of… | Try… |
| --- | --- |
| "They stood in a cool way" | "The [[Tableau\|tableau]] used three levels, with focus on the seated figure" |
| "It got exciting" | "The [[Flashback and Flash-Forward\|flash-forward]] let us know more than the character did" |
| "She talked weird" | "She dropped her [[Voice\|pace]] and volume, which read as a status shift" |

## Sentence stems that unlock a stuck reflection

- The strongest **choice** I saw was… because…
- The moment the **[[Tension|tension]]** peaked was… because…
- If this group ran it again, one thing worth **trying** is…
- Playing that role changed how I think about… — which surprised me because…

## Writing about your own work

The same rules apply to your [[Drama Journal]], with one addition: name what
*you* did, not just what the group did. "We figured out the ending" hides the
learning; "I suggested we cut the last line, and the silence landed better"
shows it. That is the evidence [[How Marks Work]] talks about.
