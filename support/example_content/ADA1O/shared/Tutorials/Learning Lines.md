"Off book" means performing without the script in your hand — and, just as
importantly, without the script in your *eyes*. A performer who is truly off
book can put their attention where it belongs: on the other actor. Getting
there is not a matter of talent. It is a matter of method, and every method
below works better than reading the scene over and over, which barely works
at all.

## Four methods that work

| Method | How it goes |
| --- | --- |
| Chunking | Learn three or four lines cold before adding the next three |
| Cue-line practice | A partner reads only your cue lines; you supply yours |
| Walking the lines | Say the lines while walking; turn at every full stop |
| Recording | Record the scene with gaps where your lines go; fill them |

Walking works because your body remembers routes better than your eyes
remember pages. Recording works because the gaps travel with you — the
bus, the shower, the walk to school.

Cue-line practice matters most, because on stage nobody will ask you "what
is your next line?" — you will hear your **speech cue** and the line must
arrive on its own. That is the same cue language a stage manager uses to
call a show, and you will meet it again in the
[[Production Roles Interview]].

> [!warning] "I basically know it" is the danger zone
> Basically-known lines survive your bedroom and die in front of an
> audience, because performing takes attention and half-learned lines need
> all of it. The test is simple: can you say the scene while doing
> something else — walking, stacking chairs, holding a plank? If not, keep
> going.

## Off-book checklist

- [ ] I can say my lines from my cues alone, with no script visible
- [ ] I can do it while moving through our blocking, not just sitting still
- [ ] I know my entrance cues — what I hear or see before I come on
- [ ] I have been wrong somewhere, recovered, and kept the scene going
- [ ] A partner has tested me at least one full day before the deadline
