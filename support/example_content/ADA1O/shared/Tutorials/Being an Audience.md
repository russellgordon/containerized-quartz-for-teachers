Half of your time in this course is spent watching. That is not the rest
period between the real work — a performer can feel the difference
between a room that is with them and a room that is elsewhere, and so
can you, the moment you are the one standing up there.

## In this classroom

- **Settle before they start.** When a group takes their opening
  positions, the room goes quiet without being asked. Their first three
  seconds are the hardest of the piece.
- **Watch the whole thing.** No phones out, no side conversation, no
  rehearsing your own piece in your lap while somebody performs theirs.
- **Laugh when it is funny.** Silence is not respect; attention is. A
  comedy played to a room determined to be polite is an unkind
  experiment.
- **Do not rescue.** When somebody blanks, the whole room wants to help.
  Do not — no whispered lines, no laughing to break the tension. Let
  them find it. Almost always they do, and it belongs to them.
- **Applaud at the end**, every time, for everybody.
- **Then say something true.** [[Giving and Receiving Notes]] is the
  method: what worked, then one honest wish.

## In a theatre

Most of the above, plus the things a building adds:

- Arrive before the house lights go down; if you are late, you wait for
  the usher, not for a convenient moment of your own choosing.
- Phones off — not silent, off. The screen is visible from the stage,
  and so is your face lit up by it.
- No photographs or recording. It is distracting, and usually it is also
  not permitted.
- Food and drink by the house's rules, not yours.
- Applaud at the curtain call; stay for it. Leaving during the bows is
  the one thing performers reliably notice.

> [!tip] The unwritten rule that matters most
> Whatever you would want from an audience while you are exposed on a
> stage, give exactly that. Every performer in this room is going to be
> in that position within the fortnight, including you.

## When you are the one performing

Audience etiquette runs both ways. Be ready in the wings, not arriving
at your entrance. Do not comment on your own piece before it starts —
"this is going to be terrible" steals the room's attention and is
usually not even true. Take the applause, and thank the crew, whose
names appear in [[Production Roles Interview]] for a reason.
