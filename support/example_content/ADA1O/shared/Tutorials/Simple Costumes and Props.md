A full costume is a trap: it eats rehearsal time and never looks as real
as you hoped. **One strong piece** does the opposite — a scarf, a
clipboard, a hat — and the audience's imagination builds the rest.

The same goes for props: one object the character actually *uses* beats
a table of set dressing. If it changes what your body does — a cane
changes your walk, a heavy box your posture — it has earned its place.

## Choosing the one piece

What must the audience know in three seconds? Find the object that says it:

| The character is… | One piece that says it |
| --- | --- |
| In charge | A lanyard, a clipboard, a set of keys |
| From another time | A shawl, a flat cap, a long skirt |
| A worker mid-shift | An apron, work gloves, a tea towel |
| Fragile or ill | A blanket round the shoulders, a mug held two-handed |

## Sourcing, borrowing, and returning

Most pieces come from home, the drama cupboard, or a classmate. Borrowing
is where [[Our Ensemble Agreement]] gets practical:

- **Ask first**, even for cupboard items — another scene may need them.
- **Label it** with your name and group, and note what you borrowed.
- **Return it clean, the day the show closes** — a company that returns
  things gets lent things.

## Safety with props

- Nothing sharp, nothing breakable, nothing that could hurt — **no real
  or realistic weapons, ever**. Pure [[Mime|mime]] reads better anyway.
- Anything thrown, swung, or dropped must be soft, and rehearsed slowly
  before it is rehearsed at speed.
- Food and liquids on stage need my okay first — spills end scenes.

> [!danger] When in doubt, ask before you bring it
> If a prop needs a second thought, it needs a conversation with me first.
