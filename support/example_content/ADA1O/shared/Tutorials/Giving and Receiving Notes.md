In theatre, a **note** is a specific observation about a specific moment,
given so the work can improve: "the entrance lands late," "I lost the
last line — the volume dropped." Companies end every rehearsal with
notes; nobody takes them personally, because a note aims at the *work*.

## Giving a note

The rule is the same one from [[Writing About Drama]]: **describe before
you judge**. Say what you saw, then what it did to you as an audience
member — the habit of [[Watching Like an Artist]], out loud.

- Weak note: "The middle bit was kind of boring."
- Strong note: "In the middle tableau, everyone was at the same level, so
  my eye had nowhere to go."

The strong note is kinder *and* more useful: it names a moment and a
cause, so the group can act on it tomorrow. A judgement with no
description is just a mood you handed someone.

> [!tip] Notes name moments, not people
> "Jayden was flat" is a verdict on a person. "The reveal in the last
> scene came and went at the same energy as everything around it" is a
> note on a moment — and it might not even be Jayden's to fix. Aim every
> note at a place in the work.

## Receiving a note

Receiving is the harder half. The whole method is:

1. **Listen to the end.** Do not compose your defence while they talk.
2. **Say "thank you."** Not "yeah, but" — the note-giver watched carefully
   on your behalf, and thanks keeps them willing to do it again.
3. **Write it down.** A note you did not write down is a note you will not
   remember by next rehearsal.
4. **Decide later.** You are not obliged to take every note — only to
   consider it once the sting fades, usually about a day.

Explaining what you *meant* to do is the classic trap. The audience only
gets what reached them; if the note says it did not reach, the intention
does not matter yet. Fix the moment so it does.
