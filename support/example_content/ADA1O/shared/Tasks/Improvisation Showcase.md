## What you are making

You and a partner will perform a short improvised scene built from three
slips of paper drawn from three envelopes: a **location**, a
**relationship**, and an **objective** — something one character wants from
the other. You will not know your slips until moments before you begin.
There is no script and no rehearsal of the scene itself; what you rehearse,
for two weeks of in-class games and exercises, is the skill of building a
scene with another person in real time.

Performed cold does not mean unprepared. It means the preparation is in
you, not on paper.

## How to work

The whole showcase rests on one habit: **accept and build**. Whatever your
partner offers, you treat as true and add to it — "yes, and".

- If your partner says "Doctor, it's spreading," you are a doctor now, and
  it is spreading. Denying the offer ("I'm not a doctor") is called
  *blocking*, and it kills a scene faster than any bad idea can.
- Make your partner look brilliant. The strongest improvisers are not the
  funniest — they are the most generous. That generosity is the same trust
  we built in [[Why Trust Comes First]].
- Let the objective drive you. A character who wants something is
  interesting; two characters who want different things is a scene. That
  gap is [[Tension|tension]], and it is your engine.
- Use [[Voice]] and [[Movement and Gesture|movement]] to establish who and
  where you are within the first few seconds, so your partner has
  something solid to build on. [[Status]] is the fastest tool you have:
  who stands taller, who looks away first.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Accepting offers | Every offer is treated as true and extended — no blocking |
| Building | Each exchange adds new information; the scene moves somewhere |
| Character | Voice and body make who-you-are readable without announcing it |
| Objective | We can tell what your character wants without being told |
| Partnership | Both players share focus; neither drags nor dominates |

> [!success]- If you go blank (click to expand)
> Going blank is normal, and there is a reliable exit: stop inventing and
> react instead. Look at your partner, name what you actually see — "you're
> shaking" — and the scene restarts itself. The audience never knows the
> difference between a plan and a recovery.
