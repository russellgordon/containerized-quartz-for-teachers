**Mime** is communicating without words: an action, an object, a place, a
feeling — carried entirely by the body. Take away speech and two things
happen at once. Every gesture you make suddenly matters more, and the
audience starts working *with* you, filling the silence with meaning. Mime
is less about pretending there is a wall[^1] and more about learning what
your hands, weight, and face can say when your voice is not allowed to help.

## How to run it

1. Choose a simple, physical task: packing a suitcase, wrapping a fragile
   gift, making tea for someone you are angry with.
2. Do it once for real, or with a real object, and notice everything —
   where the weight sits, which fingers grip, where your eyes go.
3. Now remove the object and keep the truth: the suitcase still has a lid
   that swings, the kettle is still hot, the tape still sticks to the wrong
   finger.
4. Keep the world consistent. The table stays at the same height; the cup
   stays where you put it down. One object that drifts breaks the spell for
   everything else.
5. Show it in silence. No mouthing words — if the task needs a sentence to
   be understood, the gestures are not finished yet.

## Economy and precision

The beginner's instinct is to add more movement; the skill is subtraction.
One clear gesture reads better than five approximate ones, because the
audience can only complete what it can actually see. This is
[[Movement and Gesture]] under laboratory conditions: with nothing else on
stage, the difference between a precise reach and a vague one is the whole
performance. Precision is also what makes [[Focus and Emphasis|focus]]
possible — an audience follows your eyes and hands only if your eyes and
hands commit.

Silence changes the audience's job, too. With no dialogue to carry meaning,
they lean in, read intention, and predict — which is why a mimed scene often
generates more [[Tension|tension]] than a spoken one. Try running the same
short scene twice, once with words and once without, and discuss what the
silence did to your understanding of it. That comparison is a conversation
about [[The Elements of Drama|the elements of drama]] themselves.

> [!warning] The pitfall: rushing
> Nerves speed you up, and speed blurs gesture into mush. Mime at the pace
> of the real task — slower, if anything. Stillness between gestures is not
> dead time; it is where the audience catches up. You trained that
> stillness in [[Tableau]]. Use it.

[^1]: The white-face, striped-shirt tradition — invisible walls, ropes,
    wind — comes largely from twentieth-century French mime, most famously
    Marcel Marceau. It is one style of mime, not the definition of it.
