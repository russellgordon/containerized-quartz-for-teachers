**Choral speaking** is a group performing a text with one shared voice — and
then breaking that voice apart on purpose. Thirty people in unison sound
like a wall; one voice answering them sounds suddenly, startlingly alone.
The contrast between the many and the one is the whole instrument.

The convention is ancient — the chorus of Greek tragedy commented on the
action as a single body — and completely current: chants in the stands at a
game are choral speaking, as [[Drama in Everyday Life]] points out.

## The four tools

- **Unison** — everyone together. Power, authority, inevitability.
- **Echo** — a word or phrase repeated after the main voice, fading or
  growing. Doubt, memory, mockery.
- **Repetition** — the same line returning through the piece, gathering
  weight each time it comes back.
- **Call and response** — one voice throws, many voices answer. The oldest
  structure there is, and still the most reliable.

## One against many

Almost any text gets interesting the moment you ask: *which line belongs to
everyone, and which line belongs to one person?* The group as a crowd, a
jury, a family, a chorus of doubts — set against a single speaker. That
lone voice inherits enormous [[Focus and Emphasis|focus]] without moving a
muscle, and choosing *which* words to give it is an interpretation of the
text all by itself.

## Rehearsing it

Choral work is the one convention that cannot be improvised — it is built in
repetition. Work through this list in order:

- [ ] Choose a short text — a poem, a chant, a set of headlines.
- [ ] Read it aloud around the circle once, just to hear it.
- [ ] Mark the script: unison lines, small-group lines, solo lines.
- [ ] Agree on the breath — where the whole group breathes together decides
      where the phrases are.
- [ ] Run it at half volume and half speed until the unison is truly unison.
- [ ] Add dynamics last: one whispered line, one at full voice.
- [ ] Run it three times the same way. If it survives that, it is rehearsed.

The half-volume run matters most: unison exposes every drifting pace and
swallowed consonant, and you can only fix what you can hear. This is
[[Voice]] work disguised as a group task — articulation, pace, volume, and
tone all have to be *agreed on*, not just performed.

## Where it goes wrong

The classic failure is the drone: the group finds one shared pitch and speed
and flattens every line into it. Unison does not mean lifeless — a chorus
can be furious, gleeful, or afraid together. Decide the feeling of each line
the way you would decide it for a single [[Role and Character|character]],
then do it as one.
