A **flashback** interrupts a scene to show something that already happened;
a **flash-forward** jumps ahead to show something that has not happened yet.
Both are ways of refusing to tell a story in the order it occurred — and
that refusal is a tool, because the *order* in which an audience learns
things changes what every moment means.

Played straight, a farewell at a train station is sad. Preceded by a
flash-forward showing one character's empty chair a year later, the same
farewell becomes unbearable — and not one line of it changed.

## How to run it

1. Build your scene in plain chronological order first, so everyone knows
   what actually happens.
2. Choose the moment your audience most needs to *reinterpret* — a promise,
   a lie, a goodbye.
3. Decide what the jump reveals: a cause (flashback) or a consequence
   (flash-forward).
4. Mark the jump clearly. Freeze the present scene as a [[Tableau|tableau]],
   step out of it, and let the inserted scene play; then reset and release
   the freeze. Audiences forgive almost anything except not knowing *when*
   they are.
5. Run it both ways — jump early in the scene, then late — and ask the
   audience which placement hit harder.

## Why it works: the audience knows more than the character

> [!important] Dramatic irony
> When the audience knows something a character does not — because a
> flash-forward already showed them the ending — every confident line that
> character speaks becomes painful, funny, or both. The audience is no
> longer watching to find out what happens. They are watching the character
> walk toward it.

That gap in knowledge is one of the most reliable generators of
[[Tension|tension]] there is, and it costs nothing to stage: no set, no
lights, just a reordering. When you can name the effect and explain how the
placement of a jump produced it, you are doing exactly the kind of thinking
[[Watching Like an Artist]] asks for.

> [!warning] The pitfall: the unmarked jump
> If the audience cannot tell a flashback from the present, they do not
> experience irony — they experience confusion, and confusion reads as a
> mistake. A freeze, a spoken date, a repeated gesture: pick one signal and
> keep it consistent every single time.

Time games reward planning, which makes this a natural convention to bring
into [[Scene Study from a Story]] — most stories hide their best scene
somewhere other than the beginning.
