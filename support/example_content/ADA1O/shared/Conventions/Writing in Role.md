**Writing in role** means writing *as* a character rather than about one:
the diary entry they would keep, the letter they would send — or never
send — the message thread sitting on their phone. It is [[A1.3|role play]]
with the speed turned down. No audience, no partner waiting on your next
line, no pressure to be interesting in real time. Just you, the character,
and the page.

That privacy is the point. Some discoveries about a character will not
happen in front of thirty people, and this convention gives them somewhere
to happen.

## How to run it

1. Fix the moment. Not "write as Goldilocks" but "write as Goldilocks the
   night after she ran — she cannot sleep, so she writes."
2. Choose a form the character would really use: diary, letter, apology
   never sent, voicemail transcript, note left on a kitchen table.
3. Write in first person, in the character's own vocabulary, for a set
   time — eight or ten minutes, no stopping to fix things.
4. Keep the world's details in it: names, objects, weather, what was said.
   Specifics are what make it feel found rather than invented.

> [!example]- Starters that work with almost any story (click to expand)
> - "I keep thinking about what I should have said when…"
> - "Nobody knows this yet, but…"
> - "Dear ___, by the time you read this…"
> - "The third time it happened, I stopped pretending it was an accident."
>
> Steal one, write the first sentence, and the character usually takes over.

## What it feeds

Everything. A diary entry written in role hands you the inner life you need
for [[Role and Character]] work; it gives [[Hot Seating]] an actor with real
answers instead of improvised guesses; and it is the fastest way to find the
missing scene in [[Scene Study from a Story]] — if your character wrote a
letter between chapter two and chapter three, what does it say?

It also feeds your [[Drama Journal]], where writing in role sits beside its
opposite number: reflection in your own voice. Keep the two straight — one
is the character's truth, the other is yours, and [[Writing About Drama]]
shows what the second kind looks like when it is done well.

> [!warning] The pitfall: the book report in disguise
> "Then I went to the ball and met the prince and it was magical" is a plot
> summary wearing a costume. A character writing at night does not recap
> their day — they circle the one thing they cannot put down. If every
> event gets equal space, you are writing *about* the character again.
