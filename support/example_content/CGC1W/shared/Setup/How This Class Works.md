Geography is not a subject you can learn entirely indoors. This course
runs on questions about real places — starting with the ones you can
walk to.

## A normal period

1. **Something to look at or do** — a map, a photograph, a dataset, a
   walk. Before it is explained.
2. **Working it out**, usually in threes, usually with a disagreement.
3. **Naming it**, once you have already met the idea.
4. **Where it goes next** — the task, the fieldwork, the portfolio.

## Days that are not normal

We go outside. The shoreline, the woodlot, a street in this
neighbourhood, and the vegetable farm five minutes away are all
teaching spaces in this course, and the class page tells you a week
ahead when we are using them. [[Fieldwork Safety and Ethics]] is the
agreement that makes those days possible; read it before the first one.

## What the course asks of you

- **Notice things.** The best inquiry questions in this course come from
  something a student saw on the way to school.
- **Write down what you observed**, separately from what you concluded.
  That distinction is most of geography.
- **Argue with evidence.** Every seminar in this course has more than
  one defensible answer, and "how do you know?" is the standard question.
- **Handle other people's places carefully.** We study communities where
  people live, including your own.

## The shape of the year

Four units — the land itself, what we take and make, who lives here, and
what makes a community liveable — and then [[The Local Inquiry]], where
you investigate a question about this place and present it to someone who
actually works on it. The course ends with a three-hour examination built
on maps and data you have not seen before.
