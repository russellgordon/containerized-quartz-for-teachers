A claim without a source is an opinion. In geography the source usually
has a date, a place, and a maker — record all three.

## What to write down, as you go

| Source | The citation looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Census data | Statistics Canada, Census Profile, 2021 Census, [place name], accessed 12 October 2026. |
| Climate data | Environment and Climate Change Canada, Canadian Climate Normals 1991–2020, [station], accessed 12 October 2026. |
| A government map or dataset | Government of Ontario, [dataset name], Ontario GeoHub, accessed 12 October 2026. |
| A topographic map | Natural Resources Canada, [sheet name and number], scale, year. |
| A news article | Author, "Headline", *Publication*, 3 March 2026. |
| An interview | Interview with [role, not name unless permitted], 14 October 2026. |
| Your own fieldwork | Field observation, [location], 14 October 2026, recorded in field notebook. |

That last row matters: your own observation is a legitimate source in
this course, and it needs a date and a place like any other.

## Judging before you cite

Ask who made it and why — see [[Judging a Source]]. A municipality's
own report about a development it approved is evidence of the
municipality's position, which is not the same as evidence about the
development.

## Maps and images

A map you did not make is cited like any other source, and a map you
*did* make says so, with its data source underneath: "Made with
Statistics Canada 2021 Census data".

> [!warning] Two things students lose marks for every year
> A screenshot with no source, and a figure with no year. Both are
> avoidable in ten seconds at the moment you find them, and unfixable
> the night before.

## When you cannot verify something

Say so, in the work: "I could not find current data for X; the most
recent I found was 2016." Naming the limit of your evidence is a
strength here — it is what separates a report from a guess.
