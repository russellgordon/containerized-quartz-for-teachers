Electricity in Canada is provincial, and each province's mix was settled
decades ago by what its geography made cheap. Quebec, British Columbia,
Manitoba and Newfoundland and Labrador had big rivers; Alberta and
Saskatchewan had fossil fuels underfoot; Ontario built nuclear. Nobody
alive chose that inheritance, and everybody alive is arguing about what to
add to it next.

## The situation

In 2025 Ontario's generation was 48 per cent nuclear and 23 per cent water
power, with natural gas and oil near 19 per cent and wind making up most of
the remainder. Those proportions move, and the Independent Electricity
System Operator publishes current figures — look them up rather than
quoting this paragraph, and put the year beside whatever you find. The mix
matters because it decides what a new decision costs. Every source has a
bill; they differ in who receives it and when.

## What each choice actually costs

**Nuclear** produces no combustion emissions and produces used fuel that
stays dangerous longer than any institution has existed. Canada holds
roughly 3.4 million used fuel bundles in above-ground storage at reactor
sites, heading toward about 5.9 million. In November 2024 the Nuclear
Waste Management Organization selected a northwestern Ontario site for a
deep geological repository, after the Township of Ignace voted in favour
and Wabigoon Lake Ojibway Nation confirmed its willingness — the latter
also running its own review against Anishinaabe law alongside the federal
process. Eagle Lake First Nation filed for judicial review in December
2024, arguing the site sits on its traditional territory and it was
wrongly excluded as a host community; that case is unresolved. Grand
Council Treaty #3 holds a standing position against a repository anywhere
in Treaty #3 territory. The dispute is not mainly about geology — it is
about **whose consent counts** when risk travels along watersheds and
transport routes.

**Hydroelectricity** has no fuel cost and a very long life, and it floods
a valley. BC Hydro's Site C dam on the Peace River reached full operation
in August 2025 — over 1,100 megawatts, roughly 500,000 homes. The federal
review panel had found significant adverse effects on Treaty 8 fishing,
hunting and trapping rights that could not be mitigated; West Moberly,
Prophet River and Doig River First Nations challenged the project, and it
was built anyway.

**Natural gas** is quick to build and available on demand, and it emits.
**Wind and solar** have no fuel and no operating emissions, produce on the
weather's schedule rather than yours, need land, and are fought over
locally. **Diesel** still runs roughly two hundred remote communities
until a line reaches them — as the 1,800-km Wataynikaneyap line did for
seventeen communities in northwestern Ontario in 2024, majority-owned by
the First Nations it serves.

## Questions

1. Ontario's mix was set by geography and timing. Does that make its
   emissions record a better or worse basis for judging the province?
2. The used fuel already exists and has to go somewhere. Does that
   strengthen or weaken the case for the proposed repository?
3. Site C was built after a federal panel found unmitigable effects on
   treaty rights. What can an environmental assessment actually decide?
4. Wind and solar are opposed locally and supported nationally. Whose
   preference should win, and on what principle?
5. If conservation is the cheapest supply, why is it usually the smallest
   line in an energy plan?
