A house is where population change becomes visible. Everything else about
demographics — age structure, migration, household formation — arrives in
a community as a question about what gets built, where, and for whom.

## The situation

For two decades the planning assumption in southern Ontario was steady,
rapid growth, and housing policy was written against it. That assumption
has recently stopped holding. Statistics Canada's estimates put Canada's
41 census metropolitan areas at 31,169,100 people on 1 July 2025, growing
**1.0 per cent** over the year — down from 3.5 per cent — with the Toronto
census metropolitan area recording essentially no change at all, 992
people in the downward direction. The national quarterly estimate for 1
April 2026 was 41,417,056, a **decrease** of 55,025 over the quarter.

> [!warning] Check which instrument produced your growth number
> Those figures are **estimates**, not census counts. The 2021 census is
> still the most recent published count; 2026 counts arrive 10 February
> 2027. Estimates answer "what changed last quarter" and do not belong in
> a table beside a census figure. Whichever you use, write which one it
> was and the date it refers to — [[Working With Census Data]].

Slower growth has not made housing cheap. Cost is set by what exists, what
is legal to build, and what somebody will finance, and none of those three
responds quickly to a change in arrivals.

## Who actually decides

**A municipality** writes the official plan and zoning by-law, which
together decide what may legally be built on each parcel. In most Canadian
cities the majority of residential land permits only detached houses, so
the apartment argument is confined to a few arterial roads before it
begins. **A province** sets the frame the municipality works inside and
can override it, and in Ontario has repeatedly done so.

**A developer** decides whether to build what is permitted. Approval is
permission, not a house — if costs or interest rates make a project
unprofitable, approved units stay unbuilt, which is why "units approved"
and "units completed" are different numbers with different politics.
**Non-market builders** — public, non-profit, co-operative and Indigenous
housing providers — build for households the market will not serve at a
price they can pay, and how much they build is a public spending decision.

**Existing residents** attend the public meeting. The people who would
live in the new building do not, because they do not live there yet. That
asymmetry is structural, and it is the sharpest thing to notice about how
land-use decisions get made.

## Positions that genuinely disagree

Build more, anywhere, and price falls. Build up rather than out and
protect the farmland — accepting the neighbourhood fight. Build outward
where land is cheaper — accepting the servicing costs and the lost soil,
[[Sprawl and Its Alternatives]]. Or hold that supply is not the binding
constraint at the bottom of the market at all, and only non-market housing
reaches the households in deepest trouble.

## Questions

1. Growth slowed sharply and housing stayed expensive. How much of the
   cost problem was ever about arrivals?
2. If most residential land permits only detached houses, who was that
   rule written to protect, and from what?
3. Approved units and completed units diverge. Which number should a
   government be judged on?
4. The people most affected by a new building are absent when it is
   decided. How would you fix that without removing local input?
5. Which position above would help the lowest-income household in your
   community first, and how do you know?
