The Toronto census metropolitan area held 6,202,225 people at the 2021
census, and essentially none of them grow their own food. Something feeds
them every day, it has never stopped, and almost nobody can describe how.
That gap between total dependence and total ignorance is the issue.

## The situation

Food reaches a Canadian city along a chain of specialists, and the chain
is efficient precisely because each link does only one thing. Efficient
systems are cheap, and brittle in a specific way: a failure at any link
cannot be absorbed by the others.

| Link | What it depends on | What breaks it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The farm | Soil class, growing season, water, seasonal labour | Land converted to housing; drought or a wet spring; workers unable to arrive |
| Processing and packing | A small number of large plants near production | One plant closing removes capacity for a whole region |
| Distribution centres | A few large warehouses, computerized ordering | Power, software, or a strike at a single site |
| Trucking and rail | Diesel, highways, drivers, refrigeration | Closure of one corridor; fuel price; winter |
| Retail | A handful of national chains setting price and assortment | What is not stocked cannot be bought |
| The household | Income, transport to a store, storage, time | Price rises faster than wages |

Southern Ontario supplies field vegetables, greenhouse produce, dairy and
meat in season, on some of the best soil in Canada — soil the region is
also building houses on, with Ontario's farmland falling from about 12.35
million acres in 2016 to 11.77 million in 2021. From roughly November to
May a large share of the fresh produce in a Toronto store is imported, so
the city's winter diet depends on other countries' weather, other
countries' water, and an open border.

## The comparison that shows what the system is worth

In 2025, 24.0 per cent of people in Canada lived in a food-insecure
household. In Nunavut the figure was 56.4 per cent — not because Canada
lacks food, but because everything in a store there arrives by air or on
one annual sealift, incomes do not match those prices, and access to
country food has been squeezed. The southern city and the northern fly-in
community sit at opposite ends of one system: the first has cheap calories
and a distribution problem at the household level, the second a distance
problem before the household is reached — [[Comparing Communities]].

## Positions that genuinely disagree

Growers argue farmland protection is meaningless without a price they can
farm at. Retailers argue consolidated buying is what keeps food affordable
at all. Municipalities weigh farmland against a housing shortage they are
held responsible for. Food-security organizations argue the binding
constraint is income rather than supply, and that local-food programs
address a different problem than the one people actually have. Each is
right about something.

## Questions

1. Trace one item you ate yesterday back through the table. Which link do
   you know least about?
2. Is "buy local" a food-security policy, an environmental policy, or an
   agricultural-income policy? Defend one answer.
3. If the binding constraint is income rather than supply, what would a
   serious response look like, and who would pay for it?
4. Farmland near a city is worth more as housing. What would have to
   change for that to stop being true?
5. What would a week without the highway corridor do to this city, and
   which households would feel it first?
