A shoreline is not a line. It is the current position of a process, and it
was somewhere else a century ago. Every argument on a Canadian waterfront
starts from the same awkward fact: something was built at a location that
was never going to stay put.

## The situation

Waves do the work, and their energy comes from **fetch** — the distance of
open water the wind crosses before arriving. Material travels along the
shore rather than straight out, so what erodes from one property is what
builds the beach two kilometres downdrift.

On the Great Lakes there are no tides, but water levels swing through
multi-year highs and lows, and the damaging combination is a high-level
year, a storm, and a shore with no ice on it. An ice foot absorbs winter
waves; a winter without one leaves the bank exposed to the season's
largest storms. Shoreline damage is therefore a pattern over decades
rather than an event, and one bad autumn is evidence of very little —
[[Hazard, Exposure, Risk]]. You can check the pattern yourself: compare
historical and current aerial imagery for one stretch, read the wind and
storm record from Environment and Climate Change Canada's [historical data
search](https://climate.weather.gc.ca/historical_data/search_historic_data_e.html)
(accessed August 2026), and find the federal water-level record for the
lake before arguing from any single year.

## Three choices, and what each really costs

**Armour it.** Seawalls, revetments and groynes hold one property in
place. A vertical wall reflects wave energy downward and scours the beach
in front of it; a groyne traps sediment updrift and starves the shore
downdrift. The engineering works — by moving the problem to a neighbour
who did not choose it.

**Retreat.** Setbacks for new building, relocation or buyout for what
exists. The only option that stops paying for itself forever, and the
hardest politically, because it asks somebody to give up a house standing
today against a risk arriving later.

**Do nothing.** Cheapest today, and not the same as doing nothing wrong.

> [!note]- What "do nothing" actually means (click to expand)
> On an undeveloped shore it is the ecologically correct answer: the bank
> feeds sediment to the beaches downdrift, and beach, dune and wetland
> migrate inland as they always have. Armouring there starves the whole
> system to protect nothing.
>
> On a developed shore it means somebody eventually loses a building, and
> the question becomes who pays — the owner, the insurer, or the public
> through disaster assistance. That is a decision about cost-sharing
> dressed as a decision about engineering.

## Positions that genuinely disagree

The waterfront owner wants the bank held now. The downdrift neighbour
wants the sediment armouring intercepts. The municipality carries the
road, the sewer and the liability, and must decide whether to keep
maintaining infrastructure it knows will be undermined. The conservation
authority maps the hazard and restricts building in it, lowering the value
of somebody's land. Everyone paying taxes or premiums funds the outcome
without being asked about the location.

## Questions

1. Your neighbour armours their bank and your beach shrinks. What claim do
   you have, and against whom?
2. A municipality knows a shoreline road will be undermined within thirty
   years. What should it do this year, and what would that cost a resident
   today?
3. Is a setback rule a restriction on property or a protection of it?
4. Who should pay when a house built legally in 1960 is lost in 2035?
5. What evidence would convince you a shoreline is retreating rather than
   simply having a bad year?

Gather evidence in [[The Shoreline Study]], write it up in
[[The Risk Report]], and take the cost question to
[[Who Should Pay for the Damage|Who Should Pay for the Damage?]].
