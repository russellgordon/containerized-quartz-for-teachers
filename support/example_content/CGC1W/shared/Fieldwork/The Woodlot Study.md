A woodlot is a physical system and a resource at the same time, which is
why we visit it twice with two different questions.

## First visit — the physical system

1. **The ground.** Soil depth to rock or compaction, wetness, slope, and
   which way it faces.
2. **The canopy.** Rough proportion of cover, dominant species, and
   whether the trees are all the same age.
3. **Water.** Where it collects, where it runs, and what that tells you
   about the ground beneath.
4. **The edges.** What is on the other side of each boundary, and what
   the edge itself looks like.

The question: **what does this piece of ground allow, and what does it
prevent?**

## Second visit — the working forest

The same woodlot, in Unit 2, as a resource:

- What could be harvested here, at what rate, without ending it?
- What would harvesting need — access, equipment, a market, a licence?
- What else does this stand do that is not a product: water, shade,
  habitat, carbon, the path people walk on Sundays?
- Who would decide, and who would be affected?

> [!success]- The question to sit with (click to expand)
> A woodlot managed for timber, a woodlot left alone, and a woodlot
> turned into houses are three different futures, and only one of them
> is reversible. Which one, and does that matter to your answer?

## What to record

The same two-column notebook discipline as the shoreline: what you
observed, and separately what you concluded. Sketch a plan view with a
rough scale, mark where you took each observation, and note the date and
the weather — a wet October and a dry one are different woodlots.
