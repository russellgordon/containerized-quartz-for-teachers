Five minutes' drive, and a working vegetable farm — an industry you can
stand inside. We go twice, with different questions, and both times we
are guests in somebody's workplace.

## Before we go

Read [[Fieldwork Safety and Ethics]], and write **three questions** you
genuinely want answered. Good ones are specific: how many hectares are
in vegetables, where does the water come from, what is the hardest week
of the year, who buys most of this.

Poor ones are answerable from a website, and you will waste your one
chance to ask a person who knows.

## First visit — the farm as an industry

| Look for | The question underneath |
| --- | --- |
| Land | How much, what kind of soil, owned or rented? |
| Water | Where from, how much, what happens in a dry August? |
| Inputs | Seed, fertiliser, fuel, equipment — bought where? |
| Labour | How many people, when, and where do they come from? |
| Markets | Who buys it, how far does it travel, who sets the price? |
| Risk | Weather, price, pests, a broken machine in the wrong week |

## Second visit — the food system

In Unit 4 we come back and ask a different question: this farm is one
point in a system that ends with dinner. What happens between here and a
plate, and what would change if this farm stopped?

## Recording

Notebook, not memory. Write the numbers as they are said, and read them
back — "so that is 40 hectares, of which 12 in vegetables?" A number you
half-remember is worse than no number.

> [!warning] Write down what you were told, and mark it as told
> "The grower said the well has never run dry" is evidence about what
> the grower said. That is different from evidence about the well, and
> in your report the difference matters.

Afterwards, everyone writes a thank-you. That is not a courtesy in this
course; it is why we are still welcome.
