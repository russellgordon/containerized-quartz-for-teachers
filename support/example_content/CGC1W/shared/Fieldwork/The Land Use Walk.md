Land use is a category geographers invented to describe what people
actually do with ground. The only way to learn it is to survey some.

## The survey

Working in pairs, one block at a time, record for each parcel:

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Use | Residential, commercial, industrial, institutional, open space, transport, vacant |
| Density | Detached, semi, row, low-rise, mid-rise, or none |
| Age | Rough era of construction — you can learn to read this |
| Condition | Maintained, worn, vacant, under construction |
| Access | Sidewalk, crossing, bus stop, bike lane, parking |
| Something surprising | One line. There always is one |

Sketch a base map first, then fill it in. Do not photograph the block and
classify it later — half of what matters is not visible in a photograph.

## The population edition

In Unit 3 we walk with a different question: does the census match the
street? You will have pulled the profile for this area first. Look for
what the numbers said — household size, dwelling type, age structure —
and see whether the street agrees.

Where they disagree, the reason is usually a boundary, a change since
the count, or something the census does not ask about.

## The Unit 4 edition

The same walk again, feeding [[The Land Use Proposal]]: this time you are
looking for a site, and for what its neighbours would say about it.

> [!tip] The vacant lot is the interesting one
> Every empty parcel in a built-up area is empty for a reason — cost,
> contamination, a dispute, a plan, or somebody waiting. Finding out
> which is a whole inquiry, and it is usually a good one.
