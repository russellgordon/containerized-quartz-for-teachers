Geographic writing has a job: make a reader understand a place they have
not stood in, and believe your account of it.

## The four habits

**1. Say where.** Every claim in geography has a location, and vague
location is the most common weakness in student work. Not "in the north"
— name it, and give the reader a sense of how far and in which direction.

**2. Separate observation from inference.** "The bank is undercut and
tree roots are exposed" is an observation. "The river is eroding this
bank" is an inference, and a good one — but it is a different kind of
statement, and mixing them is how a field report becomes unreliable.

**3. Use the numbers, with their units and their year.** A figure
without a date is not evidence in a course about change.

**4. Explain the pattern.** Describing a distribution earns a little;
saying what produced it earns the rest.

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| "Population is going up" | "The census counted X in 2016 and Y in 2021 — a rise of Z per cent" |
| "It's near the water" | "About 400 m from the shoreline, on the sheltered side" |
| "The soil is bad" | "Thin soil over bedrock, with standing water in the low corner" |
| "This is unsustainable" | Name what runs out, for whom, and how soon |

## Words that carry weight

*Watershed*, *density*, *tenure*, *hazard*, *risk*, *sprawl*,
*sustainability*. Each means one thing here; use them precisely or use
plain words instead.

> [!tip] Write the map caption first
> If you can write one sentence saying what your map shows and why it
> matters, the paragraph beneath it almost writes itself. If you cannot,
> the map is not finished — see [[Making a Thematic Map]].
