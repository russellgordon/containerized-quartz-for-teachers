**Launched mid-Unit 3, due at the end of it.** Pairs.

Your own community, read through its census data, and compared with one
other community that is genuinely different.

## What you produce

**Three maps or graphs made from real census data**, each answering a
question you asked first. Age structure, language, household size,
income, mobility, dwelling type — choose what your question needs.

**A written profile** of about 600 words covering:

1. The demographic shape of your community — what the numbers actually
   say, not what you expected.
2. How it compares with your chosen community, and one reason for the
   difference that comes from geography rather than guesswork.
3. Migration: who has been arriving, who has been leaving, over what
   period.
4. Where people live within the community, and what put them there —
   physical geography, transport, land cost, or history.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Data handling | The right table found, the right variable chosen, the year stated |
| Graphs and maps | Correctly built and correctly read — see [[Working With Census Data]] |
| Interpretation | You explain a pattern rather than describing it |
| Comparison | Two places genuinely compared, not two profiles side by side |

> [!warning] Census geography is not the same as a neighbourhood
> A census tract has a boundary drawn for counting, not for living in.
> When your map says something surprising, check whether the boundary
> caused it before you build an explanation on it.

[[Reading a Population]] and [[Settlement Patterns]] are the ideas;
the neighbourhood walk in this unit is your ground-truth — the census
says one thing, and the street will tell you whether you believed the
right thing.
