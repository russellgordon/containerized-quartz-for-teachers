**Due: the last week of Unit 2.** Individual. A visual piece plus about
400 words.

One ordinary object you own, traced from raw material to disposal.

## Choose something small and specific

A phone, a pair of jeans, a hockey stick, a can of tomatoes, a bicycle
tire, a laptop charger. Something you can hold. The more ordinary it is,
the better this works.

## What you produce

**A journey map or diagram** — not a poster of pictures, a map that
shows movement: where the materials came from, where it was made, how it
reached you, and where it goes when you are done with it. Distances
matter, so put them on.

**The writing** answers four questions:

1. Which stage of this journey has the largest environmental effect, and
   how do you know?
2. What in the journey surprised you?
3. What would have to change for the journey to be shorter — and what
   would be lost if it were?
4. Whose work is in this object, and under what conditions?

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Research | Real supply-chain evidence, cited, not a guess drawn as a diagram |
| The visual | Readable, accurate, and it carries the argument |
| Communication | Clear for an audience who has not studied this — see [[Citing Geographic Sources]] |
| Honesty | You say which parts of the journey you could not find out |

> [!tip] The gaps are part of the finding
> Most supply chains are hard to trace past the second step, and that
> difficulty is itself worth writing about: a company that cannot tell
> you where its cotton was grown has told you something.

[[The Life of a Product]] sets this up, and [[Moving People and Goods]]
is where the transport half of your map comes from.
