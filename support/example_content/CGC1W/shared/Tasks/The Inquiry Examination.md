**The final evaluation. Three hours, in the examination period.**
Individual.

Unseen maps, unseen data, and questions that reach across the whole
course.

## What it looks like

You are given a small dossier about a place you have not studied: a
topographic extract, a thematic map, a table of census or climate data,
and two short written sources that disagree with each other.

You answer in three parts:

1. **Read the evidence** (about 45 minutes). What do the map and the
   data actually show? Describe patterns and trends precisely, with
   figures, and say what the sources leave out.
2. **Explain it** (about 75 minutes). Why is this place like this? Use
   physical geography, resources and industry, population, and land use
   as the evidence requires — a strong answer crosses strands, because
   real places do.
3. **Judge it** (about 45 minutes). A decision is proposed for this
   place. Assess it, from more than one perspective, and recommend.

The remaining time is for reading. Use it — the strongest papers spend
twenty minutes before writing anything.

## What is being examined

| Criterion | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| Reading evidence | Map and data read accurately, including scale and units |
| Credibility | You notice what a source is for, and who made it |
| Explanation | Physical and human geography connected, not listed |
| Judgement | A recommendation that names its cost and its uncertainty |
| Communication | Structured and plain under time pressure |

## Preparing for it

The three review periods are built for this: unseen dossiers worked
against the clock, then compared. Bring your fieldwork notes — the
skills you built at [[The Shoreline Study]] and on [[The Land Use Walk]]
are exactly what part 1 asks for, and [[Judging a Source]] is the whole
of the credibility mark.

> [!tip] Answer the question that was asked
> Every year, the most common lost mark is a good essay about the wrong
> thing. Underline the verb — *describe*, *explain*, *assess* — and give
> it what it asked for.
