"Which treaty covers this place?" is an answerable question, and the tools
that answer it are free, public, and unusually clear about their own
limits. Reading those limits carefully is most of the lesson.

## What a treaty is

A treaty is an agreement between the Crown and one or more Indigenous
nations. It is not a document that finished its work in 1850 — it sets out
obligations both parties still hold, and courts still interpret them.
Section 35 of the *Constitution Act, 1982* recognizes and affirms existing
Aboriginal and treaty rights. The parties did not always understand a
treaty the same way at signing, and those differences are part of what a
treaty *is* rather than a footnote to it. Some land is covered by a
historical treaty, some by a modern agreement, and some by neither.

## The anchor: Ontario's map

Ontario publishes an interactive map of treaties and reserves at
[ontario.ca/page/map-ontario-treaties-and-reserves](https://www.ontario.ca/page/map-ontario-treaties-and-reserves)
(updated April 2024; accessed August 2026). Search an address, then click
inside a shaded area to see the name and date of the treaty there.

**More than 40 treaties and other land agreements** cover Ontario, from
the Niagara Purchase of 1781 through the Williams Treaties of 1923, plus
adhesions and the proposed Algonquins of Ontario settlement. The
province's [treaties hub](https://www.ontario.ca/page/treaties) carries the
background, and its [teaching and learning
resources](https://www.ontario.ca/page/teaching-and-learning-resources)
page includes lesson kits written for Grade 9 students on **Treaty No. 9**
and the resource *Gdoo-Sastamoo Kii Mi — This is our Understanding*.

> [!warning] What Ontario says about its own map
> The province states the map "was designed for illustrative purposes,"
> that "Treaties and the treaty relationship are more than what is
> recorded in historical maps," and that the map and its materials "are
> not intended to be a complete source of treaty history and do not
> replace engagement with local Indigenous peoples."
>
> It adds that the map "does not purport to reflect actual treaty
> boundaries" and "should not be relied on to assess legal rights."
>
> So it answers *which treaty is associated with this location*, for
> teaching. It does not answer *where exactly the boundary runs*, and was
> never meant to.

## A second view, held to the same standard

Native Land Digital, an Indigenous-led not-for-profit, maps territories,
languages and treaties at [native-land.ca](https://native-land.ca/)
(accessed August 2026). Quote its own words: the map "does not represent
or intend to represent official or legal boundaries of any Indigenous
nations. To learn about definitive boundaries, contact the nations in
question." It states plainly that its maps "are not official sources" and
that the map "is not a legal document."

Comparing its territory layer with Ontario's treaty layer for the same
point is worth doing, because the two often do not line up — and why they
do not is the substance of the topic, not an error by either.

## For a nation's position, go to the nation

No map states what a nation currently wants. That comes from the nation's
own statements, council releases and court filings — dated, read in full,
attributed. Different nations take different positions on the same
project, and one nation's position can be more layered than a headline
allows.

[[The Resource Investigation]]; the concepts are in
[[Land, Treaty, and Title]].
