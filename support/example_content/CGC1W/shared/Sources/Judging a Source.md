Everything you find was made by somebody, for a reason, at a moment, with
money from somewhere. Four questions — **who, why, when, who paid** — sort
almost anything, and none of them requires you to already know the
geography.

None of the four tests truth. A company release can be entirely accurate;
a government page can be badly out of date. What the questions tell you is
what a source is *for*, and therefore what it is likely to leave out.

## Four questions on four real kinds of thing

| The artefact | Who and why | What it will not tell you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A mining company's project page | The proponent, describing its own project to investors and regulators | Whether affected communities agree, and what the objections actually say |
| A provincial or federal government page | A ministry describing its own policy | The case against the policy, and what an auditor found |
| A news story | A journalist on a deadline, funded by subscriptions or advertising | Whether anything changed after publication |
| A social media post | Anyone at all, including people with a direct stake | Where the claim originally came from |

## The failure that catches everybody

The commonest error is not believing a lie. It is **believing something
that was true.** A page saying a project "requires an environmental
assessment" may have been right when written and wrong now, because the
law changed. Find the date every time — and if there is no date, the
absence is the finding.

The second commonest is treating a proponent's page as neutral because it
is calm. Tone is not evidence. A company describing its own mine and a
nation describing the same mine are both interested parties, and both are
owed the same reading.

## Where this course sets a higher bar

**Indigenous nations' positions** come from the nation's own published
statements, not from a summary, a government page, or a news paraphrase.
Nations differ from one another, and one nation's position can be more
layered than "for" or "against" — see [[Treaty and Territory Sources]].

**Text produced by generative artificial intelligence** has no author, no
funder, and no date of observation, and it will state things fluently
without having checked them. It can help you find a source; it is never
the source. If you cannot trace a claim to something published with an
author and a year, you do not have it yet.

## The habit

Before a fact goes into your work, write the source, the publisher, the
date of publication, and the date you looked. Then ask the last question:
**would somebody who disagrees with me accept this source?** If yes, use
it. If no, find the one they would, and use both.

Formats are in [[Citing Geographic Sources]]; where to look in the first
place is [[Finding the Right Data]].
