**The question:** who holds authority over land in Canada and the
resources under it — and where does that authority come from?

Treaties are not history. An agreement signed in 1850 and one signed in
2005 both bind the parties today, and land claims are legal processes
running now. Land here can be privately owned or public (Crown) land, and
that same ground may lie under a historical or a modern treaty or be
territory never surrendered — the categories stack, and surface and
subsurface rights need not share a holder.

> [!warning] How this seminar runs
> Argue with positions, not with people. Name your source every time you
> state a fact. Nobody here speaks for a nation they do not belong to,
> and nobody will be asked to. "I don't know" is a real contribution; a
> confident wrong answer is not.

## Prepare

Find out which treaty or territory this school sits within, from the
nation's own publications or a government record rather than somebody's
summary — [[Judging a Source]] is the test. Then bring **one** current
resource decision — a mine, a transmission line, a forestry licence — and
four facts: who proposes it, whose land it is on, what form of tenure
applies, and what stage the process has actually reached.

## Three positions that genuinely conflict

**Consent.** Where title was never surrendered, a project needs the
agreement of the nation that holds the land. Section 35 of the
*Constitution Act, 1982* recognizes and affirms existing Aboriginal and
treaty rights, and governments carry a duty to consult and accommodate.
In 2014, in *Tsilhqot'in Nation v. British Columbia*, the Supreme Court
of Canada declared Aboriginal title over a territory for the first time.

**Jurisdiction.** Provinces administer most public land and resources,
the federal Crown holds others, and Indigenous governments exercise their
own. Where these conflict, who decides is itself the dispute.

**Certainty.** Industry, unions, and some communities argue that
unresolved claims freeze investment and the work attached to it. Others
answer that "certainty" has usually meant certainty for the developer.

Different nations take different positions, and each decides for itself.
Open with your case, name the fact that would change your mind, and
expect no verdict — [[Land, Treaty, and Title]] and [[Who Decides?]] go on.
