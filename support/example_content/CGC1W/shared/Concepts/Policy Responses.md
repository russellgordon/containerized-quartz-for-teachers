A policy is a government's answer to a demographic fact. Because the fact
usually arrives years before the answer, most policy is catching up — and
because governments change, most policy is also unfinished.

## What the responses actually look like

Settlement services for newcomers are federally funded and locally delivered:
language classes, credential assessment, help finding housing and a first job.
They are ordinary and unglamorous, and their quality varies enormously by city,
because the organizations delivering them differ.

Aging-in-place programs — home care, accessible transit, snow clearing,
seniors' centres — are cheaper per person than institutional care and generally
what people say they want. They fail when the pieces are funded separately, so
a person gets the home care and not the ride to the appointment.

Infrastructure responses are the slowest. A transit line planned for a growing
suburb takes a decade; a hospital longer. Governments therefore build for the
population projected, not the one present, and are wrong in both directions
regularly.

Energy and utility responses can be structural. The Wataynikaneyap transmission
line, finished in 2024, connected remote First Nations in northwestern Ontario
to the provincial grid after decades of diesel generators that capped how much
housing a community could even build. A power line is a population policy when
the lack of one was the ceiling.

> [!tip] Four questions that turn a description into an evaluation
> **Who is eligible, and who falls just outside?** Eligibility rules are
> where a program quietly decides who it is not for.
>
> **Does it reach the people with the greatest need, or the people
> easiest to reach?** These are rarely the same group.
>
> **What does it cost per person served, compared to doing nothing?**
> Doing nothing also has a cost; it is just paid elsewhere.
>
> **Does it survive an election?** A three-year pilot is not a policy.

## Writing about policy without taking sides badly

You will be asked to recommend something. A recommendation is not an opinion —
it is a claim about what evidence supports, stated with its costs admitted and
its limits named. "Expand X" is weak. "Expand X for this group, at this cost,
because the data show this gap, accepting that it does nothing for that other
group" is a brief.

That is the shape of [[The Policy Brief]]. The demographic facts a policy is
answering are in [[What Population Change Does]].
