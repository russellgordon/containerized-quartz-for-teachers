Countries choose pictures of themselves. Canada's are mostly landforms:
mountains, ice, granite, water, a leaf. That is unusual. Plenty of
countries picture themselves with a building or a person instead.

A symbol is a choice, and every choice leaves something out. The Group of
Seven painted the Shield — rock, spruce, wind-bent pine — and those
canvases became visual shorthand for the whole country, even though
almost nobody lived where they were standing. The maple leaf on the flag
represents a tree whose native range covers only the southeastern corner
of the country.[^1] Neither fact makes the symbol false. They make it a
claim about what matters, which is a different and more interesting
thing.

## Places that name what they do

Where a landscape has been lived in for a long time, the names usually
describe it. Iqaluit means "place of many fish" in Inuktitut. Nunavut
means "our land". These are not decorations added to a map; they are the
map, recording what the place gives and whose it is. When a Canadian
place carries two names, comparing them tells you who did the naming and
what each namer thought was worth recording.

## Parks are identity with a fence around it

National and provincial parks turn a landscape into an official symbol.
That has a history worth stating plainly: several parks were created on
land from which Indigenous people were removed or excluded, and their
relationship to those places did not end when the boundary was drawn.
Some parks are now managed cooperatively. Gwaii Haanas, on Haida Gwaii,
is run by a board on which the Council of the Haida Nation and Parks
Canada each have a seat, and neither side can act alone.

The useful question is not *which symbols are the right ones*. It is
*whose relationship to this landscape is the symbol recording, and whose
is it leaving out*. Carry that question into
[[How Communities Value Land and Water]], and use it when you argue for
the identity of your region in [[The Region Study]].

[^1]: Sugar maple grows naturally in Ontario, Quebec, and the Maritimes.
    There is no native maple forest in Nunavut, and very little on the
    Prairies. The flag was first raised on 15 February 1965, after years
    of parliamentary argument about exactly this kind of question.
