From the air, the way buildings sit on the land falls into a few shapes, and
each shape is a record of a decision — usually about water, soil, or who did
the surveying.

| Pattern | What it looks like | Where you find it in Canada |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scattered | Buildings spread singly across the land | Farmsteads on the Ontario and Prairie survey grids |
| Linear | A single line following something | Long lots along the St. Lawrence; towns strung on a rail line or highway |
| Clustered | Buildings gathered tightly, land open around them | Villages, and most communities of the North |

## The survey is why

Two different surveys produced two of those patterns. Along the St. Lawrence,
land was divided under the seigneurial system into long narrow strips running
back from the river, so that every holding touched the water — the road and the
highway later followed the same line. Fly over the south shore and you can
still read a seventeenth-century decision about river access.

Southern Ontario was surveyed instead into a grid of concessions and lots of
about a hundred acres each, with a road allowance between. That grid produced
scattered farmsteads, roads that meet at right angles, and villages spaced
roughly a day's cart trip apart. The concession roads around the farm we visit
are that survey. So is the shape of most of the fields.

## Size, distribution, density

Three separate ideas, easily confused. **Size** is how many people.
**Distribution** is where they are relative to each other across a region.
**Density** is how many share a square kilometre. A community can be small and
dense — many northern communities are, because the built-up area is tightly
bounded — or large and thin, like a suburb.

What decided all three, historically: flat land, water for drinking and
transport, soil that grows food, a climate that permits farming, and a
connection to somewhere else. That last one keeps changing, which is why
settlement patterns are not fixed. A town founded on a canal, then served by
rail, then bypassed by a highway, has been three different places.

Most Canadians now live in a narrow band across the south, and the rest of the
country is not empty — it is occupied at low density, by communities whose
locations follow rivers, coasts, traplines and resources rather than roads.

Read the pattern on the ground in [[The Land Use Walk]], and read where it came
from in [[Where People Settled, and Why]].
