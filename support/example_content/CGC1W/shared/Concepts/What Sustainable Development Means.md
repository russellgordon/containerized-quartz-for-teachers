The definition everyone quotes came out of a United Nations commission in
1987: development that meets the needs of the present without
compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
It is a good sentence and it settles nothing, because it does not say
what counts as a need, or how far ahead "future generations" reaches, or
who decides when those two collide.

That is why there are competing **models**. A model is an attempt to make
the definition operational — to turn a sentence into something you can
actually check a decision against.

**The three pillars** treat environment, society, and economy as three
overlapping circles, with sustainability in the middle. It is easy to
teach and easy to abuse, because three circles of equal size invite you
to trade a little environment for a little economy indefinitely.

**The doughnut** sets two hard rings. The inner ring is a social
foundation nobody should fall below — food, water, housing, health,
education. The outer ring is an ecological ceiling nothing should push
past. The safe space is the ring between them, and neither boundary is
negotiable against the other.

**The UN Sustainable Development Goals** are seventeen goals agreed in
2015 with targets and indicators attached. Their strength is that they
are measurable and that almost every country has signed on. Their
weakness is that goals can pull against each other, and the framework
does not tell you which one yields.

**Indigenous frameworks** in Canada and elsewhere generally start
somewhere different: not from managing a stock of resources, but from
obligations owed to land, water, and animals, and to people not yet born.
The principle often expressed in Haudenosaunee teaching — that a decision
should be weighed for its effect seven generations ahead — is a
requirement about time horizon, and it changes what counts as an
acceptable answer.

> [!success]- One question that separates the models (click to expand)
> Ask: *what does this model refuse to trade away?* The three pillars
> refuse almost nothing, which is why a company can adopt it painlessly.
> The doughnut refuses to cross either ring. The SDGs refuse to abandon
> any of the seventeen, but give no rule for conflicts. Indigenous
> frameworks generally refuse to compromise an obligation that runs to
> future generations. Run this question on any sustainability claim you
> meet, including the ones in your own writing.

Choosing a model is not a neutral step; it decides in advance which
arguments can win. That is the subject of
[[Arguing About Sustainability]], and you will apply one of these models
to a real object in [[The Product's Journey]].
