A **pattern** is where and when something happens. A **trend** is which
direction it is moving over time. They are different claims and they need
different evidence, so keep them apart in your writing.

Canada's physical events are not scattered at random. Earthquakes cluster
off the coast of British Columbia, where an oceanic plate is sliding
beneath the continent, and in a second, quieter band along the St.
Lawrence and Ottawa valleys, where the crust is still adjusting along
ancient faults. Tornadoes concentrate in southern Ontario and the
southern Prairies, in late spring and summer, where warm moist air from
the south meets cool dry air from the north. Avalanches belong to the
Western Cordillera, because avalanches need slopes. Hurricanes reach
Atlantic Canada in late summer and autumn, weakened but not finished, and
almost never reach it in April.

Each of those is a pattern, and each has a physical reason. If you can
state the pattern but not the reason, you have described rather than
explained.

## Trends are harder

A trend needs the same measurement, made the same way, over a long
stretch of time. That is rarer than it sounds. Two of the clearest in
Canada right now:

- The area burned by wildfire in a bad year has grown. The 2023 season
  burned on the order of fifteen million hectares, several times the
  average of the preceding decades.
- Glaciers in the Western Cordillera and the Arctic have been losing mass
  for decades, measured by repeat surveys of the same ice.

> [!warning] More reports is not the same as more events
> Canada's confirmed tornado count rose sharply after researchers began
> systematically surveying remote damage with satellite imagery and
> drones. More tornadoes were *found*. That is a change in detection, not
> necessarily a change in the atmosphere. Before you call something a
> trend, ask whether the way it is counted changed during your time
> series. This question will save you from a bad conclusion more often
> than any other question in this course.

The Canadian Disaster Database is the usual starting point, and it is a
record of *disasters* — events that hurt people — not of events. A
landslide in an empty valley may never appear in it. Bring that caution
with you into [[Judging a Source]], and use it when you assemble the
evidence for [[The Risk Report]].
