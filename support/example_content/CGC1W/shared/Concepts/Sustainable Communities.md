A sustainable community is not a green one. It is one that can still be here in
fifty years — which requires an economy that survives a shock, a society people
want to stay in, and an environment that keeps working. Fail any of the three
and the other two eventually follow.

## Economic sustainability means surviving the loss

The multiplier effect describes what a large employer really is. A mill paying
four hundred people also supports the diner, the garage, the dentist, the
hockey program and part of the municipal budget. When it closes, none of those
lose a customer gradually; they lose a share of the town's income at once. Some
communities never recover. Elliot Lake, Ontario, lost its uranium mines in the
1990s and rebuilt itself around affordable housing for retirees — a second
economy chosen deliberately, from the assets it had left.

The lesson is not "diversify", which is easy to say. It is that resilience is
decided years before the shock, by whether anyone was building a second thing
while the first still worked.

> [!warning] Watch what a community can lose in one decision
> A single employer, a single road, a single water source, a single grade
> school. Fragility is usually singular. When you assess a community's
> sustainability, count how many of its essentials have exactly one
> supplier — that number tells you more than any environmental score.

## Social sustainability is infrastructure too

People stay where they have somewhere to go. Libraries, arenas, parks,
community centres, a main street with the lights on — these are not amenities
in the decorative sense. They are where a community's ties get made, and ties
are what makes people put up with a hard winter or a long commute.

Green space does double duty: it cools, it absorbs rain, it holds habitat, and
it is one of the few places where people who share nothing else meet.
Naturalized parks planted with native species do the ecological part better
than mown grass and cost less to maintain — a rare case of the cheaper option
also being the better one.

## Judge the plan, not the vocabulary

Every municipality has a sustainability plan and the vocabulary is standard.
The questions that separate a real one: does it set targets with dates, must
anyone report progress publicly, is it funded in the actual budget, and does it
survive the next council. A strategy with none of those is a document, not a
plan.
