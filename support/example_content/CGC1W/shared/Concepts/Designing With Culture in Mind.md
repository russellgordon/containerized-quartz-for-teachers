Every community was designed by somebody, from somewhere, carrying assumptions
about how people live. When the assumptions match the residents, nobody
notices. When they do not, the mismatch shows up as housing that does not fit
and buildings people avoid.

## Three places where the design started with the residents

**Oujé-Bougoumou, Eeyou Istchee, Quebec.** The Cree of Oujé-Bougoumou were
relocated seven times in under fifty years as mining moved across their
territory, ending up scattered and badly housed. Recognition came in 1989 and a
construction agreement in 1992; the village they then built on the shore of
Lake Opemiska was laid out with the architect Douglas Cardinal, and is heated
by a district system burning sawdust from nearby mills. It exists because the
community spent decades demanding it, not because anyone offered.

**Sen̓áḵw, Vancouver.** Squamish families were removed from this land at the
mouth of False Creek in 1913. About ten acres came back to the Nation by court
settlement in 2003, and because it is reserve land, City of Vancouver zoning
does not apply. The Nation is building thousands of rental homes there, at a
density no adjacent block could get approved for. The design element that
mattered most was jurisdictional: who holds the land, and who has the authority
to say yes.

**Six Nations of the Grand River, Ontario.** Its community plan states
environmental goals in terms of what the territory should provide — water clean
enough, forest cover large enough, species present enough that people can
safely eat the food and use the medicines that come from its forests and
rivers. That is a different way of writing a target than a percentage of green
space, and it produces different decisions.

> [!note] Housing that fits what people actually do
> Inuit organizations have argued for decades that housing designed in
> the south and shipped north arrives without what a northern household
> needs — a cold porch to work in, storage for a snowmobile and gear,
> room to butcher and share country food, space for more than a nuclear
> family. None of that is style. It is what the building is for.

## The line between design and decoration

Culturally informed design is not a mural, a name, or a pattern applied to a
wall of a building somebody else planned. Those can be genuine, and they can
also be the visible part of a project where every real decision was made
elsewhere. The test is whether the community held authority over the plan,
whether the design changes how the place works rather than how it looks, and
whether it is still theirs to change in twenty years.

Ask that, in that order, of any project you assess in [[The Local Inquiry]].
The broader argument is in [[Who Decides?]].
