Almost nobody moves for one reason. People move because something at home
pushes and something elsewhere pulls, and because they can — the money, the
papers and the relatives are as much a part of the explanation as the job
posting.

## Two different movements

**Migration within Canada** needs no permission. People move province to
province chasing work and housing costs, which is why Alberta gains people when
oil and gas hire, and why interprovincial flows reverse when that changes.
Rural areas lose young adults to cities and often gain retirees back. Movement
between First Nations communities and cities runs both directions and across
lifetimes: in 2021, 44.3 per cent of Indigenous people lived in large urban
centres, and city and home community frequently remain a single connected life
rather than a departure.

**Immigration to Canada** is controlled. The federal government sets a levels
plan each year, sorted into categories — economic, family, and refugees and
protected persons — and admits people against those targets. Numbers changed
sharply this decade: after several years above 450,000 permanent residents
annually, the plan for 2026 through 2028 sets the target at 380,000 per year,
alongside a deliberate reduction in temporary residents such as international
students and foreign workers.

> [!note] The words are not interchangeable
> A **permanent resident** may live and work anywhere in Canada
> indefinitely but cannot vote and can lose that status. A **citizen**
> may vote and holds a Canadian passport. A **non-permanent resident** is
> here on a study permit, work permit, or while a refugee claim is
> decided. "Landed immigrant" is the old term for permanent resident and
> still appears in older documents. Newspapers blur these constantly. In
> this course, do not.

## Where the movement lands

Newcomers do not spread evenly. They arrive where relatives already are, where
the jobs are, and where a community speaks their language — so Toronto,
Montreal, Vancouver and Calgary take a disproportionate share, and every
province has programs trying to change that. Francophone immigration outside
Quebec is one such effort, aimed at keeping minority-language communities
viable.

Push and pull explain the decision. They do not explain the destination. For
that you need networks — who is already there — and this is the single most
reliable predictor of where a migrant settles.

The consequences, for both ends of the move, are worked out in
[[What Population Change Does]], and you will map real flows in
[[The Population Profile]].
