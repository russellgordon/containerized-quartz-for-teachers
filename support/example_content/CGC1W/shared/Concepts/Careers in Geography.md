Geography is not often a job title. It is a way of working that shows up
inside other people's jobs: knowing where things are, why they are there,
and what happens if they move. The geospatial part of it — GPS, GIS,
remote sensing, surveying — has quietly become standard equipment in
trades and professions that never used to touch it.

| Job | The geography inside it | The tools |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land surveyor | Establishing legal property boundaries that hold up in court | Satellite positioning, total station, survey records |
| Hydrographic surveyor | Charting the seafloor so ships do not hit it | Multibeam sonar, tide models, charting software |
| Land-use planner | Deciding what may be built where, and defending it publicly | GIS, zoning by-laws, official plans |
| Emergency management coordinator | Mapping hazard, exposure, and evacuation routes before the event | Hazard databases, GIS, radio and alerting systems |
| Forestry technician | Inventory, growth rates, and where the harvest is allowed | GPS, aerial and satellite imagery, stand data |
| Conservation officer | Enforcing harvest and land regulations across a large area | Vehicles and boats, patrol mapping, field evidence |
| Agricultural equipment technician | Keeping guidance systems on tractors and sprayers accurate | GPS receivers, correction signals, diagnostics |
| First Nation lands manager | Administering land holdings, leases, and registries for a First Nation | Land registry systems, GIS, survey plans |
| Marine navigator or pilot | Moving a ship safely through a specific, difficult stretch of water | Radar, electronic charts, local knowledge of the run |
| Transit planner | Where people are, where they need to go, and what it costs to connect them | Ridership data, census data, network modelling |

Two of those deserve a note. **Land surveyor** is a licensed profession
in every province, and the boundary a surveyor establishes is a legal
fact, not an estimate — which is why the training is long. **First Nation
lands manager** exists because many First Nations administer their own
lands directly, under their own land codes, rather than under the federal
default, and that work needs people who can read a survey plan and run a
registry.

Several of these are skilled trades or technologist roles reached through
college and apprenticeship rather than a university degree. The
agricultural equipment technician is a good example: the calibration work
is straightforward geospatial technology, and there is a shortage of
people who can do it.

You will use several of these tools yourself in [[Using a Web GIS]], and
you will do the whole cycle — question, evidence, analysis, audience — in
[[The Local Inquiry]], which is very close to what most of this list does
all day.
