Quality of life is not one thing, so it cannot be one number. Geographers get
around that by choosing indicators — measurable things that stand in for
something larger — and the choosing is where the judgement lives.

## What the standard indicators are standing in for

Life expectancy at birth is not really about death. It is a summary of
nutrition, housing, medical care, water, injury and violence, compressed into a
single year count. Infant mortality is the same trick applied to the most
fragile year of life, which is why it moves early when conditions change.

The Human Development Index takes three dimensions — a long and healthy life,
knowledge, and a decent standard of living — and combines them so countries can
be ranked. It was built deliberately to stop economies from being judged on
income alone. Canada scores high on it, consistently.

That is exactly the problem.

> [!warning] A national average is a place nobody lives
> Canada's overall numbers describe a country that, on paper, has largely
> solved food access. In 2025, 24.0 per cent of people in Canada lived in
> a food-insecure household — and in Nunavut the figure was 56.4 per
> cent. Both facts come from the same survey in the same year. An average
> that spans them tells you about neither.

Every indicator has this property. Clean water is near-universal nationally,
while some First Nations have lived under boil-water advisories for years at a
time.

## Indicators move together, and that is data

Two indicators rising and falling in step is called a correlation, and plotting
one against the other on a scatter graph is one of the fastest analytical moves
in this course. Income and life expectancy correlate. Education and income
correlate. Infant mortality and access to prenatal care correlate.

Correlation is not cause. But a strong correlation is a question worth chasing,
and a break in one — a place that has the income but not the outcome — is
usually the most interesting point on the whole graph. Look for the outlier
before you look at the trend line.

When you write about well-being, name the indicator, the year, the geography,
and who is excluded. Then say what it cannot see: almost none of these numbers
measure loneliness, safety, belonging, or whether people want to stay.

pulled yourself in [[Working With Census Data]].
