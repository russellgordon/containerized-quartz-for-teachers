Weather is what happened on Tuesday. Climate is what usually happens in
March, worked out from thirty years of Tuesdays. Almost every argument
about climate that goes wrong goes wrong right there — one cold week is
weather, and it settles nothing about climate either way.

Climate does not vary randomly across Canada. Six controls do the work,
and once you know them you can predict a station's climate graph roughly
right before you look it up.

**Latitude.** The further north, the lower the sun rides and the more
atmosphere its light must cross. This sets the ceiling on how warm a
place can get.

**Nearness to water.** Water heats and cools far more slowly than land.
Coastal places have mild winters, cool summers, and a small annual range.
Inland places swing hard in both directions.

**Ocean currents.** The Pacific coast is washed by relatively mild water;
the Labrador Current runs cold water south past Newfoundland. Two
coastlines at similar latitudes, two very different winters.

**Winds and air masses.** Canada sits in the belt of westerlies, so most
of our weather arrives from the west — except in summer, when warm humid
air can push up from the Gulf of Mexico into southern Ontario and give us
the sticky nights and the evening thunderstorms.

**Elevation.** Air cools as it rises. A mountain valley and the peak
above it are in different climates without being in different places.

**Relief.** Mountains force air upward, which cools it and wrings out its
moisture on the windward side. The leeward side gets the leftovers. This
is why the interior of British Columbia has desert-like pockets a few
hundred kilometres from a rainforest.

Some of what shapes our climate is not local at all, and that is
[[Global Systems, Local Weather]]. Where the controls change fastest, the
regions in [[Canada's Physical Regions]] tend to change too.
