Every list of location factors is the same list: raw materials, energy,
transport, labour, market, land, government policy, community
consent, environmental constraint. Reciting it earns nothing. The
analysis is in saying **which factor dominates for this industry, and
what the firm gives up to satisfy it**.

| Industry | The factor that usually decides | What gets sacrificed to it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mining and oil extraction | The deposit — it cannot be moved | Everything else; the road, the power line, and the housing get built to reach it |
| Smelting and heavy processing | Cheap, abundant electricity | Distance from the market; the product is shipped a long way |
| Food processing | Nearness to a perishable input | Cheaper land elsewhere |
| Auto assembly | Market access plus a dense transport network | Land cost, in exchange for being close to customers and parts suppliers |
| Retail and personal services | Population and traffic | High rent, because being anywhere else means no customers |
| Knowledge industries | A supply of skilled people | Almost anything; they will pay for the location those people want |

The Holland Marsh, north of Toronto, is a worked example of several
factors agreeing. A wetland was drained to expose black muck soil,
unusually rich in organic matter. That soil grows vegetables well, water
for irrigation is at hand, the climate suits the crop, a very large
market sits an hour south, and a major highway runs alongside. Change any
one of those and the farms are somewhere else. The farm five minutes down
the road runs on a smaller version of the same reasoning, as
[[The Farm Visit]] should have made obvious.

## The factor most lists leave out

In Canada, land access is not settled by price alone. Where a project may
affect Aboriginal or treaty rights, the Crown has a legal duty to consult
the Indigenous nations concerned, and where appropriate to accommodate
them. That duty affects timing, design, and sometimes whether a project
proceeds at all — which makes it a location factor in the plainest sense,
even though it appears on no economics textbook's list. See
[[Land, Treaty, and Title]] for what that duty is and where it comes
from.

The useful move, when you analyze a real site, is to ask what the firm
paid extra for. Whatever it overpaid for is the factor that mattered
most, and it is usually visible on a map.

You will apply this directly against a real community's economy in
[[Industries Built on People]].
