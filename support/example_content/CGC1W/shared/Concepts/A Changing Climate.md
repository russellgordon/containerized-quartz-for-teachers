Canada is warming faster than the world as a whole — roughly twice the
global rate, and about three times in the North. That is not a Canadian
misfortune; it is what physics predicts for a large landmass at high
latitude, where less snow and ice means a darker surface, which absorbs
more sunlight, which melts more snow and ice.

The consequences do not stay in one category, and a good analysis says
which category it is talking about.

Take thawing permafrost in the Arctic. Environmentally, ground that has
been frozen for millennia releases methane as it thaws. Socially, houses,
runways, and pipelines built on that ground shift and crack, and they
were not designed to be moved. Economically, the repair bill falls on
communities with the smallest tax base in the country. Politically, a
longer open-water season makes the Northwest Passage more navigable,
which pushes Canada's claim to those waters from a quiet question into a
loud one. One physical change, four kinds of consequence.

## Two different verbs

| The problem | Mitigation — reduce the cause | Adaptation — reduce the harm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hotter, longer summers in cities | Fewer emissions from buildings and vehicles; carbon audits on new public projects | Tree canopy, porous paving, cooling centres open during heat warnings |
| More intense rainfall | Emissions reduction, since the intensity tracks warming | Redrawn flood maps, updated zoning by-laws, bigger storm sewers |
| Shorter winter road season in the North | Emissions reduction | All-season road construction, more air and barge freight, rescheduled resupply |
| Drought on the Prairies | Emissions reduction | Drought-tolerant varieties, irrigation, changed crop insurance |

Notice that the mitigation column is nearly the same line every time and
the adaptation column is different every time. Mitigation is one global
problem. Adaptation is a thousand local ones, and it is where a
municipality actually has power.

Both are needed, and treating them as rivals is a common mistake.
Adaptation without mitigation is running to stay in place. Mitigation
without adaptation ignores warming already locked in.

Communities that depend directly on ice, snow, and animal populations
notice change first and in the most detail. Several northern monitoring
programs now combine satellite data with locally held Inuit and First
Nations observations of ice, water, and animal behaviour, because the
satellite sees the whole coast weekly and the local record sees one bay
over sixty years. You need both.

This is the heart of [[The Risk Report]], and it shapes every argument in
[[Energy Choices]].
