Before anyone extracts a resource, someone must hold the legal right to
the land it sits under. In Canada that right takes several forms, and the
form decides who has to agree before anything happens. **Fee simple** is
private ownership. **Crown land**, held by a federal or provincial
government, covers most of Canada's land area. **Reserve land** is held
by the Crown for a First Nation's use and benefit, though many First
Nations now administer their own lands under their own land codes. Much
of Canada is also **treaty land**, and much of it is **unceded**.

## Treaties are agreements, and they are still running

A treaty is an agreement between the Crown and an Indigenous nation. The
Peace and Friendship Treaties in the Maritimes in the 1700s transferred
no land. The Robinson Treaties of 1850 and the Numbered Treaties of
1871–1921 were written by the Crown as surrenders in exchange for
reserves, payments, and continued hunting and fishing rights. Modern
treaties negotiated since the 1970s, such as the Nunavut Agreement, set
out land, resource revenue, and self-government in far more detail.

None of this is closed history. Section 35 of the *Constitution Act,
1982* recognizes and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights, and
treaty obligations bind the Crown now. The Robinson Huron annuity had
been fixed at four dollars a person since 1875; after years of litigation
Canada and Ontario agreed in 2023 to pay ten billion dollars for past
annuities, finalized in 2024. The treaty itself continues.

Nations also disagree about what particular treaties mean. The Crown's
written texts speak of surrender; many First Nations hold that what their
ancestors agreed to, as their oral histories record it, was to share the
land. That gap is still being worked through — do not write as though it
were settled.

## Title, claims, and consent

Where no treaty was ever made — much of British Columbia, and areas
elsewhere — land is **unceded**. Aboriginal title is a nation's
collective right to the land itself, arising from occupation before the
Crown asserted sovereignty. In 2014, in *Tsilhqot'in Nation v. British
Columbia*, the Supreme Court declared Aboriginal title over about 1,750
square kilometres — the first such declaration in Canadian history.

A **land claim** is a legal process, not a demand: comprehensive claims
concern territory where no treaty exists, specific claims allege that an
existing obligation was not honoured. Separately, where a decision may
adversely affect asserted or established rights, the Crown has a duty to
consult and, where appropriate, accommodate. Canada has also legislated a
commitment to the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples,
which speaks of free, prior, and informed consent. How those two
standards fit together is argued about now, and Indigenous nations do not
hold one common position on it.

> [!note] Use the words precisely
> "Land claim" names a process. "Unceded" describes a legal status.
> "Historic treaty" says when a treaty was signed, not whether it still
> operates. Find out which treaty or territory your school stands on.
