Comparison is the cheapest analytical tool in geography and the easiest to do
badly. Two places, one measure, and the differences do the explaining —
provided the measure means the same thing in both places.

## Make the comparison honest first

Before you say anything about two communities, four things have to match: the
**year**, the **boundary**, the **definition**, and the **source**. Population
counted in 2016 against population counted in 2021 is a trend, not a
comparison. A city's own boundary against another city's metro area is
nonsense. "Unemployment" measured two ways is two different words.

Once those match, differences mean something. Until they match, every
difference you find may be an artefact of the paperwork.

> [!tip] Ratios travel; totals do not
> Comparing a town of 9,000 to a city of 900,000 on total anything will
> only tell you which is bigger. Convert to a rate — per thousand people,
> per square kilometre, as a percentage of the population — and the two
> become comparable. This single move rescues most weak comparisons.

## Then ask what would explain a difference

A community's numbers come from its history and its position. A resource town
has a young working-age bulge and few seniors, because people arrive for work
and leave when it ends. A retirement destination inverts that. A university
town has a spike at ages 18 to 24 that vanishes at 25. A suburb built in one
decade ages all at once, so its schools fill and then empty on a schedule you
can predict twenty years out.

Reach outside Canada and the same logic holds, with the range widened.
Comparing Canada's age structure to a country with high birth rates shows two
different futures: one worrying about who will fund pensions, one worrying
about who will employ a very large generation of young people. Neither is a
defect. They are different points in the same demographic transition.

## Three comparisons, not two

Your community against one other community tells you they differ. Your
community against two others starts to tell you which factor matters — because
you can hold something constant. Two towns the same size, one on a highway and
one not. Two cities the same age, one with a university. That is as close as
geography gets to a controlled experiment, and it is worth building your
comparison to allow it.

Do this properly, with real numbers, in [[The Population Profile]], and keep
the sources straight with [[Citing Geographic Sources]].
