Every parcel of land in a community is doing something, and what it is doing is
mostly a decision somebody made and wrote down. Land use is the map of those
decisions, and it explains more about daily life than any other single layer.

| Use | What it needs nearby | What it produces for neighbours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Residential | Schools, shops, transit, quiet | People, and demand for everything else |
| Commercial | Customers, parking or foot traffic, visibility | Jobs, traffic, tax revenue |
| Industrial | Rail, highway, power, water, space | Jobs, noise, trucks, sometimes emissions |
| Institutional | Central access, transit | Hospitals, schools, arenas — and no tax revenue |
| Open space | Almost nothing | Cooling, drainage, recreation, habitat |
| Agricultural | Class 1 soil, water, room to work | Food, and pressure to sell for housing |
| Transportation | Continuity above all | Access, and the barriers that divide a place |

## Distribution is the part that bites

Which uses exist matters less than where they sit relative to each other.
Industry beside housing is an old, ordinary injustice, and it is almost never
randomly distributed — the cheapest housing has usually ended up next to the
loudest, dirtiest land use, in Canadian cities and everywhere else. Ask who
lives at each interface and the map turns into an argument.

Distance matters just as much. A park nobody can walk to is not doing the work
of a park. A grocery store three bus transfers away has effectively been
removed from the neighbourhood, even though it is on the map. The question is
never "is there one?" but "for whom, and how far?"

## Zoning is what is allowed, not what is there

Zoning is the municipal bylaw specifying what may be built on each parcel.
Actual use is what stands there now. They differ constantly: land zoned for
industry sitting empty, a house operating as a business, a parking lot waiting
for a market that never came. Both layers are real and you need both. The
zoning map tells you what a council intends; the walk tells you what happened.

Accessibility runs through all of it. Curb cuts, step-free entrances, and where
the benches are decide whether a community is usable by people with
disabilities and by anyone pushing a stroller or using a walker — which, over a
lifetime, is nearly everyone.
