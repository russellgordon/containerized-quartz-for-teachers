Where your electricity comes from was decided before you were born, by a
government, on the basis of what the local geography made cheap. That is why
the same appliance has a different carbon footprint in Montreal, Toronto, and
Edmonton.

## Canada does not have one grid

Electricity is provincial. Quebec, British Columbia, Manitoba, and Newfoundland
and Labrador built on big rivers and run overwhelmingly on hydroelectricity.
Ontario built nuclear: in 2025 its supply was about 46 per cent nuclear and 23
per cent water power, with natural gas and other sources near 19 per cent and
wind and solar most of the rest. Alberta and Saskatchewan built on the fossil
fuels beneath them.

None of those were moral choices. They were geography and timing. Which means
the transition ahead of each province is a different job, and comparing
provincial emissions without saying what each started with is not analysis.

## Three levels of decision, very different sizes

Governments set the frame: what may be built, what is subsidized, what a tonne
of carbon costs, what a building code requires. Corporations decide where to
invest and how much energy their operations use — industry consumes far more
than households, so one industrial decision can outweigh a great many personal
ones. Individuals decide about the trip, the thermostat, the appliance.

> [!success]- Why the individual level still matters (click to expand)
> Not because your thermostat moves the national total. Because
> individual decisions aggregate into demand, and demand is what
> utilities plan around. Millions of households insulating, or driving
> less, changes what has to be built — and a generating station not built
> is the cleanest energy there is.
>
> There is a second reason. Individuals vote and show up at council. The
> largest effects available to you personally are political, not
> domestic.

## Access is not evenly distributed

Roughly two hundred remote communities in Canada — many of them First Nations
and Inuit — have run on diesel generators trucked or flown in, with the fuel
cost, the noise, and the hard ceiling that puts on new housing. The
Wataynikaneyap transmission line, completed in 2024 and majority-owned by the
First Nations it serves, connected seventeen such communities in northwestern
Ontario to the provincial grid. Energy policy there was not about emissions
first. It was about whether a community could grow at all.
