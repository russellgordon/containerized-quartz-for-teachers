Communities are built where the land offers something — a harbour, a river
crossing, flat ground, soil. The same features that made the site worth
choosing are usually the ones that will test it, and the testing now arrives
more often than the original builders planned for.

## The site gives, and the site takes

Shorelines are the clearest case. Water gave transport, food, power and flat
ground, so almost every old Canadian settlement sits on one — and shorelines
flood, erode, and rise. Tuktoyaktuk, an Inuvialuit community on the Beaufort
Sea, is losing up to a metre of coastline a year as sea ice forms later, storms
reach further, and the frozen ground holding the bank together thaws. Tens of
millions of federal dollars have gone into armouring the shore, and the work is
openly described as buying time rather than solving it.

Permafrost changes what a building even is. Inuvik was laid out with its
structures on piles driven metres into ground that is frozen hundreds of metres
deep, lifted clear of the surface so the building's own heat does not thaw its
foundation, with water and sewer running above ground in insulated boxes called
utilidors. That is not decoration. Buried pipe freezes, and heat melts the
ground out from under whatever stands on it.

> [!warning] Ordinary rain is now a design problem
> The most expensive weather in southern Canada is not the hurricane. It
> is the short, intense downpour that overwhelms a storm sewer built for
> a lighter storm, backs up into basements, and floods roads. Cities are
> responding with things that sound minor and are not: permeable paving,
> restored wetlands, rain gardens, oversized culverts, and rules keeping
> new building out of floodplains. Green infrastructure is drainage
> equipment that happens to be alive.

## Planning ahead of the hazard

The strongest tool a municipality has is refusal — not permitting building in
the floodplain, on the unstable slope, on the eroding bank. It is also the
least popular, because the risk is a probability and the lost development is a
certain number of dollars, and one of those is much easier to put in a press
release.

Prevention and protection also differ in cost and in fairness. A seawall
protects who is behind it and often worsens erosion further along the shore. A
wetland left intact protects everyone downstream and produces no
ribbon-cutting. Ask both what it protects and what it moves.
