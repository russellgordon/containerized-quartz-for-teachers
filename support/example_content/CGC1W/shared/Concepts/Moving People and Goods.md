Transport decisions outlive almost everything else a community does. A road
built in 1960 is still deciding where people can live today, because everything
since has been built around it. That is why arguments about a bus route get
heated: they are arguments about the next fifty years.

## Capacity is about people, not vehicles

A traffic lane moves a fairly fixed number of vehicles an hour no matter how
wide the road gets. The same width of dedicated lane carrying buses, or a rail
track, moves several times as many people, because each vehicle carries more.
Widening a highway also reliably fills it again — induced demand, the
observation that new capacity attracts new trips until congestion returns to
roughly where it was.[^induced]

This is not an argument that highways are useless. Freight needs them, and most
of Canada is nowhere near dense enough for rail transit. It is an argument that
"we will fix congestion by adding lanes" is a claim with a poor record, and you
should ask for the evidence.

## Different geographies, different answers

Between cities, Canada is testing whether high-speed rail finally works here.
Alto, the planned electrified line linking Toronto, Ottawa, Montreal and Quebec
City, is in its design phase, with the first segment announced between Ottawa
and Montreal and construction not expected until around the end of this decade.
Rail suits that corridor because the cities are large, close, and strung in a
line.

Within cities, the choice is between serving and shaping: build transit where
people already are, or use it to decide where they go next.

Outside cities the problem changes again. Rural communities have distance and
too few riders for conventional transit. Many northern communities have no
all-season road at all and depend on winter roads built on frozen ground for a
season that is shortening — which turns a transport question into a
cost-of-living question, since whatever a truck cannot bring must fly.

The environmental test is never the vehicle's emissions alone. It is emissions
per person moved, the land the corridor consumes, what it cuts in half, and
what it makes possible to build. A line that lets four thousand homes go up
beside it does more than the trips it carries.

Where this shows up in a product's price is [[The Product's Journey]]; the
neighbourhood-scale consequences are in [[Forces That Shape a Community]].

[^induced]: Measured repeatedly since the 1990s in North America and
    Europe, with a consistent result: added capacity on a congested urban
    route is largely absorbed within a decade.
