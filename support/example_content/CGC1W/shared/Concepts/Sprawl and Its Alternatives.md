Sprawl is not "suburbs". It is a specific pattern: low density, separated uses,
and street networks that make driving the only practical way to get anywhere.
Each of those three can be changed on its own, which is why the alternatives
are not one alternative.

## What spreading outward costs

The land a city grows onto was doing something first, and near most Canadian
cities it was farming. Ontario's farmland fell from about 12.35 million acres
in 2016 to 11.77 million in 2021 — roughly 319 acres a day, though the
definition of a farm also changed between those censuses, so treat the headline
as a strong signal rather than a precise rate. Class 1 soil is the product of
thousands of years, and it sits precisely where cities want to expand.

| Cost | Why it follows from the pattern |
| --- | --- |
| Farmland loss | Cities grew where soil was best, so expansion eats it |
| Longer trips | Separated uses mean nothing is near anything |
| Infrastructure | More pipe, road and wire per household, forever |
| Runoff | Pavement sheds water that soil used to absorb |
| Heat islands | Asphalt and roofs store heat; a suburb runs warmer than nearby countryside |
| Habitat | Wetlands drained and woodlots cleared do not come back |

The infrastructure cost surprises people. A subdivision is built by a developer
and then maintained by the municipality for a century. Low density means fewer
households paying for each kilometre of water main, and that arithmetic
surfaces decades later as a repair backlog.

## The alternatives, honestly

**Intensification and infill** — building within the existing footprint, on
parking lots, over plazas, in gentle density along main streets. The cheapest
option per household and the most locally contested.

**Urban growth boundaries** — a line beyond which servicing is not extended.
Ontario's Greenbelt, roughly two million acres around the Greater Toronto Area,
is the largest such protection in the country, and the political fight is
always about moving the line.

**Walkable, mixed-use neighbourhoods** — putting daily needs within a short
walk, so a trip stops requiring a car. A design choice, not a density
threshold.

None is free. Intensification raises land prices where it happens. Boundaries
push growth to the far side of the line unless housing actually gets built
inside it. The work is choosing which costs a community will accept, in
writing.
