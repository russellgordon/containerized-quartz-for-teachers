A resource map of Canada is a geology map wearing different colours. Lay
one on the other and the symbols line up, because the physical process
that made the rock is the same process that put the resource there.

Work through it as cause and effect rather than as a list to memorize.

**Ancient volcanism and mountain-building on the Shield** concentrated
metals in the rock — nickel, copper, gold, cobalt, and the platinum group.
That is why Sudbury, Timmins, Val-d'Or, and Flin Flon are mining towns and
why the Ring of Fire deposits sit where they do in the James Bay Lowlands
of northern Ontario.

**A warm shallow sea over the Interior Plains**, and the organic material
that settled into it, became the sedimentary layers now drilled for oil
and natural gas. The same sea, evaporating, left the potash beds of
Saskatchewan — a deposit large enough that Canada is one of the world's
leading suppliers of the fertilizer that grows other countries' food.

**Glaciers** sorted and dumped enormous volumes of sand and gravel across
southern Canada. That is aggregate: the least glamorous resource in this
course and the one every road, foundation, and sidewalk is made of.
Aggregate is cheap per tonne and expensive to move, so it is quarried
close to where it is used, which is why the pits are near the cities.

**Shallow continental shelves** on three coasts hold the fisheries. The
Grand Banks are shallow because they are drowned parts of the continent,
not deep ocean.

> [!tip] The question that unlocks every resource map
> Do not ask "what is here?" Ask "what process put it here, and where else
> did that process happen?" A student who knows that potash forms from
> evaporating seawater can predict where to look for potash on a map they
> have never seen. That is the difference between knowing facts and
> reading a map.

Fresh water deserves its own warning. Canada holds a very large share of
the world's fresh water, but a great deal of it is stored rather than
renewed each year, and much of what does flow drains north — away from
the southern band where most Canadians live. Abundance and accessibility
are two different measurements.
