Nobody decided that a neighbourhood should become unaffordable. It happened
through thousands of separate transactions, each reasonable on its own, adding
up to something no one chose. That is what a force is in geography — a pressure
with no author.

## Money, politics, and who ends up living where

**Economic forces** work through price. When a neighbourhood becomes desirable,
rents rise, buildings are renovated or replaced, and the people who made it
desirable often cannot afford to stay. That is gentrification: not new arrivals
being villains, but the arithmetic of land value working faster than incomes
do.

**Political forces** work through decisions. A transit line, a zoning change, a
highway extension, a decision to permit or refuse — each is made by people who
can be named, in meetings that are open to the public and largely unattended.

**Social and cultural forces** work through who wants to be where. Researchers
use "youthification" for the concentration of young adults in central
neighbourhoods near work and transit, which reshapes what gets built: smaller
units, more rental, different retail.

## One corridor holding all three

Along Eglinton Avenue West in Toronto, Little Jamaica grew up as a
concentration of Black-owned businesses — barbershops, record stores,
restaurants — with a reach far beyond the blocks it occupies. Then the Eglinton
Crosstown light rail was built along it, and the construction ran for the
better part of a decade. Storefront access was disrupted, rents rose in
anticipation of the finished line, and many businesses closed.

The responses are as instructive as the damage. Toronto adopted a Cultural
District Plan for the area — its first — and residents formed a community land
trust to buy and hold property so that the culture has somewhere to remain. A
transit line is supposed to be a public good. It is, and it also transferred
value from tenants to landowners along the way, and both of those had to be
dealt with.

> [!note] Transit is not neutral
> Every rapid transit line raises land values near its stations. That is
> the point — density is what makes transit pay. It is also what displaces
> the tenants who were relying on the bus. Nothing about this is a
> surprise, which is why the interesting question is what the plan does
> about it before construction starts, not after.
