Canada exports enormous quantities of food and cannot feed everyone inside it.
Both are true, which tells you the food system's problem is not production. It
is distribution, cost, and distance.

## Insecurity is a distribution problem

In 2025, 24.0 per cent of people in Canada lived in a food-insecure household —
meaning they had trouble affording enough food. In Nunavut the figure was 56.4
per cent. Nothing about Nunavut's hunger is caused by a shortage of food in
Canada. It is caused by everything in a store there having arrived by air or on
one annual sealift, by incomes that do not match those prices, and by the
erosion of the alternative.

That alternative is country food — caribou, char, seal, berries — hunted and
shared. It suits the people eating it better than air-freighted groceries and
costs far less, and access to it has been squeezed by the price of equipment
and fuel, by wildlife rules, and by changing ice.

Communities are rebuilding it deliberately. In Taloyoak, Nunavut, the Spence
Bay Hunters and Trappers Association's Niqihaqut — "our food" — project is
building a local harvesting and processing operation so that country food can
be gathered, prepared and distributed within the community, tied to a proposed
Inuit protected area across the surrounding peninsula. It is a food policy and
a land policy at once, designed by the people who will eat the result.

> [!tip] "Food desert" is a measurement, not an insult
> The term means an area where people cannot readily reach a store
> selling fresh, affordable food — usually mapped by drawing walking or
> transit distance around supermarkets. It fits a downtown block with no
> grocer and a rural township twenty minutes from one. The map is half
> the answer; whether people can afford what is on the shelf is the rest.

## What the farm shows you

Southern Ontario's advantage is soil and season, and both are finite. The farm
we visit competes with land prices set by housing, with weather getting less
predictable, and with customers expecting a price set by growers in warmer
countries. Every strategy you will hear discussed — buying local, farmers'
markets, protecting prime agricultural land, cutting food waste — aims at one
of those three pressures. Ask which one, and you will know whether it addresses
the actual problem.

Guelph and Wellington County have gone furthest on waste, building a regional
circular food economy out of a federal Smart Cities prize: recovering edible
food before it is thrown out, and finding uses for what is left.
