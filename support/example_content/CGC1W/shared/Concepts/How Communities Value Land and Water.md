"Value" is doing two jobs in this course and they are easy to confuse.
One is a price. The other is what something means, what it is for, and
what is owed to it. A stretch of shoreline can be worth very little in
the first sense and be the most important place in a community in the
second.

Most environmental conflict in Canada is not a disagreement about facts.
The parties usually accept the same measurements. They are holding
different valuations of the same place, and no amount of extra data
settles that.

## Conservation and preservation are not synonyms

**Conservation** manages use so it can continue: a harvest limit, a
rotation, a licence. **Preservation** removes use: a protected area with
no extraction at all. Canada does both, sometimes on adjacent parcels,
and people who agree that a place matters can still disagree hard about
which approach it deserves.

## Responsibility as a form of value

For many First Nations, Métis, and Inuit communities, the relationship
with a particular place is framed as obligation rather than ownership —
duties to the water, the fish, and the generations who will need both. It
is expressed publicly as well as privately. For more than twenty years,
Anishinaabe women have organized water walks, carrying a copper vessel of
water along the full shoreline of lakes and rivers, to press the point
that water is owed something. Treaty and traditional territories carry
these obligations regardless of who currently holds the deed.

That is not the same as an environmental group's position, and it should
not be summarized as one. Flattening several distinct positions into
"people who care about the environment" is the most common way a student
analysis goes wrong here.
