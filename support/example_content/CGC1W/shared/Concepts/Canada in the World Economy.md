Canada is a wealthy country with a small population and an enormous
resource endowment. That combination produces an economy that is **open**
— a large share of what we make is sold abroad — and **concentrated**,
because for decades the great majority of it has gone to one customer.
Statistics Canada put the United States' share of Canadian merchandise
exports at 71.7 per cent in 2025, down from 75.9 per cent in 2024.

We export what our geology and climate gave us and what our factories
assemble: crude oil, natural gas, minerals and metals, potash, uranium,
lumber, wheat and canola, vehicles and parts. We import mostly finished
manufactured goods — electronics, machinery, consumer products — often
made in part from materials we sold someone else first.

Openness has an obvious upside. A country of roughly forty million people
cannot consume all the potash it produces, and selling the surplus pays
for everything we do not make here. The downside is equally plain: a
decision taken in another country's capital can reshape a Canadian town's
economy without anyone in that town being consulted.

## Geography does not stop at the border

The Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence are one navigation system and one
watershed, shared with the United States and managed by joint
institutions. Fish stocks, migratory birds, air quality, and river flows
do the same. Trade agreements — CUSMA is the current one covering Canada,
the United States, and Mexico — set the rules for goods; they do not
change the fact that the water is shared regardless.

There is also a labour geography inside the food you eat. A substantial
part of Canada's fruit and vegetable harvest is brought in by
international agricultural workers who come for a season under federal
programs, live near the farms, and go home. Their working conditions are
a live and contested question, and any honest account of Canadian
agriculture includes them.

> [!warning] Check the year, and check what is being counted
> Trade shares move, and they have moved noticeably in recent years. A
> figure you find online may be two years old, may cover goods only when
> you needed goods and services, and may be in Canadian or American
> dollars. Cite the source and the year every time — and when two sources
> disagree, the usual reason is that they are counting different things,
> not that one of them is lying.
