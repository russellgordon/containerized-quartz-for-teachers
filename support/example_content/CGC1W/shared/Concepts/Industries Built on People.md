Almost nobody you know digs anything out of the ground. Resource
extraction is central to Canada's exports and marginal to Canada's
employment, and holding both of those in your head at once is the point
of this page. The large majority of Canadians work in services.[^1]

Three families of industry run on people rather than deposits.

**Knowledge-based industries** sell what someone worked out: software,
engineering, scientific research, education, journalism, design. Their
main input is trained people, and their main cost is paying them.

**Service industries** do something for someone: health care, banking,
insurance, retail, logistics, transportation, the trades. This is the
largest group by far, and it includes most of the jobs that keep a town
functioning.

**Cultural industries** produce film, television, music, publishing,
performance, and sport. Canada supports these deliberately through
funding and content rules, on the argument that a small population beside
a very large neighbour will otherwise consume mostly imported culture.

## Why this changes the map

A mine must be at the ore body. A knowledge industry has no such anchor,
so it clusters instead — beside universities, beside other firms doing
similar work, and in places where the people it needs are willing to
live. Waterloo Region grew a large software industry around its
universities. Montréal built aerospace and, later, visual effects and
game studios. Vancouver is one of North America's busiest film production
centres. Halifax has concentrated ocean technology around its port,
navy, and marine research.

None of those locations was determined by geology. They were determined
by who was already there, which is a much less stable foundation. A mine
lasts until the ore runs out. A cluster lasts until the people leave.

That is the real vulnerability of a human-capital industry, and it is why
provinces compete on housing, transit, and schools rather than on mineral
rights. When you assess a community's economic future, count the
graduates as carefully as you count the tonnes.

The jobs themselves, and the geographic skills inside them, are
[[Careers in Geography]]. Why any industry sits where it does is
[[Why Industries Locate Where They Do]].

[^1]: Statistics Canada's Labour Force Survey has put services-producing
    industries at close to four in five Canadian jobs for many years
    running. The exact percentage moves a little each release; the shape
    of the answer does not.
