Stand in the woodlot in a light rain and almost nothing reaches you. The
canopy holds some of it, the leaf litter soaks up more, and what gets
through moves down into the soil among the roots. Stand in a parking lot
in the same rain and every drop is heading for the storm drain within
seconds.

That is the whole idea. Human activity mostly does not create new
physical processes. It speeds existing ones up, slows them down, or moves
them somewhere else.

## The four moves

**Sealing the surface.** Roofs, roads, and parking lots turn rain that
would have soaked in into runoff. The water arrives at the creek faster
and all at once, so the creek rises higher, cuts its banks harder, and
falls again quickly. The flood is downstream of the pavement, not under
it.

**Removing cover.** Roots hold soil and slow water. Take the trees off a
slope and the slope becomes more likely to fail. Take the vegetation off
a field and wind and rain start removing the topsoil, which took
centuries to build.

**Redirecting water.** Draining a wetland removes the sponge that used to
absorb a storm and release it slowly. Damming a river holds sediment
behind the dam, so the shoreline downstream is starved of the material
that used to replace what waves take away.

**Fragmenting ground.** Roads, seismic lines, and rail cuts do not remove
much area, but they cut habitat into pieces and give predators easy
straight lines to travel. The land looks nearly intact from the air and
does not function as if it were.

> [!note] The effect and the cause are rarely in the same place
> This is why blame is hard to assign and why municipalities argue about
> it. A subdivision built at the top of a watershed can raise flood risk
> for a neighbourhood several kilometres downstream that had no say in
> the approval. When you analyze a proposal, always ask where the water
> goes after it leaves the site.

The moves also run in reverse. Wetlands can be rebuilt, streams
re-meandered, dams removed, and slopes replanted, and the physical
processes respond. Restoration is slower and more expensive than not
damaging the place in the first place, which is an argument you will use
more than once.

You measured some of this yourself in [[The Woodlot Study]]. What to do
about it, when a community wants the land anyway, is
[[Building Where Nature Pushes Back]].
