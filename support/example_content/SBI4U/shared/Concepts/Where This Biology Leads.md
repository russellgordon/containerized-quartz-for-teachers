Grade 12 biology is a prerequisite course, which means most people meet
it on the way to somewhere. Here is where it actually goes, and who
built the ideas you are being taught.

## The work this course prepares you for

| Field | What the work is | What it needs from this course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medicine and dentistry | Diagnosis and treatment, after a long training | Physiology, homeostasis, and the ability to reason from evidence |
| Nursing and paramedicine | Assessment and intervention, immediately | Systems, feedback, and measurement under pressure |
| Pharmacology | What a molecule does in a body, and at what dose | Enzymes, membranes, metabolism |
| Biotechnology | Engineering organisms and their products | Molecular genetics, and the ethics that follow it |
| Genetic counselling | Explaining risk to families making decisions | Inheritance, plus communication that survives fear |
| Public health and epidemiology | Populations, not patients | Population dynamics and data handling |
| Research science | Asking a question nobody has answered | Everything, plus tolerance for being wrong |
| Ecology and conservation | Field data, models, and management advice | Population dynamics, sampling, statistics |
| Agriculture and food science | Yield, safety, and sustainability | Metabolism, genetics, and population thinking |
| Laboratory technology | Running the tests everything else depends on | Precision, protocol, and honest recording |

Two honest notes. Several of these are college diplomas or
apprenticeship-style routes rather than long degrees, and the people in
them are not doing lesser work. And every one of these jobs involves more
writing and more statistics than students expect — which is why the
reference pages in this course exist and are marked.

## Whose shoulders this stands on

**Maud Menten** was born in Port Lambton, Ontario, graduated in medicine
from the University of Toronto in 1907, and was among the first Canadian
women to earn a medical doctorate. In 1913, working with Leonor
Michaelis in Berlin, she co-authored the paper that produced the
**Michaelis–Menten equation** — the relationship between substrate
concentration and reaction rate that you will use in
[[Proteins and Enzymes]] and in your own enzyme investigation.
She spent most of her career as a pathologist at Pittsburgh, publishing
on blood sugar, haemoglobin, and kidney function.

That equation is a century old and still on the first page of every
enzyme kinetics course in the world. It is worth knowing that it has a
Canadian name on it, and that the person who put it there had to leave
the country to do the work.

**Lap-Chee Tsui's** team at Toronto's Hospital for Sick Children
identified the cystic fibrosis gene in 1989 — the first gene found by
mapping its position rather than by knowing its protein, which is the
method that made the Human Genome Project plausible.

> [!note]- Why this is in a biology course rather than a careers talk
> Because the ideas have histories, and the histories say something
> about who gets to do science. Menten did her most cited work abroad,
> partly because Canadian universities of the time did not readily
> employ women in research. Knowing that is part of understanding how
> the field arrived at where it is.

## What to do about it

If any row of that table interests you, the useful next step is not more
reading. It is one conversation with somebody doing the job, and one
piece of hands-on experience — a hospital volunteer shift, a lab
placement, a summer with a conservation authority. Both are more
persuasive on an application than a mark, and far more useful to you in
deciding.
