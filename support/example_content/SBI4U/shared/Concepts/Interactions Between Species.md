## Predator–prey cycles

The Canadian lynx and snowshoe hare record, from Hudson's Bay Company fur
returns, is the textbook case: roughly ten-year oscillations with the
predator lagging the prey. It is also more complicated than the textbook
version — food supply and disease matter too — and saying so is better
science than the clean story.

## Competitive exclusion

Two species cannot occupy exactly the same niche indefinitely; one
outcompetes the other or they diverge. Resource partitioning — warblers
feeding at different heights in the same tree — is what divergence looks
like.

## Why introduced species are so destructive

They arrive without the predators, parasites, and competitors that limited
them at home. Zebra mussels in the Great Lakes are the local example, and
their population curve is exponential until it is not.


## Pull one thread and the whole thing moves

A change in one population does not stay in that population. The routes
it travels are predictable, even when the size of the effect is not:

- **Down the food chain.** Remove a predator and its prey increases,
  which suppresses whatever the prey eats. Sea otters, urchins, and kelp
  forests are the standard case, and the kelp is three steps from the
  otter.
- **Up the food chain.** A collapse at the bottom starves everything
  above it, which is why a plankton failure is a fisheries problem.
- **Sideways, through competition.** One species released from pressure
  outcompetes its neighbours for the same resource.
- **Through the habitat itself.** Beavers, corals, and forest trees
  build the structure other species live in; losing them removes homes
  rather than food.

An **invasive species** is this argument run at speed: zebra mussels
filtering a lake clearer than its own food web can tolerate, or emerald
ash borer removing a canopy tree and with it the shade, the leaf litter,
and the insects that depended on both.

Two lessons follow. Effects are often **delayed** — the response can
take generations, so a system can look fine while it is already
committed. And they are frequently **non-linear**: nothing, nothing,
nothing, then collapse. Which is why monitoring a single population,
which is what [[Population Study]] asks you to do, is worth so much more
than it first appears.
