Grade 12 biology asks you to do two things a survey course does not: pose
a question precise enough to answer, and read the literature that has
already tried. Both are learnable, and both are marked.

## A question you can actually answer

Most student investigations fail at the question rather than at the
bench. "Does temperature affect enzymes?" cannot fail and cannot
succeed — it has no measurable answer. Tighten it in three moves:

1. **Name the variable you will change**, and its levels. Temperature →
   10, 20, 30, 40, 50 °C.
2. **Name what you will measure**, and how. "Activity" → volume of
   oxygen produced in 60 seconds.
3. **Name what stays fixed.** Enzyme concentration, substrate
   concentration, pH, volume.

> "How does temperature (10–50 °C) affect the rate of oxygen production
> by catalase acting on 3% hydrogen peroxide, with concentration and pH
> held constant?"

That question tells you what to do on Monday. It also tells a reader
exactly what your answer covers, which is the same thing.

**A hypothesis is not a guess.** It is a prediction with a mechanism
attached: *rate will rise to about 40 °C and fall sharply after, because
the active site's shape depends on bonds that heat disrupts.* The
mechanism is what makes it testable and what makes a surprising result
interesting rather than annoying.

## Reading what has already been done

| Where | Good for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Review articles | The state of a field, with references worth following | A review is somebody's summary; go to the primary work for detail |
| Primary research papers | What was actually measured, and how | Sample sizes, controls, and whether the conclusion matches the data |
| Government and agency data | Population, health, and environmental figures | Definitions change between years |
| Textbooks | Settled explanation | Silent about anything unresolved |
| News coverage | What is being claimed publicly | The gap between the paper and the headline is where the errors live |

Your school or public library gives you database access from home. Read
a paper in this order: abstract, then discussion, then methods, then
results. The methods are where a paper is judged, and they make far more
sense once you know what the authors think they found.

## Judging a source

Five questions, in order, and the first failure ends it: **who wrote it,
what is the evidence, when, who benefits, and does anyone independent
agree?**

The last one catches most bad research: three articles can look like
three sources and trace back to one press release. Follow each claim to
its origin before you count it as corroboration.

Bias is not the same as wrong. A company's study of its own product can
be sound and still needs independent confirmation; an advocacy group's
report can be excellent evidence. Note the interest, look for the
independent check, and say in your writing what you found.

## Recording as you go

From the first source, keep: author or organisation, date, title, where
you found it, and **one sentence in your own words**. That sentence
prevents accidental copying later, because you have already put the idea
in your own language — and it is what turns a bibliography into notes
you can actually write from.

Cite where you use a claim, not only at the end. "A 2021 review in
*Nature Reviews Genetics* found…" tells the reader what your sentence
rests on at the moment they need to know.
