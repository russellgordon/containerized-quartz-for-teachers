## In order of speed

1. **Ask in class, at the time.** If you are confused, so are three
   other people who have not said anything yet.
2. **Check the concept page.** Most "I don't get it" moments are
   answered there, and the backlinks show you which class we did it in.
3. **Work through [[Getting Unstuck]].** It is a twenty-minute routine,
   and it resolves a good share of what looks unresolvable at first —
   particularly if the real problem is that the units have not been
   written down.
4. **Come to a help session.** Times are on [[Help Sessions]]. Drop in;
   no appointment, no warning needed.
5. **Email me.** Say what you tried. It makes the answer far more useful
   and it usually saves an entire round trip.

## Bring the specific thing, and bring the numbers

One calculation that comes out wrong beats "the mole". One step of a
titration where you lose the thread beats "solutions". The narrower the
question, the faster and better the answer — and narrowing it yourself
often solves it before you get to me.

Bring the working, not a summary of the working. "I get 0.0421 and the
answer is 0.0842" tells me in one glance that a factor of two went
missing, which is a thirty-second conversation. "I did it wrong" is a
ten-minute one.

## Help with a result, not just a method

The most useful conversation of the week is often somebody arriving with
data that did not do what they expected — a yield above 100%, three
trials that agree beautifully with each other and not with anybody else,
a precipitate that will not settle. That is not a failure to be fixed
quietly before anyone sees it. It is the interesting case, and it is
exactly what [[Mistakes Are Data]] is about.

Bring the notebook with the raw readings in it. A calculated answer on
its own can only be checked for arithmetic; the readings can be checked
for chemistry.

> [!note] Ask early
> Chemistry builds, and this course builds harder than last year's did.
> A shaky grip on writing formulas in Unit 1 becomes a wall when you
> need a balanced equation for stoichiometry in Unit 3, and by then the
> gap is four weeks wide instead of one. Nobody has ever irritated me by
> asking a question too early.

If the difficulty is not the chemistry — a week when things are hard for
other reasons — say so, in whatever words you like. We will sort out the
work; that part is always solvable.
