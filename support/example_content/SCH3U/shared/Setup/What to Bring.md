Every class:

- [ ] Lab notebook — bound, not loose paper, not a binder
- [ ] Pen for writing, and a sharp pencil and ruler for graphs
- [ ] Scientific calculator — one that does scientific notation and
      logarithms. A phone will not be permitted on tests
- [ ] The periodic table and data sheet issued in this room
- [ ] Charged laptop
- [ ] Your [[Chemistry Journal]], if you keep it separately

## About the calculator

Get one you can use without thinking, and use the same one all
semester. You will spend the whole of Unit 3 moving between numbers like
$6.022 \times 10^{23}$ and numbers like 0.0374, and a calculator you
are fighting with is a calculator that will lose you an exponent.

Two things to find on yours in the first week: the key that enters a
power of ten directly, and how many digits it shows before it switches
to scientific notation. Both will matter more than you expect.

## Lab days

Check the class page the night before. If it links an investigation, it
is a lab day, and the following are not negotiable:

- [ ] Closed-toe shoes. You will be sent out, and there is nothing I can
      do about it
- [ ] Long hair tied back before you come in, not at the bench
- [ ] Sleeves that can be rolled or fastened; nothing dangling from
      wrists or neck
- [ ] Bags at the front of the room, not underfoot
- [ ] The investigation page read, not skimmed — including where the
      waste goes

> [!note] Why the shoe rule has no exceptions
> Everything that goes wrong in a lab goes down. Broken glass, hot
> liquid, and corrosive solution all end up on the floor, and the floor
> is where your feet are. This is a school board requirement and not a
> preference of mine, so please do not spend a lab period negotiating it
> with me.

## About the notebook

Bound, in ink, in order, with nothing torn out. That combination is what
turns a pile of notes into a **record**: readings written as they happen
rather than reconstructed afterwards, mistakes struck through with a
single line and initialled rather than scribbled out, and the odd
observation kept even when you cannot explain it.

Two habits specific to this course. Write the **raw reading** as the
instrument gave it, before any arithmetic — a calculated answer with no
reading behind it cannot be rechecked. And write the resolution of each
instrument once at the top of the page, so that three weeks later you
can still say what your numbers were worth. [[Measuring Well]] explains
why both of those pay off.

The habit is the same at every level of science. Research and industry
notebooks settle patent disputes and safety investigations, and they do
it on exactly these properties — contemporaneous, unaltered, honest.
