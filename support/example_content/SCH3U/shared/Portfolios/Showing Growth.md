Mid-semester, stop adding entries for one sitting and write one that
looks backwards.

## Do this

1. Re-read [[Your First Entry]], including the sentence about how you
   think chemistry knows things.
2. Find an early piece of work and a recent one on the **same skill** —
   two conclusions, two sets of limitations, two attempts to say what
   your numbers are worth.
3. Put them side by side and name three specific differences. Quote
   yourself in both directions.

Same skill is the hard part. A first bond-type lab and a titration are
different jobs, and comparing them shows nothing except that the
titration came later. Comparing two *conclusions* — the paragraph where
you say what you found and how much you trust it — is a fair comparison,
because you wrote that paragraph both times.

## Not this

> "I have improved a lot and learned many things. My labs are much
> better than they were at the start and I understand the mole now."

Nothing in that paragraph could be checked, argued with, or shown to be
wrong. A claim nobody could contradict is not evidence of growth — it is
a sentence about wanting to have grown.

## This

> My first conclusion, from [[Testing for Bond Type]], was: "Sample C
> was ionic because it conducted." One sentence, no numbers, and
> "conducted" is doing all the work — I did not say what the reading
> was, what the blank was, or how big a reading has to be before it
> counts.
>
> My conclusion from the precipitation lab says: "We calculated that
> 1.42 g of calcium carbonate should form from the limiting reagent, and
> recovered 1.31 g, a yield of 92%. Weighing by difference on a balance
> that reads to 0.01 g puts about ±0.02 g on the recovered mass, so the
> yield is 92% give or take roughly 2 — meaning 92% and 94% are the same
> result and 92% and 82% are not. Precipitate left on the filter paper
> and in the beaker can only lower the recovered mass, so 92% is a floor
> rather than an estimate that could err either way."
>
> Three differences. The second one gives numbers with units instead of
> a verb. It states how well the number is known, so a reader can tell
> which comparisons the data can support. And it says which
> **direction** the main limitation pushed, which the first one could
> not have done because it had no direction in it to push.
>
> The change in my head: I used to think the conclusion was where you
> announce the result. I now think it is where you say what the result
> is worth, and those turn out to be different paragraphs.

## What I am reading for

That last line. Three differences in the work, and then one sentence
about what changed in your thinking to produce them. The second is worth
more than the first, and it is also the part people leave out.

> [!question] A useful prompt if you are stuck
> What do you now do automatically that you had to be told to do in the
> first month? Writing the units before the number. Checking a
> difference against the instrument's resolution before calling it a
> difference. Recording the raw reading as well as the answer. Automatic
> is exactly the evidence — you stopped noticing you were doing it.
