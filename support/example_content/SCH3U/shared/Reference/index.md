These are the pages you keep open during a problem set, not pages you
read once.

Everything else on this site is written to be understood — a
[[Concepts/index|concept page]] wants you to follow an argument, an
investigation wants you to make a decision. These five want nothing. They
are lookup sheets. You will use them dozens of times this semester and
you are not expected to work through them from top to bottom even once.

| Page | Open it when |
| --- | --- |
| [[Polyatomic Ions]] | you are writing a formula and need a charge |
| [[Solubility Rules]] | you need to know whether a product falls out as a solid |
| [[The Activity Series]] | you need to know whether a single displacement happens at all |
| [[Significant Figures and Units]] | you have an answer and are deciding how to write it |
| [[Naming Rules at a Glance]] | you have a formula and need its name, or the reverse |

## Two of them you should eventually stop needing

[[Polyatomic Ions]] and [[Naming Rules at a Glance]] are the ones to work
towards not opening. About twenty ions and one set of naming rules carry
almost every question you will be asked, and every quantitative page from
[[The Mole]] onwards assumes you can write a correct formula without
stopping to think. Reaching for the sheet three times in a calculation
does not just cost time — it breaks the thread of the problem you were
actually solving. Use [[Naming Practice]] until the sheet is a check
rather than a source.

[[Solubility Rules]] and [[The Activity Series]] are different. Those
are **measured data**, not rules you could have derived, and practising
chemists look them up too. There is no virtue in memorising a solubility
table, and a fair assessment will give it to you.

> [!tip] Check against the booklet you will be given
> Data tables differ slightly between sources — the boundary between
> "slightly soluble" and "insoluble", the exact order of two adjacent
> metals, whether STP means 100 kPa or 101.325 kPa. None of these pages
> can be authoritative over the data booklet you are handed for an
> assessment. Compare them early in the semester so that no difference
> is a surprise later, and if you find one, bring it up — spotting it is
> a good sign rather than a nuisance.
