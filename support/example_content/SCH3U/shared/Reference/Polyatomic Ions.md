A polyatomic ion is a group of atoms covalently bonded to each other that
carries an overall charge and travels through reactions as a single unit.
Sulfate goes into the beaker as $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ and comes out as
$\text{SO}_4^{2-}$.

Use this sheet while you are writing formulas. The reasoning behind it is
in [[Naming and Formulas]].

Ammonium is the only common positive polyatomic ion, and it is the only
cation in this course that is not a metal. It behaves like a group 1
metal ion in every formula you will write.

## Four patterns that cut the memorising in half

The tables above look like forty separate facts. They are closer to
twenty facts and four rules.

**-ate and -ite differ by one oxygen, and the charge stays the same.**
Sulfate is $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ and sulfite is $\text{SO}_3^{2-}$. Nitrate
is $\text{NO}_3^-$ and nitrite is $\text{NO}_2^-$. Learn the *-ate*
version of each and the *-ite* follows.

**per- and hypo- extend the ladder at both ends.** With chlorine and
oxygen there are four steps, and the pattern is identical for bromine and
iodine:

| Name | Formula | Oxygens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Perchlorate | $\text{ClO}_4^-$ | one more than chlorate |
| Chlorate | $\text{ClO}_3^-$ | the reference point |
| Chlorite | $\text{ClO}_2^-$ | one fewer than chlorate |
| Hypochlorite | $\text{ClO}^-$ | one fewer than chlorite |

Hypochlorite is the active ingredient in household bleach, which is why
this ladder is worth knowing rather than merely learning.

**Adding a hydrogen adds an H and cancels one negative charge.** A
hydrogen ion attaching to a polyatomic ion makes the charge less
negative by exactly one:

$$\text{PO}_4^{3-} \rightarrow \text{HPO}_4^{2-} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-$$

Carbonate to hydrogen carbonate works the same way, and it is why baking
soda, $\text{NaHCO}_3$, needs only one sodium ion while washing soda,
$\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$, needs two.

**thio- means an oxygen has been swapped for a sulfur.** Sulfate,
$\text{SO}_4^{2-}$, becomes thiosulfate, $\text{S}_2\text{O}_3^{2-}$ —
same charge, one oxygen replaced.

> [!note] Brackets are not optional
> When a formula needs more than one of a polyatomic ion, the whole ion
> goes inside brackets with the subscript outside. Calcium nitrate is
> $\text{Ca(NO}_3\text{)}_2$ — one calcium ion, two nitrate ions, and
> therefore two nitrogen atoms and six oxygen atoms. Writing
> $\text{CaNO}_{32}$ is a different and non-existent substance, and the
> mistake propagates silently into every molar mass you calculate
> afterwards.

## The eight to learn first

If you memorise nothing else, memorise these. They account for most of
the questions in [[Naming Practice]] and nearly every precipitation
reaction in [[Solubility Rules]].

$\text{OH}^-$ · $\text{NO}_3^-$ · $\text{HCO}_3^-$ ·
$\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ · $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ · $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ ·
$\text{PO}_4^{3-}$ · $\text{NH}_4^+$
