Physics labs look tamer than chemistry labs, and that is exactly why
people get hurt in them. Nothing here smells dangerous. A 2 kg mass
dropped on a foot, a stretched spring released, or a power supply turned
up "just to see" will still ruin your week.

## Before every investigation

- [ ] Read the whole procedure before touching anything.
- [ ] Know where the power cut-off, the first-aid kit, and the eyewash are.
- [ ] Bags under the bench, not in the aisle.
- [ ] Long hair tied back near anything that spins.

## The four that matter most here

| Hazard | Where it shows up | What we do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Falling mass | Pulleys, free fall, energy labs | Nothing heavier than 1 kg above bench height; feet clear |
| Stored elastic energy | Springs, elastics, launchers | Never aim along a bench; release slowly |
| Heat | Calorimetry, hot plates | Assume glass and metal are hot; tongs, not fingers |
| Electricity | Circuits, motors | Low voltage only; power off while you rewire |

## Eye protection

Goggles for anything that can launch, snap, or shatter — springs,
elastics, glass tubing, and every heat investigation. If you are unsure,
put them on; nobody has ever been sent to the office for wearing goggles.

## When something goes wrong

Tell me immediately, even if it seems small, and even if it was your
fault. A spill nobody mentions is how the next class gets hurt.

> [!warning] The rule that has no exceptions
> If an investigation is not on the page for that day, we are not doing
> it. Improvised experiments are how good students end up in the office —
> bring me the idea and we will run it properly next class.
