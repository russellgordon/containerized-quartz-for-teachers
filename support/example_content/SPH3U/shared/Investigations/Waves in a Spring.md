> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Time:** half a class, on the floor of the lab. **Pairs.** Nothing to
> hand in; the observations are used all unit.

A long spring stretched between two people is the cheapest wave laboratory
there is, and almost everything in [[Wave Properties]] can be seen in it
directly.

## Safety

Never let go of a stretched spring. Stretch it no more than about three
times its natural length, and keep the floor clear.

## What to try

1. **A single pulse.** Flick sideways once. Watch it travel, reflect off
   your partner's fixed end, and come back INVERTED. Why inverted?
2. **Change the tension.** Stretch further and send another pulse. Does it
   travel faster? What does that say about what sets wave speed?
3. **Change the pulse size.** A bigger flick. Does a bigger pulse travel
   faster? (Predict before you try.)
4. **Two pulses at once.** One from each end, same side, then opposite
   sides. Watch what happens when they meet, and what happens after.
5. **Continuous waves.** Shake steadily. Increase your rate. Watch the
   wavelength shorten while the speed stays put.
6. **A standing wave.** Adjust your rate until the pattern stands still.
   Count the nodes. Then find the next frequency that works.

## The observation to carry forward

Steps 2 and 3 together are the whole argument that wave speed belongs to
the medium, not the source — the idea [[Sound Waves]] leans on, and the
one that makes $v = f\lambda$ useful rather than trivial.
