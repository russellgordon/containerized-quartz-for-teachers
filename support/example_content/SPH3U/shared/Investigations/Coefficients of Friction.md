> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Time:** one class. **Pairs.** Bring one shoe you are willing to drag
> across a bench.

$\mu$ is not a property of one material. It is a property of a PAIR of
surfaces, and this is where that stops being a sentence and becomes a
number you measured.

## Safety

Nothing heavy near the bench edge. If you use a force sensor, do not yank
it.

## Method

Two ways, and every group does both:

**A — The tilt method.** Raise one end of a board until the object just
begins to slide. At that angle, $\tan\theta = \mu_s$. No force sensor
needed, and it measures STATIC friction — see [[Inclined Planes]].

**B — The drag method.** Pull the object along a level surface at constant
speed with a force sensor or spring scale. At constant speed the applied
force equals the friction force, so $\mu_k = F_f / F_N$. This measures
KINETIC friction.

## What to record

For at least three surface pairs (shoe on tile, wood on wood, wood on
sandpaper): the tilt angle, the drag force, the normal force, and both
coefficients.

## Analysis

1. Is $\mu_s$ larger than $\mu_k$ for every pair you tested? Should it
   be?
2. Double the normal force by stacking a mass on top and repeat one pair.
   Does $\mu$ change? Should it?
3. Which of your two methods was more repeatable, and why do you think so?
