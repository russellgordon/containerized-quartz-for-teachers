> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Time:** two classes — one to plan and run, one to analyse. **Pairs.**
> You choose the launcher, the measurements, and the question.

[[Projectile Motion]] claims that the horizontal and vertical motions of a
projectile are independent. This is where you find out whether the claim
survives contact with a ball, a ramp, and a tape measure.

## Safety

Launch across the room, never toward a person or a window. Everybody in
the flight path wears eye protection. Nothing heavier than a marble
leaves the bench.

## Your question, not mine

Pick ONE relationship to investigate, and write it as a question with a
measurable answer:

- How does launch **height** affect the range of a horizontally launched
  ball?
- How does launch **speed** affect the range?
- How does launch **angle** affect the range, at a fixed speed?
- Does the ball's **mass** affect the range at all? (Predict first. This
  one is more interesting than it sounds.)

Then write a **prediction with a mechanism attached**: not "the range
will increase", but "the range will be proportional to the square root of
the height, because the time of flight comes from the vertical drop and
the horizontal speed is unchanged." A prediction with a reason behind it
is a hypothesis; one without is a guess.

## Choosing your equipment

Nobody is going to hand you a kit. Look at what is on the bench and
choose:

| To measure | Options in this room | What each costs you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Launch speed | Photogate, video at 240 fps, or calculate from a ramp height | Photogate is precise but fiddly; video is forgiving but slower |
| Range | Tape measure and carbon paper, or a sand tray | Carbon paper marks the exact landing point; sand is easier to reset |
| Launch angle | Protractor on the launcher, or a phone level app | The app is more precise than the eye by a factor of about five |
| Height | Metre stick to the bottom of the ball, not the top of the ramp | The wrong reference point is the most common error here |

Write down **which you chose and why** — that judgement is part of what
this investigation assesses.

## Method

1. Fix everything except your one variable. Say in writing what you fixed
   and how.
2. Take **five** trials at each of at least **five** values. One trial is
   an anecdote.
3. Record the spread, not just the mean. Your uncertainty comes from the
   spread — see [[Uncertainty and Error]].

## Analysis

1. Graph your relationship, and **linearise it** if it is not already
   straight — plot range against $\sqrt{h}$ rather than against $h$, and
   say why that is the right axis.
2. Predict, from theory alone, what the slope should be. Then compare it
   with the slope you measured, as a percentage difference.
3. If they disagree by more than your uncertainty, say what would explain
   it. Air resistance and a mis-measured launch height are both real
   candidates; "human error" is not an explanation of anything.

> [!tip] The comparison that makes this investigation worth doing
> Run one trial from the same height with a ball you simply *drop*, and
> time it. If the fall time matches the flight time of your launched
> ball, you have demonstrated the independence of the two directions
> yourself, on a bench, in about four minutes.
