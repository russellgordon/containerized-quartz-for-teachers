When acceleration is constant, you do not need a graph for every question.
Four equations connect the five quantities — $v_1$, $v_2$, $a$,
$\Delta d$, $\Delta t$ — and each one leaves out exactly one of them.

## The four

$$\Delta d = \left(\frac{v_1 + v_2}{2}\right)\Delta t \qquad \text{(no } a\text{)}$$

$$v_2 = v_1 + a\Delta t \qquad \text{(no } \Delta d\text{)}$$

$$\Delta d = v_1\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}a(\Delta t)^2 \qquad \text{(no } v_2\text{)}$$

$$v_2^2 = v_1^2 + 2a\Delta d \qquad \text{(no } \Delta t\text{)}$$

## How to choose one

List what you know and what you want. Whatever quantity is neither known
nor wanted is the one to leave out — and that names your equation. This is
faster and far more reliable than hunting for a familiar-looking formula.

## Where they come from

They are not handed down. The first is the definition of average velocity
for constant acceleration; the rest follow from a velocity–time graph,
where the area under a straight line is a rectangle plus a triangle. We
derive them in class rather than memorise them, which is also what the
curriculum asks for.

> [!warning] The condition everyone forgets
> Constant acceleration. All four equations are silent about what happens
> if $a$ changes mid-problem — and in the real world it usually does.
