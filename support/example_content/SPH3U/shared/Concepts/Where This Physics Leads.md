Grade 11 physics is a prerequisite course, which means most people meet it
on the way to somewhere else. Here is where it actually goes, and some of
the people who built the ideas you are being taught.

## The work this course prepares you for

| Field | What the work is | What it needs from this course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Engineering (any branch) | Making something that has to not fail | Forces, energy, circuits — all of it, plus the habit of stating assumptions |
| Skilled trades — electrician, millwright, powerline technician | Building and maintaining what engineers draw | Circuits, series and parallel, power, and safety that is not negotiable |
| Medical physics and radiation therapy | Aiming radiation at a tumour and not at anything else | Energy, nuclear physics, and uncertainty you can defend |
| Diagnostic imaging (MRI, ultrasound) | Making the inside of a body visible | Waves, resonance, magnetism |
| Acoustic consulting | Designing rooms and reducing noise | Everything in the waves unit, plus the ability to explain it to a client |
| Aerospace and automotive | Motion, forces, and materials, at scale | Kinematics, Newton's laws, projectile motion |
| Energy — nuclear, hydro, wind, grid operations | Keeping a province's lights on | Energy transformations, efficiency, thermodynamics |
| Geophysics and meteorology | Reading the Earth and the atmosphere | Waves, energy transfer, data analysis |
| Physics research | Asking a question nobody has answered yet | Everything, plus tolerance for being wrong in public |
| Teaching | Explaining it to the next people | The parts you find hardest now are the ones you will teach best |

Two honest notes. Several of those rows are apprenticeships or college
programmes rather than long degrees, and the people in them are not doing
lesser work — a powerline technician's mistake and a physicist's mistake
do not carry the same consequences. And nearly every job on that list
involves more writing than students expect, which is why
[[Writing About Physics]] exists and is marked.

## Whose shoulders this stands on

**Harriet Brooks** (1876–1933), born in Exeter, Ontario, was the first
Canadian woman to work in nuclear physics. Working with Ernest Rutherford
at McGill, she showed that a radioactive element decays into a *different*
element, and she was first to observe the recoil of a radioactive atom as
it emits a particle. She left research in her early thirties, under the
convention of the day that a married woman gave up her position — which
is part of the story of the field as much as her results are.

**Richard E. Taylor** (1929–2018), from Medicine Hat, Alberta, shared the
1990 Nobel Prize in Physics for the experiments that fired electrons into
protons and found that protons are not simple — the first direct evidence
for quarks.

**Willard Boyle** (1924–2011), raised in Amherst, Nova Scotia, shared the
2009 Nobel Prize for co-inventing the charge-coupled device. The camera in
your phone descends from that sketch; so does the Hubble Space Telescope's.

**Donna Strickland**, at the University of Waterloo, shared the 2018 Nobel
Prize for chirped pulse amplification — a method she developed as a
doctoral student for making laser pulses enormously intense without
destroying the equipment. It is the technique behind laser eye surgery.

**Art McDonald**, at Queen's University, shared the 2015 Nobel Prize for
showing that neutrinos change type as they travel, and therefore have
mass. The measurement was made two kilometres underground in a working
nickel mine near Sudbury, because that much rock was the only way to
screen out everything that was not a neutrino.

> [!warning] The one to learn from rather than admire
> **Louis Slotin**, born in Winnipeg, died in 1946 of radiation exposure
> after a screwdriver slipped during a criticality demonstration he was
> performing by hand — a procedure colleagues had already called
> reckless. Every rule on [[Safety in the Lab]] is written the way it is
> because somebody found out the hard way. Competence is not a substitute
> for procedure, and neither is confidence.

## What to do about it

If any row of that table interests you, the useful next step is not more
reading. It is one conversation with somebody doing the job, and one piece
of hands-on experience — a co-op placement, a shadow day, a summer at a
trades camp. Both tell you more in an afternoon than a year of research,
and both are more persuasive on an application than a mark.
