Two things in this course are not about physics and are marked anyway:
posing a question precise enough to answer, and finding out what is
already known without being fooled. Both are learnable.

## A question you can actually answer

Most investigations fail at the question rather than at the bench. "Does
mass affect motion?" cannot fail and cannot succeed — there is no
measurement in it. Tighten it in three moves:

1. **Name what you will change**, and its levels. Launch height → 0.40,
   0.60, 0.80, 1.00, 1.20 m.
2. **Name what you will measure**, and how. "Range" → horizontal distance
   from the launch point to the carbon-paper mark, in metres.
3. **Name what stays fixed.** Launch speed, ball, surface, angle.

> "How does launch height (0.40–1.20 m) affect the horizontal range of a
> ball launched horizontally at a fixed speed?"

That question tells you what to do on Monday, and it tells a reader
exactly what your answer covers.

**A hypothesis is not a guess.** It is a prediction with a mechanism
attached: *range will be proportional to the square root of the height,
because the time of flight comes from the vertical drop while the
horizontal speed is unchanged.* The mechanism makes it testable, and makes
a surprising result interesting rather than annoying.

## Finding out what is already known

| Where | Good for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Textbooks and reference tables | Settled values — $g$, specific heats, resistivities | Silent about anything unresolved, and sometimes about the conditions a value assumes |
| Government and agency data | Energy production, emissions, road safety figures | Definitions change between years; check the footnotes |
| Engineering standards and manufacturer data | What a real device actually does | Written to sell or to comply, not to explain |
| University and research sites | Current work, explained by the people doing it | The gap between a press release and the paper it describes |
| News coverage | What is being claimed publicly | Headlines routinely overstate; follow the link to the source |
| Video and forums | An intuition you were missing | No review of any kind — verify anything you would put in a report |

Your school or public library gives you database access from home, and a
librarian will find things faster than you will. Ask.

## Judging a source

Five questions, in order, and the first failure ends it: **who wrote it,
what is the evidence, when, who benefits, and does anyone independent
agree?**

That last one catches most bad claims: three articles can look like three
sources and trace back to one press release. Follow each claim to its
origin before you count it as corroboration.

Bias is not the same as wrong. A manufacturer's efficiency figures can be
accurate and still need an independent check; an advocacy group's report
can be excellent evidence. Note the interest, look for the independent
check, and say in your writing what you found.

> [!warning] Numbers travel further than their conditions
> "LEDs are 90% efficient" is meaningless without a stated wattage,
> colour temperature, and driver. Whenever you copy a number, copy the
> conditions with it — and give the units. Half the wrong answers in this
> subject are right numbers under conditions that did not apply.

## Recording as you go

From the first source, keep: author or organisation, date, title, where
you found it, and **one sentence in your own words**. That sentence
prevents accidental copying later, because you have already put the idea
in your own language, and it turns a bibliography into notes you can
actually write from.

Cite where you use a claim, not only at the end. "Ontario's grid was 51%
nuclear in 2023, per the IESO's year-end data…" tells a reader what your
sentence rests on at the moment they need to know.
