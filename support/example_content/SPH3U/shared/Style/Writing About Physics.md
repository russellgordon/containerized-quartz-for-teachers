Physics writing is not English writing with numbers in it. It has its own
moves, and they are learnable.

## Say what you measured, then what it means

A lab conclusion that starts "In this lab we learned about acceleration"
has said nothing. One that starts "The cart's acceleration was
$1.9 \pm 0.1\ \text{m/s}^2$, about a fifth of $g$, which is what a
$12^\circ$ ramp predicts" has said everything.

## Every number carries a unit and an uncertainty

$9.8$ is not an answer. $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ is. And if you measured it
yourself, $9.8 \pm 0.3\ \text{m/s}^2$ is the honest version — see
[[Uncertainty and Error]].

## Name the direction

Physics is full of quantities that are meaningless without a direction. "A
velocity of $4\ \text{m/s}$" is incomplete; "$4\ \text{m/s}$ east" or
"$-4\ \text{m/s}$, taking east as positive" is a statement someone can
check. State your positive direction once, at the top, and hold it.

## Words we use precisely

| Word | What it means here | What it does not mean |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | How fast, no direction | Velocity |
| Velocity | How fast, with direction | Speed |
| Acceleration | Rate of change of velocity | Going fast |
| Weight | The force of gravity, in newtons | Mass |
| Energy | A quantity that is conserved | Enthusiasm, "energy fields" |

The last row matters more than it looks. When a lab says energy was
"lost", it was not destroyed — it left as heat and sound, and a good
conclusion says where it went. See [[Conservation of Energy]].

> [!tip] The test for a good sentence
> Could a classmate who missed that class reproduce your reasoning from
> what you wrote? If not, something is in your head that never made it to
> the page.
