> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** a working motor plus a five-page report. **Groups of two.**
> **This is the culminating task for the course.** Due in the last week.
> **Assessed on:** all four categories.

Build the motor from [[Building a Simple Motor]], then take it seriously:
measure it, improve it, and explain it — including the moment when you run
it backwards and it becomes a generator.

## Part 1 — The build, measured

- Your motor, working, with a diagram showing current, field, and force.
- Its speed in revolutions per second, measured three ways or three times.
- Its efficiency, estimated: electrical energy in against mechanical
  energy out. This is hard to do well and the attempt is what is marked.

## Part 2 — One improvement, tested

Change one variable — turns in the coil, magnet strength, current — and
measure the effect on speed. Predict first. Report the prediction whether
or not it survived.

## Part 3 — The reverse

Disconnect the battery, connect a sensitive meter, and spin the coil by
hand. Record what you see. Explain it with
[[Electromagnetic Induction]], and connect it to how the electricity in
this room was generated — see [[Where Our Electricity Comes From]].

## Part 4 — The argument

Two pages: where this physics shows up in Ontario's grid, what it costs,
and what you think should change. Use numbers from the course. An opinion
without a number in it is not a physics argument.

> [!note] Why this is the culminating task
> It touches every strand: forces on a current-carrying wire, energy
> conversion and efficiency, the field concept, and a societal argument
> that only works if the numbers are right. If you can do this, you can do
> the course.
