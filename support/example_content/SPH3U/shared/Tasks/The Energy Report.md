> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** four pages, or a recorded presentation of the same
> substance. **Individually.** Four class periods of work time, then due.
> **Assessed on:** Knowledge, Thinking and Investigation, Communication,
> Application.

Pick one way of turning energy into electricity, and follow the energy all
the way through it — from what goes in, to what comes out, to what is
lost, to who lives beside it.

## Choose one

Nuclear fission (a CANDU reactor), hydroelectric, natural gas, wind,
solar photovoltaic, geothermal, or biomass. Ontario runs the first four
at scale, which makes them easier to research honestly.

## What to produce

1. **The energy path.** A diagram tracing the transformations from the
   original store to electricity in a wire, naming each form using the
   terminology from [[Work]], [[Kinetic Energy]],
   [[Gravitational Potential Energy]], and [[Thermal Energy and Heat]].
   Most of these paths pass through thermal energy; say where, and say
   what carries it — conduction, convection, or radiation.
2. **The numbers.** Input energy, useful output energy, and per cent
   efficiency, each with a source. Then put your method into this table
   beside two others, so the comparison is visible:

   | Method | Typical efficiency | What limits it |
   | --- | --- | --- |
   | Yours | | |
   | One other thermal method | | |
   | One non-thermal method | | |

3. **Where the losses go.** Not "some energy is lost" — energy is never
   lost. Say what form it ends up in, at what temperature, and where it
   physically goes. A thermal station's cooling water and a wind
   turbine's wake are both answers to this question.
4. **The consequences.** On the basis of your research: what does this
   technology do to the people and the environment around it? Include at
   least one impact its supporters emphasise and one its critics do, and
   say which evidence you found more convincing and why.
5. **Who does this work.** Name two occupations that keep your technology
   running — one requiring a degree, one requiring a licence or an
   apprenticeship — and say what each actually does on a shift.

## Sources

At least four, at least two of them primary — an operator's own data, a
regulator's report, a government dataset. [[Asking and Researching Well]]
covers where to look and how to judge what you find; a claim that traces
back to a press release is one source, not three.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | The energy path is complete and the terminology is exact |
| Thinking | The efficiency comparison is fair — like measured against like, conditions stated |
| Application | The physics in the report explains the consequences, rather than sitting beside them |
| Communication | Sources cited where the claims are made; a reader could check any number in it |

> [!tip] The trap in this task
> Efficiency figures are quoted under wildly different conditions, and
> comparing a nameplate figure with a real-world one makes any method look
> like a winner. Say what conditions each of your numbers assumes. A
> report that gets a lower mark for honest comparison than a glossy one
> would be a badly marked report — that is not what happens here.
