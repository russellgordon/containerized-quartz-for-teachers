## What it is for

Everything else this semester was done with a partner, a bench, and time
to look things up. This is the one piece of evidence that is
unambiguously yours, and it is the closest thing in this course to how
SPH4U and first-year physics will assess you.

## What is on it

| Part | Roughly | What it asks you to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Kinematics | 20% | Read and produce motion graphs; the kinematic equations; a projectile problem in two dimensions |
| B. Forces | 20% | Free-body diagrams; Newton's laws applied to systems; friction and inclines |
| C. Energy and society | 20% | Work, power, efficiency; conservation of energy through a real device; a thermal calculation |
| D. Waves and sound | 20% | Wave properties; resonance and standing waves; interference; the Doppler effect |
| E. Electricity and magnetism | 20% | Circuit analysis; Ohm's law; the right-hand rules; induction |

Woven through every part: **the skills strand**. One question gives you
data you have not seen and asks you to graph it, state a conclusion, and
say what its uncertainty allows you to claim. Another asks for a vector
diagram or a free-body diagram drawn to convention. A third asks you to
work in symbols before substituting numbers.

## What to expect, precisely

- **Show the physics, then the arithmetic.** A correct number with no
  reasoning earns little; correct reasoning with an arithmetic slip earns
  most of the marks.
- **State your positive direction** in every problem with a sign in it,
  and hold it. Most lost marks in kinematics are sign errors that a
  stated convention would have prevented.
- **Units and significant figures are marked**, per
  [[Units and Significant Figures]]. A bare number is not an answer.
- **Diagrams are marked** on convention: labelled axes with scales, free-
  body diagrams with every force named by what exerts it, ray and wave
  diagrams drawn with a ruler.
- **Terminology is expected to be exact**: mass and weight, speed and
  velocity, heat and temperature, current and potential difference.

## How to prepare

1. **Redo questions, do not reread them.** Five from each set, cold:
   [[Motion Graph Practice]], [[Kinematic Equation Practice]],
   [[Projectile Practice]], [[Free-Body Diagram Practice]],
   [[Force and Acceleration Practice]], [[Energy Practice]],
   [[Waves and Sound Practice]], [[Circuit Practice]].
2. **Redraw the standard diagrams from memory** — a velocity–time graph
   with its area shaded, a block on an incline, a standing wave in a
   closed tube, a series-parallel circuit. If you cannot draw it, you
   cannot yet explain it.
3. **Re-read your own investigations.** What you wrote about what went
   wrong at the bench is the most efficient revision there is, because
   you wrote it at the moment of understanding.
4. **Say the explanations out loud.** Every explanation mark on this
   paper is a sentence you could have said to somebody.
5. **Bring questions to the review classes.** There are four of them, and
   this page is what is on the examination.

> [!tip] In the three hours
> Budget by the weightings above. Do the calculation-heavy parts while
> you are fresh. If a problem stalls, write what you know, draw the
> diagram, and name the principle you would apply — partial reasoning
> earns partial marks and a blank space earns none.

## How this is assessed

Against the same expectations as everything else. Per [[How Marks Work]],
this examination is part of the final 30% of the course mark alongside
[[The Energy Report]], so that neither one afternoon nor one report
decides your grade alone.
