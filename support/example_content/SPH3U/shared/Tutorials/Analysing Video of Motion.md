A phone camera is a motion sensor you already own. Thirty frames a second
is enough to measure the acceleration of almost anything we can safely
drop, roll, or throw.

## Filming so the video is usable

1. **Camera still.** Brace it, or put it on a stack of books. A moving
   camera makes everything in the frame appear to move.
2. **Perpendicular.** Film across the motion, not down its line.
3. **A ruler in frame.** Something of known length, in the same plane as
   the motion — a metre stick taped to the wall behind is ideal. Without
   it there is no scale, and no measurement.
4. **Plain background.** The software finds the object by contrast.

## Getting numbers out

Any frame-by-frame tool works, including the one on the lab computers.
The routine is always the same: set the scale from your known length, set
the origin, then step through frames marking the object. You end up with a
table of time and position — which is exactly what
[[Position–Time Graphs]] is about.

## The trap

Frame rate is not always what the phone claims, especially in slow motion.
Check it once by filming something with a known period — a pendulum, or a
metronome — and counting frames.

> [!note] Why we do this at all
> A motion sensor gives you the graph and hides the measurement. Video
> gives you the measurement and makes you build the graph. In this course
> you should do both at least once, and notice that they agree.
