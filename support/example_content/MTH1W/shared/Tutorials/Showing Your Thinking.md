An answer with no visible thinking is a rumour: it might be true, but
nobody — including you in three weeks — can tell why. In this course
the reasoning *is* the work, on paper and at the boards alike, and
showing it is a concrete skill with named parts, not a vague demand
for more ink.

## The four-part habit on paper

1. **Restate** — one line saying what the problem actually asks, in
   your own words. Half of all wrong answers are right answers to a
   misread question.
2. **Reason** — the steps in order, with a word or two about *why*
   each move is legal. "Divide both sides by 4" beats a bare arrow.
3. **Units** — carried through every line, not sprinkled on at the
   end. If the units arrive as dollars per gigabyte, you know what
   your number means; if they arrive wrong, something upstream broke.
4. **Answer sentence** — a full sentence a stranger could read on its
   own: "The cheaper plan overtakes at 6 GB per month." A boxed number
   is not an answer to a question about phone plans.

## At the whiteboards

One rule does most of the work: **the person holding the marker writes
only other people's ideas.** It forces reasoning out loud — you cannot
write what nobody has said — so every line on the board has already
survived one listener. And cross out rather than erase: a corrected
line is a record of thinking, which is what the board is *for*, as
[[Mistakes Are Data]] argues at length.

## The checklist

- [ ] Could someone follow this without me standing beside it?
- [ ] Is there a why beside every non-obvious step?
- [ ] Do the units survive from the first line to the last?
- [ ] Does it end in a sentence answering the actual question?
- [ ] Are my crossed-out attempts still visible?
