Desmos is free, runs in any browser, and is the fastest way in the
world to ask a graph a question. Treat it as a thinking tool — a place
to test conjectures — not an answer machine you obey.

## Plot, slide, test

Type an equation and it appears; that part needs no tutorial. The
thinking starts with **sliders**: type $y = mx + b$ and Desmos offers
to make $m$ and $b$ adjustable. Drag them and watch. What does $m$ do?
What does $b$ do? What happens when $m$ goes negative, or hits zero?
Two minutes of sliding builds intuition an hour of hand-plotting
cannot — and it all connects straight back to
[[Slope and Rate of Change]] and [[Linear Relations]].

The habit that keeps it a *thinking* tool: predict first. Say out loud
what the graph will do, then slide. When the graph surprises you, you
have found the exact edge of your understanding — the same
try-before-peeking rule the Exercises use.

## When technology helps — and when it slows you down

Desmos shines when the question is about *shape and behaviour*:
comparing a line to a curve, watching a family of lines pivot,
checking a conjecture on ten cases in ten seconds. It is slower than
your head for $7 \times 8$, for judging whether an answer is the right
size, and for everything [[Estimation Duels]] trains. Reaching for a
tool you do not need is its own kind of slow — the skill is knowing
which moment you are in.

## Further along

Desmos will also fit models to data: paste in a table, and regression
finds the line or curve that best describes it. That matters once we
start [[D1.3|testing models]] against real measurements in the data
unit — [[Scatter Plot Practice]] is where it first appears, and
[[Bungee Drop]] is where a fitted line earns real trust, one storey at
a time.
