These questions follow [[Ratios, Rates, and Proportions]] — the
comparisons behind them were argued out in class during
[[Would You Rather]] and [[Estimation Duels]]. Try each before peeking.

## Questions

1. Which is the better buy: 3 granola bars for $\$5$, or 5 of the same
   bars for $\$8$? Show the comparison.
2. A juice mix uses concentrate and water in the ratio $3:2$. How much
   water goes with 12 cans of concentrate?
3. A bus travels 240 km in 3 hours at a steady speed. Find its speed,
   then find how long 400 km would take at that speed.
4. In a survey, 42 of 150 students walk to school. What percent is
   that?
5. **Explain why.** Paint is mixed 2 parts blue to 3 parts yellow.
   Jordan adds one extra cup of each colour and says the shade will
   not change. Explain why Jordan is wrong.
6. **Pose your own.** Write a rate problem from your own life — data
   plans, allowance, practice time, anything real — and solve it.
7. **Challenge.** One tap fills a tub in 20 minutes; a second tap
   fills it in 30 minutes. Running together, how long do they take?

## Answers

> [!success]- Answer 1
> Compare unit prices: $\$5 \div 3 \approx \$1.67$ per bar versus
> $\$8 \div 5 = \$1.60$ per bar. The 5-pack wins — the point of a
> unit rate is that it makes unequal groups comparable.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> The ratio scales: $3:2$ becomes $12:8$ after multiplying both parts
> by 4. So 8 cans of water.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> Speed $= 240 \div 3 = 80$ km/h. Then $400 \div 80 = 5$ hours —
> the same unit-rate idea, pointed in opposite directions.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> $\frac{42}{150} = \frac{28}{100} = 28\%$. Scaling the denominator
> to 100 *is* what "percent" means.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> Adding the same amount to both parts changes the ratio: $2:3$
> becomes $3:4$. As fractions of the mix, blue goes from
> $\frac{2}{5}$ to $\frac{3}{7}$ — a slightly bluer shade. Ratios
> are preserved by *multiplying*, not by adding.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> Answers vary — a strong one names two quantities, states the rate
> with units, and uses it to predict something. Record yours in your
> [[Math Journal]]; posing problems is itself the skill here.

> [!success]- Answer 7
> Think in tub-per-minute rates: $\frac{1}{20} + \frac{1}{30}
> = \frac{3}{60} + \frac{2}{60} = \frac{5}{60} = \frac{1}{12}$ of a
> tub each minute — so 12 minutes. Sense check: faster than either
> tap alone, but not faster than half of 20.
