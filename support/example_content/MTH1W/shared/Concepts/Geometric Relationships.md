The shapes round of [[Which One Doesn't Belong]] worked because every
shape carries properties — and any property can be the reason a shape
stands apart. This page collects the properties this course leans on
most, the ones that turn "it looks about right" into "it must be so".

## Properties worth carrying everywhere

A circle is the set of points at one fixed distance — the radius —
from the centre, which is why every radius is equal and why a compass
draws one. The angles inside any triangle sum to $180°$, no matter how
the triangle is stretched. And for **right** triangles only, the side
lengths obey

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

where $c$ is the hypotenuse, the side facing the right angle. The
relationship works in both directions: it finds a missing side when
you know the triangle is right, and it *tests* for squareness when you
do not — measure a doorframe's sides and diagonal, and the numbers
tell you whether the corner is true. Builders were using the $3$–$4$–$5$
version of this idea thousands of years before anyone wrote a proof.

## Composite shapes: divide and conquer

Real designs are rarely single shapes — a running-track boundary is a
rectangle wearing two semicircular caps. The strategy never changes:

- [ ] Split the figure into shapes whose properties you know
- [ ] Hunt for hidden right triangles — draw a height or a diagonal
      and one usually appears
- [ ] Label every length you know, and name the ones you need
- [ ] Solve piece by piece, then check the total against an estimate —
      does the answer survive [[Checking Your Own Work]]?

More than one split is usually possible, and comparing two groups'
different splits of the same figure is a whiteboard argument worth
having. These properties become design constraints in
[[Design Under Constraints]], and they pair naturally with the
dimension-scaling ideas in [[Measurement and Optimization]].
