Your [[Bungee Drop]] cord stretched by roughly the same amount for
every added storey — and that steadiness is the whole definition. A
relation is **linear** when one quantity changes by a constant amount
each time the other ticks up by one. Same story, four costumes:

| Representation | Where the rate hides | Where the start hides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Story | "$15 per month, $50 to join" | the joining fee |
| Table of values | equal jumps down the column | the value when $x = 0$ |
| Graph | the [[Slope and Rate of Change\|slope]] | the $y$-intercept |
| Equation $y = mx + b$ | the $m$ | the $b$ |

The gym membership $C = 15m + 50$ is the same object as its table, its
graph, and its story. Moving fluently between the four — and knowing
where the rate of change and the initial value sit in each — is the
skill this course returns to more than any other.

## Linear or not? Look at the shape first

Before any calculation, ask what the graph *looks like*. Equal steps
produce a straight line — that is not a coincidence but the visual
signature of a constant rate. Curves mean the rate itself is changing:
the painted-face counts in [[The Painted Cube]] bend upward because
each layer adds more than the last. In a table, check the *first
differences* — the jumps between consecutive values. Constant jumps
mean linear; growing or shrinking jumps mean the relation is bending,
and a graph will show you which way.

Shape-first thinking also powers prediction. A straight trend invites
you to extend the ruler; a curve warns you that tomorrow's change will
not match today's. Sketch relations in [[Using Desmos]] and practise
saying what the shape claims *before* reading any numbers —
[[Slope and Graphing Practice]] builds exactly this reflex, and
[[Which Phone Plan]] is where two competing linear stories finally
cross.
