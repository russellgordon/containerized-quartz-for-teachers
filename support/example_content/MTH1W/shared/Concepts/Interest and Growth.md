Some things gain value while you own them and some lose it — a rare
guitar may **appreciate** while the van that carries it
**depreciates**. Graphs of value over time tell these stories at a
glance, and the shape ideas from [[Linear Relations]] read them:
straight means a steady gain or loss each year, curved means the rate
itself is changing. Interest is the same story told about money you
have lent or borrowed — and the shape of *that* graph decides what
borrowing really costs.

## Simple grows straight, compound bends

**Simple interest** pays on the original amount only: the same dollars
every year, a straight line. **Compound interest** pays interest on
the interest — each year's growth joins the principal and starts
earning too, which is repeated multiplication, the territory of
[[Powers and Exponent Rules]]. Ten years of $5\%$ compound growth
multiplies by $1.05$ ten times: a curve that starts lazily and
steepens forever.

> [!success]- Quick self-check (click to expand)
> Invest \$1000 at $5\%$ per year for $10$ years. Simple:
> $1000 + 10 \times 50 = \textdollar 1500$. Compounded annually:
> $1000 \times 1.05^{10} \approx \textdollar 1628.89$. The extra \$128.89 is
> interest earned by earlier interest — and the gap between the line
> and the curve only widens from here.

## The four levers of a loan

Every borrowing arrangement is set by the same levers, and comparing
[[F1.3|their effects on the overall cost]] is what informed borrowing
means: the **rate** (a higher percentage compounds the pain), the
**time** (longer borrowing means more compounding periods — a lower
monthly payment can cost far more in total), **how the interest is
calculated** (compounding monthly rather than yearly pulls interest
forward, so it starts earning sooner), and the **down payment** (every
dollar paid up front is a dollar that never earns interest against
you). None of this needs to stay abstract — the same $1.05^{10}$
arithmetic runs your [[A Budget Calculator in Code]], and it is one of
the most direct answers this course has to [[When Will I Use This]].
