The mobile puzzles from the whiteboards — shapes hanging in balance,
find the weight of each — are equations wearing a friendlier outfit.
An equation is a claim that two expressions weigh the same, and the
equals sign is the pivot point. Whatever you do to one side, you must
do to the other, or the claim stops being true.

## Undo, in reverse order

An equation like $3x + 5 = 20$ describes a story done *to* $x$: it
was tripled, then five was added. Solving retraces the story
backwards, undoing the last operation first — subtract $5$ from both
sides, then divide both sides by $3$:

$$
3x + 5 = 20 \;\Rightarrow\; 3x = 15 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 5
$$

There is no single sacred layout for this work. Some people write both
sides at every step; some reason it as "three of something is $15$".
What matters — see [[Showing Your Thinking]] — is that each line is a
true statement that follows from the one before.

## Verifying is part of solving, not a bonus

A solution is a *claim*, and claims get checked. Substitute your
answer back into the **original** equation — not a later line, which
may already contain your mistake. In this course,
[[C1.5|verifying your solution]] is not optional polish; an unverified
solution is an unfinished one.

- [ ] Substitute the value into the original equation
- [ ] Evaluate each side separately — do not solve again
- [ ] Confirm both sides land on the same number
- [ ] If they differ, celebrate: you found the error before anyone
      else did, which is the whole point of [[Mistakes Are Data]]

The habit generalises far beyond equations — it is the core move in
[[Checking Your Own Work]]. [[Equation Practice]] builds fluency, and
[[Getting Unstuck]] has strategies for equations that fight back.
