You met this idea arguing over [[Which Phone Plan]] was actually
cheaper: the plans refused to be compared until someone found the cost
of *one* gigabyte on each. A comparison of two quantities is a
**ratio**; when the quantities carry different units — dollars and
gigabytes, kilometres and hours — the comparison is called a **rate**.

## Unit rates are the great equaliser

Three avocados for $4.50 or five for $7.00? The offers live in
different worlds until you bring both down to the price of one:

$$
\frac{4.50}{3} = 1.50 \text{ \$/avocado} \qquad
\frac{7.00}{5} = 1.40 \text{ \$/avocado}
$$

Dividing to find "how much for one" puts every offer, speed, and
recipe on the same footing. A **proportion** is the claim that two
ratios are equal — that both quantities were scaled by the same
factor. Checking that claim, rather than assuming it, is most of the
work in [[B3.5|problems involving rates, percentages, and proportions]].

> [!success]- Quick self-check (click to expand)
> A recipe uses 2 cups of oats for 3 servings. How many cups for 8
> servings? Per serving: $\frac{2}{3}$ of a cup. For eight:
> $8 \times \frac{2}{3} = \frac{16}{3} = 5\frac{1}{3}$ cups. Notice
> the unit rate did the heavy lifting — no cross-multiplying ritual
> required.

## The course's quiet superpower

Proportional reasoning rarely gets a unit title, yet it is working
underneath almost everything here: percent problems, unit conversions
in [[Measurement and Optimization]], the steady growth that makes
[[Linear Relations]] linear, and interest rates in
[[Interest and Growth]]. When a problem feels stuck, "what is this per
*one*?" is one of the most reliable questions in [[Getting Unstuck]].

[[Ratio and Rate Practice]] builds fluency, and [[Estimation Duels]]
is where a good internal sense of rate starts winning arguments.
