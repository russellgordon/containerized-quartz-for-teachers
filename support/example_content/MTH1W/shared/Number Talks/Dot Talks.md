An arrangement of dots flashes on the screen for about three seconds —
too fast to count one by one. The question is never just *how many?* It
is *how did you see them?* Your brain groups dots before you can stop
it, and those groupings are arithmetic in disguise.

## How we play

1. The image appears briefly, then disappears. Think with your head up
   and hands down; show a quiet thumb when you have a total.
2. We collect every answer on the board first, without judgment.
3. Then the real work: you describe *how you saw it*, and the image
   returns so we can circle each way of seeing in a different colour.

> [!example]- One image, four ways of seeing (click to expand)
> Ten dots: two tilted squares of four, with two dots between them.
>
> - "Four and four and two" — the squares, then the leftovers.
> - "Two rows of five" — a mental rearrangement into $2 \times 5$.
> - "Twelve take away two" — a full $3 \times 4$ grid with two holes.
> - "Five pairs" — skip-counting by twos.
>
> Four ways of seeing — four correct pieces of arithmetic, one total.

## One variation

Give each dot a value of $\frac{1}{2}$ instead of $1$. The same picture
now asks for $10 \times \frac{1}{2}$, and seeing in groups starts paying
off in fraction arithmetic — the heart of [[Fraction Talks]].

> [!tip] Structure beats speed
> Nobody wins by counting fastest. A slower seeing that uses groups,
> arrays, or missing pieces scales up; one-by-one counting never does.
