Four items appear in a two-by-two grid — numbers, graphs, equations,
shapes — and the question is which one doesn't belong. The trick: there
is a defensible answer for *every* corner. Your job is to find a reason,
say it precisely, and listen for reasons that pick the other corners.

## How we play

1. The grid goes up. Study all four corners in silence.
2. Point (on the count of three) at your corner, then build a defence.
3. We argue in rounds until every corner has been defended at least
   once. The vocabulary we need gets written down as it appears —
   because "it looks weird" convinces nobody.

> [!example]- One grid, four defences (click to expand)
> The corners: $y = 2x$, $y = x^2$, $y = 2x + 1$, $y = -2x$.
>
> - "$y = x^2$ — the only one whose graph is not a straight line."
> - "$y = 2x + 1$ — the only one that misses the point $(0, 0)$."
> - "$y = -2x$ — the only one that falls as $x$ grows."
> - "$y = 2x$ — the only one that belongs to all three other groups."
>
> Every defence leans on a property with a name — that is the point.

## One variation

Build your own grid. Making all four corners defensible is harder than
it looks — and it quietly demands the exact comparisons of graphs,
tables, and equations that [[Linear Relations]] runs on.

> [!tip] Precision is the sport
> "It's different" scores nothing. "It is the only non-linear one"
> scores. Every round is secretly a vocabulary lesson you run yourself.
