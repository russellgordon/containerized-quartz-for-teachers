A single claim goes on the board, wearing an equals sign:
$37 + 48 = 35 + 50$. Your job is not to compute both sides — it is to
decide whether the claim is *true or false*, and to defend the verdict.
The equals sign is not a command meaning "the answer comes next"; it is
a claim that two things are the same, and claims can be tested.

## How we play

1. The claim appears. Decide in your head: true, false, or can't tell
   yet. No pencil arithmetic unless you truly need it.
2. Verdicts first — a show of hands — then the defences.
3. The best defences reason about structure — and never fully compute.

> [!example]- One claim, four defences (click to expand)
> The claim: $37 + 48 = 35 + 50$.
>
> - "True — two moved from the 37 to the 48, so the total is safe."
> - "True — both sides make 85. Computing works, it is just slower."
> - "I saw a balance scale: two off one pan, two onto the other."
> - "I lined them up: 35 is 2 less, 50 is 2 more — it evens out."
>
> Four defences — and only one of them needed actual arithmetic.

## One variation

Let letters in: is $4(n + 2) = 4n + 8$ true for *every* $n$, some $n$,
or none? Testing claims like these is exactly how
[[Algebraic Expressions]] tells equivalent expressions from impostors.

> [!tip] False is a full answer
> A crisp "false, because the left side is 2 more" is worth more than a
> timid "true?". Verdicts need reasons, in both directions.
