A string is a chain of related problems, served one at a time:
$16 \times 5$, then $16 \times 50$, then $16 \times 49$. Each answer is
a stepping stone to the next, and the last one — the one that looked
hard on its own — falls if you let the earlier answers do the lifting.
Strings train you to *use what you already know*, on purpose.

## How we play

1. One problem at a time. Solve it in your head; thumb when ready.
2. A few people defend their methods; we record each one on the board.
3. The next problem appears. Before computing from scratch, ask: what
   from the last answer can I reuse?

> [!example]- Four methods for $16 \times 49$ (click to expand)
> By the time this appears, $16 \times 50 = 800$ is on the board.
>
> - "One group of 16 less than $16 \times 50$: $800 - 16 = 784$."
> - "Halve and double: $16 \times 49 = 8 \times 98 = 784$."
> - "Split the 16: $(10 + 6) \times 49 = 490 + 294 = 784$."
> - "Factor the 49: $16 \times 7 \times 7 = 112 \times 7 = 784$."
>
> Four routes, one answer — and the first one barely needed effort.

## One variation

Fraction strings: $\frac{1}{2}$ of 48, then $\frac{1}{4}$ of 48, then
$\frac{3}{4}$ of 48. The same reuse instinct carries straight into
[[Fractions, Decimals, and Percents]].

> [!tip] Lazy, in the best way
> Mathematicians are proudly lazy: they refuse to compute what they can
> deduce. If a problem feels brand new, look back — it rarely is.
