How many litres of water does our school use in a day? Nobody in the
room knows — that is the point. An estimation duel asks you to commit
to a number anyway, defend it, and bracket the truth: name a value you
are sure is *too low* and one that is *too high*. Calculators can tell
you what the buttons said, never whether an answer is reasonable.

## How we play

1. A prompt goes up — a photo, a jar, a receipt. Silent thinking; no
   phones.
2. Write three numbers: too low, too high, and your best estimate.
3. Duel: convince your partner, then agree on a joint bracket you can
   both defend. The answer comes last; the tightest bracket wins.

> [!example]- One prompt, four defences (click to expand)
> *How many slices of pizza does our class eat in a school year?*
>
> - "Thirty students, one slice a week, forty weeks — about 1200."
> - "Pizza day is monthly: $30 \times 2 \times 10 = 600$."
> - "I anchored on three slices per person per month: about 900."
> - "Mine was 5000 — too high, but my bracket still caught the truth."

## One variation

Estimation tennis: one partner may only say "higher" or "lower" while
the other narrows in. Unit prices and speeds — the territory of
[[Ratios, Rates, and Proportions]] — make excellent rallies.

> [!tip] Defend the bracket, not the bullseye
> An estimate without a defence is a guess. Say what you anchored on —
> a known size, a rate, a comparison — and your range becomes evidence.
