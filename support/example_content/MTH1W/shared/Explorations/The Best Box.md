One square sheet of card, 20 cm on a side. Cut equal squares from the
four corners, fold up the flaps, tape the seams: an open-top box.
Every group starts with an identical sheet — yet the boxes that come
back hold wildly different amounts. Same material. Different choices.

## The task

Your group's box should hold as much as possible. Decide the size of
the corner cut, build the box, and compute its volume — then argue
about whether a different cut would beat it. Small cuts make a wide,
shallow tray; big cuts make a tall, narrow chimney. Somewhere between
the extremes is the best box. Find it, and be ready to explain how you
know nothing better is hiding between the sizes you tried.

> [!question]- Getting started (click to expand)
> - Try wildly different cuts first — 1 cm, 5 cm, 9 cm — before fine
>   tuning. What happens at each extreme, and why?
> - Keep a table: cut size, then length, width, height, volume. Where
>   does the volume rise, and where does it turn around?
> - When one dimension grows, what happens to the other two? You are
>   trading, not just gaining.

## What mathematics tends to surface

Changing one dimension changes the volume in competing directions at
once — taller, but narrower — and the table makes that tug-of-war
visible as numbers that rise, peak, and fall. Graphing volume against
cut size gives a curve, not a line, and hunting its peak by testing
values in between — 3 cm, then 3.5, then 3.3 — is the whole spirit of
[[Measurement and Optimization]].

## An extension

Same sheet, different fold: roll it into an open tube — a cylinder —
and compare what prism and cylinder each make of the card. And if the
sheet were 40 cm on a side, would the best cut simply double, and
would the best volume? Test your instinct before you trust it.

> [!note] The answer is not on this page
> On purpose. The best cut is the class's to find, defend, and refine
> together at the boards.
