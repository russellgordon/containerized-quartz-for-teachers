You have heard "a million" your whole life — a million dollars, a
million views, a million people. But have you ever *felt* the size of
it? Today your group picks a claim about a million of something and
finds out whether the number means what everyone assumes it means.

## The task

Choose one question (or invent your own) and work it out at the board,
showing every assumption. How long is a million seconds — could you
wait it out? How far is a million steps — could you walk it in a
school year? How tall is a million sheets of paper — taller than the
school? Convert your answer into units a person can feel, and be ready
to defend every estimate hiding inside it.

> [!question]- Getting started (click to expand)
> - You do not need exact figures — you need *defensible* ones. About
>   how long is one step? Measure one; do not debate it for ten
>   minutes.
> - Chains of unit conversions are friendlier one link at a time:
>   seconds to minutes to hours to days.
> - If your numbers grow too many zeros to trust, that is not failure
>   — that is a notation problem asking to be solved.

## What mathematics tends to surface

Estimates built from rates (100 steps per minute, 500 sheets per
5 cm of stack) chain into proportions, and the zeros pile up until
someone reinvents the shorthand $10^6$. This is where
[[Scientific Notation]] stops being a rule and starts being a relief —
and comparing a million to a billion, $10^6$ to $10^9$, turns
[[Powers and Exponent Rules]] into a way of *seeing* size, not just a
way of simplifying expressions.

## An extension

A billion is a thousand millions. Redo your claim for a billion and
notice what your writing hand does about all those zeros. Then bring
your best question to the [[Fermi Festival]], where estimates like
these compete in public.

> [!note] The answer is not on this page
> On purpose — every group's assumptions differ, so every group's
> million differs. The class compares and defends them together.
