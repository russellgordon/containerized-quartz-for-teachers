Build a cube out of smaller cubes — say $4 \times 4 \times 4$ — and
dip the whole thing in paint. Let it dry, then break it back into its
64 unit cubes. Some little cubes got painted on three faces. Some on
two, some on one — and some never saw paint at all.

## The task

For the $4 \times 4 \times 4$ cube: how many unit cubes have exactly
three painted faces? Two? One? Zero? Convince your group with reasons
before you count — then count to check. Next, do $5 \times 5 \times 5$
without building it. Then the real question: for an
$n \times n \times n$ cube, find an expression for each of the four
counts, and show that together they account for every unit cube.

> [!question]- Getting started (click to expand)
> - Where on the big cube must a three-face cube live? Hold a real
>   cube and point before you argue.
> - Is $3 \times 3 \times 3$ a friendlier place to start? Small cases
>   are not cheating — they are data.
> - Keep a table of your counts as the cube grows. Which count never
>   changes? Which grows steadily? Which do neither?

## What mathematics tends to surface

Each kind of cube lives somewhere geometric — corners, edges, faces,
hidden core — and each home grows differently: one count stays fixed,
one climbs by the same amount each time, two accelerate. Groups
rediscover the split between linear and non-linear growth here, long
before [[Linear Relations]] names it — and the jump from counting to
an expression in $n$ is exactly what [[Algebraic Expressions]] later
makes official.

## An extension

Add your four expressions and simplify. What *must* the total equal —
and why does that check your work better than any answer key? Then
paint an $a \times b \times c$ box instead of a cube and see which of
your arguments survive.

> [!note] The answer is not on this page
> On purpose. Your class builds this mathematics together at the
> boards; a printed solution would end the thinking before it starts.
