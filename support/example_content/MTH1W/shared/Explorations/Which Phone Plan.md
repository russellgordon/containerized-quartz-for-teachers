Two phone plans are competing for your money. Plan A charges $20 a
month, plus $4 for every gigabyte of data you use. Plan B charges a
flat $50 a month, data included. Everyone in the room has an instinct
about which is better. Instincts are not receipts.

## The task

Which plan is cheaper? Prove it — with a table, a graph, an argument,
anything that would convince a skeptical bill-payer. Your answer must
say not just *which*, but *when*: for what kind of user does the
winner change? Then a third offer appears — Plan C, no monthly fee,
$8 per gigabyte — and your group's job becomes a complete buyer's
guide: for every level of data use, which plan wins, and why?

> [!question]- Getting started (click to expand)
> - Pick easy usages — 0 GB, 5 GB, 10 GB — and just compute each
>   plan's bill. A table is a perfectly respectable weapon.
> - Put your bills on a graph, cost against gigabytes. What does each
>   plan look like — and what does a *crossing* mean in dollars?
> - Why does Plan C start out winning, then stop? Which part of its
>   cost is doing that?

## What mathematics tends to surface

Each plan is a starting fee plus a rate, and each graph is a line
whose steepness *is* its price per gigabyte — the heart of
[[Linear Relations]] and [[Slope and Rate of Change]]. The crossing
points, where two bills agree to the cent, are exactly what
[[Solving Equations]] is for: set two costs equal and ask *when*. The
buyer's guide itself is a financial argument — the mathematics is what
makes it honest.

## An extension

Real plans bend: 10 GB included, then $10 per extra gigabyte. Add a
bent plan to your graph and update the buyer's guide. Can you design a
plan that is never the cheapest choice for anyone — and how can you
tell, from the graph alone, that nobody should buy it?

> [!note] The answer is not on this page
> On purpose. Each class builds its own buyer's guide at the boards —
> and defends it, crossing point by crossing point.
