## What you are making

A **two-page recommendation** about one money decision, addressed to the
person who has to make it. Not a hypothetical: a decision somebody
genuinely faces — you, a family member, a team, a club.

Some that work well:

- Buying a used phone outright, or paying it off monthly with a plan.
- Saving for something over a year: which account, and how much a month.
- A first car: purchase price against insurance, fuel, and repairs.
- Financing a laptop at "0% for 12 months", read properly.
- A club's fundraiser: what to charge, and what has to sell to break
  even.

## What must be in it

**1. The situation, and where it came from.** A past or current
situation — yours or somebody's you can ask. What was decided, what it
cost, and what the person would do differently. Real numbers, or clearly
labelled realistic ones if the person prefers privacy.

**2. Two options, side by side**, each as a table AND a graph. Same
axes, same scale — that is what makes them comparable, and
[[Linear Relations]] is why the graph usually answers the question
faster than the table.

**3. Where the lines cross**, if they do, and what that point means in
the situation's own words. "At fourteen months the plan overtakes buying
outright" is a sentence somebody can act on.

**4. Interest, if there is any.** Show what changes when the rate
changes, when the borrowing time changes, and when interest is
calculated more often. One of those three surprises most people, and
your job is to say which and by how much.

**5. Something that grows or shrinks over time.** A value that
appreciates or depreciates — a car losing value, savings growing — with
a graph over at least five years, and the shape described honestly.
A straight line and a curve are different claims about the future.

**6. A budget, modified.** Build a simple monthly budget for the
person, then change one circumstance — hours cut, rent up, a raise, a
repair — and show what has to give. Say which change you would advise
and why.

## The working periods

| Day | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Situation chosen; the person interviewed; real numbers gathered |
| 2 | Two options tabled and graphed; the crossing point found |
| 3 | Interest and the growth-or-decline graph |
| 4 | The budget, modified — then read by another pair who challenge one number each |

## How this is assessed

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Real | An actual situation, with numbers somebody recognises |
| Compared fairly | Both options on the same axes, over the same time |
| The crossing point is explained | In the situation's words, not "they intersect" |
| Interest understood | Rate, time, and frequency each shown to matter |
| Growth described honestly | Straight or curved, and what that predicts |
| Budget is realistic | The modification forces a genuine trade-off |
| Written for the person | Two pages they could use to decide |

> [!question]- If the numbers feel too personal to share
> Then change them and say so at the top: "the amounts here are
> realistic but not exact". The mathematics is identical, and nobody is
> owed your family's finances. What cannot be faked is the structure of
> the decision — that has to be somebody's real situation.

## Reflect

A [[Math Journal]] entry: which number turned out to decide it, and was
that the number you expected? Then: what would you now ask before
signing anything with the word "monthly" in it?
