When a scientist runs an experiment and gets a surprising result, she
does not crumple the printout and apologise — the surprise *is* the
finding. A wrong answer works the same way: it measures the gap between
how you thought the mathematics worked and how it actually works, and
it is the only measurement of that gap you will ever get. This is why
the rule at our whiteboards is that first attempts are never erased —
cross them out, keep them visible, and let them tell you what they
know.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Think of a mistake you made this month that taught you more than a
   right answer would have. What exactly did it teach — and would you
   have learned it any other way?
2. Programmers expect code to fail on the first run; the whole craft
   of [[C2.3|reading and altering code]] assumes it, and debugging has
   a name because bugs are normal. Why does mathematics feel
   different — and should it?
3. What separates a mistake worth studying from a slip worth shrugging
   off? Who gets to decide which is which?
4. Erasing a wrong attempt feels *good*. What is that feeling
   protecting — and what does the erasing cost?
5. If mistakes are data, is a page of all-correct answers good news,
   or evidence that the work was too easy to teach you anything?

The norm this discussion protects is written into
[[Our Classroom Norms]]: mistakes are data — and growth. A crossed-out
line on a whiteboard is not a record of failure; it is the room's raw
data. When we tour the boards to consolidate, those visible wrong
turns are how the whole class learns from a mistake only one group had
to make — the best exchange rate in education.
