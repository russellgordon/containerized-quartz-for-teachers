An archive is not a library. A library holds published things, arranged so
you can find a subject. An archive holds what an organisation or a family
accumulated while doing something else, kept in the order it was created in
— so you find material by tracing who made it, not by topic.

That single difference explains most of the frustration students feel in
their first hour, and most of the speed they gain in their second.

## The ladder

Federal material in the United States is described from the top down:
**record group** — everything from one agency or bureau — then **series**,
then **file unit**, then **item**. A description of a whole series may be
all that exists, because no archive has ever described its holdings item by
item.

So work down the ladder rather than searching across it. Ask which agency
would have been required to create the document you want, find its record
group, then read the series descriptions. A search box rewards you for
guessing the cataloguer's words; the ladder does not require you to guess.

> [!example]- What a catalogue record is really telling you (click to expand)
> A record in the National Archives Catalog gives you a title, a date range,
> a creating agency, a level of description, and a reference. What it does
> not always give you is the thing itself: some records are fully digitised
> and readable now, and some are a paragraph describing thirty boxes in a
> building in Maryland. Check the level of description before you plan an
> afternoon around it — and copy the reference exactly, because it is how
> anyone, including you next month, finds the same item again.

Most archival description is online; most archival material is not. Prefer a
digitised collection when the deadline is a week away, and treat an
undigitised series as a finding about what has not been made easy to see.

## Where we will actually work

The **National Archives Catalog** is the entry point for federal records,
with the Founding Documents and the Milestone Documents as curated
starting points. **DocsTeach**, run by the National Archives Foundation, is
free and holds 13,639 primary source documents arranged for teaching — the
quickest place to find a usable document when your own search has stalled.
For people rather than policies, the **1950 census** is name-searchable at
1950census.archives.gov, and the National Archives' **Citizen Archivist**
programme lets you transcribe records yourself, which teaches more about
description in an hour than any explanation.

## Before you close the tab

Copy the repository, the record group or collection, the reference, the
title and the date the moment you open an item, along with the date you
looked. [[Citing Historical Sources]] explains the form, and
[[Where the Records Live]] has the full list of addresses. Then run the
routine in [[Reading a Primary Source]] on what you found.
