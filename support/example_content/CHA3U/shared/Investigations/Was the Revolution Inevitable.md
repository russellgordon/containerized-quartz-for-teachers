Thirteen colonies that had spent a century inside the British Empire, and
had just been defended by it at enormous expense, were at war with it within
twelve years of the peace of 1763. The investigation is whether that outcome
was built into the relationship or produced by decisions that could have
gone another way.

"Inevitable" is the word to take apart first. Nothing is inevitable in
history except in hindsight, so the useful version of the question is: at
what point did the alternatives run out, and what closed them?

## Three claims, and what each would require

**Structural.** The colonies had grown into economies, assemblies and
populations that no imperial administration could govern from three
thousand miles away. To argue this you need evidence of divergence before
1763 — trade patterns, colonial legislatures acting as governments, the long
practice the curriculum calls salutary neglect.

**Fiscal and legal.** A war debt forced Britain to tax and regulate where it
previously had not, and the colonial response hardened with each measure.
This is an argument about a sequence: Revenue Act, Stamp Act, Declaratory
Act, Townshend Duties, and the Quebec Act of 1774, which the curriculum
notes elicited outrage in the colonies.

**Contingent.** Particular people made particular choices — in Parliament,
in Boston, in the Continental Congress — and other choices were available at
each step. This argument needs the alternatives that were actually proposed
at the time, not ones you can imagine.

## The evidence set

Your class works with the statutes themselves; colonial assembly petitions
and resolves; the newspaper and pamphlet argument on both sides of the
Atlantic; and the Declaration of Independence, read as a legal indictment
rather than as a preamble — its long central list of grievances is a source
about which measures had actually bitten, and which had not.

Then read past 1776 to B1.4's other documents. The Articles of
Confederation, the Constitution and the Bill of Rights tell you what the
revolutionaries feared once they had won, and a revolution's own settlement
is evidence about what it was for.

> [!success]- The turn most answers get wrong (click to expand)
> Writing that "taxation without representation caused the Revolution" and
> stopping. That is the period's own slogan restated, and slogans are
> sources, not conclusions. The claim becomes an argument only when you show
> which measure produced which response, name a moment at which a different
> decision was available, and explain why it was not taken. If your evidence
> cannot do all three, say so — a bounded conclusion marks better than a
> confident one resting on nothing.

## What the class produces

A written judgement with its criteria stated first, using
[[Cause and Consequence]] to separate long-term conditions from decisions.
Expect the room to divide, then carry the disagreement into
[[What Was the Revolution For|What Was the Revolution For?]] and the written argument into
[[The Revolution Question]].
