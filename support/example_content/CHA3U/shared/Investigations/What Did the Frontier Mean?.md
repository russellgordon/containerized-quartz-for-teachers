"Frontier" is not a place. It is a word one society used for the edge of its
own expansion, and it only works as a description if you stand in a
particular spot and face west. This investigation asks what the same
territory, in the same decades, meant to the people already living on it, to
the people moving onto it, and to the government that was disposing of it.

## The same land, four accounts

| Who | What the land was | What the record shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Native American nations | Home, hunting territory, and treaty ground | Treaties, council speeches, petitions, court cases |
| Settler families | A farm that could be paid for in labour | Land entries, letters home, county records |
| Speculators and railways | An asset to be surveyed and sold | Company records, land grants, maps |
| The federal government | Territory to be organised into states | Statutes, surveys, army returns, appropriations |

Read across the row before you argue down the column. Any one of these
accounts, taken alone, produces a story that is internally consistent and
historically useless.

## The policy record is explicit

This is not a case where you must infer intent. The curriculum's own list
names the Indian Removal Act of 1830, forced migrations, the Indian
Appropriations Act of 1851 and the creation of western reservations. Removal
was legislated, litigated, funded and reported on; there are appropriations,
muster rolls, agents' letters and route accounts. Where a policy leaves that
much paper, an argument that it was accidental or unforeseen has a great
deal of evidence to get past.

Note also that the forced migration of Native nations and the arrival of
Irish and German immigrants in eastern cities are the same period's
population history, and the curriculum places both under migration. Holding
them in one frame — who was moved, who moved, and who chose — is the
strongest structure available for this investigation.

## Working question for your notebook

Take one specific place. Establish who lived there in 1820, who lived there
in 1870, and what documents record the transition. A single county, done
properly with named sources, produces a better argument than a national
survey assembled from generalisations. [[Where the Records Live]] is where
to begin; the treaties and statutes are in [[Government Records]].

## What the class produces

A short paper that answers the title question three times, once from each of
three positions, and then states which account the evidence best supports
and why. Avoid the tidy move of concluding that everyone had their own
truth: the accounts are not equally supported, and saying which is better
evidenced is the historical judgement being asked for.

Set the result against [[Whose Story Gets Taught?]] later in the year, and
use it in [[The Union Divided]].
