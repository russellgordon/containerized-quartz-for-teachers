For long stretches of American history the only account by the people a
policy was done to is an account they had to fight to publish, or one that
was taken down by somebody else. Testimony is therefore not a supplement to
the documentary record. In the history of slavery it is frequently the only
place a person speaks at all.

## Two bodies of material, and they are not the same

**The published narratives of the nineteenth century.** Formerly enslaved
people wrote and dictated accounts that were printed, sold and read in
enormous numbers — the curriculum names Frederick Douglass and Solomon
Northup among the individuals whose work shaped American culture in this
period. These were written into an argument. Many were published with
abolitionist support, prefaced by white sponsors vouching for their truth,
and shaped for a reading public that had to be persuaded. Knowing that is
not a reason to discount them; it is the context that tells you what each
passage was for.

**The interviews of the 1930s.** Under a federal work-relief programme,
interviewers recorded conversations with people who had been born into
slavery and were by then in their eighties and nineties, seventy years after
emancipation. The provenance matters at every level: most interviewers were
white and local, in a segregated South, asking people who depended on relief
about the people who had owned them; the transcripts were rendered in
dialect spelling chosen by the interviewer; and the questions came from a
prepared list. The result is indispensable and heavily mediated, and both
halves of that sentence have to appear in your writing.

> [!important] A witness is not an illustration
> Testimony is used badly when a single sentence is lifted to decorate a
> paragraph that had already reached its conclusion. Use it as evidence: say
> who spoke, to whom, when, in what circumstances, and who holds the record
> now. Then let it complicate your argument. If the testimony you quote does
> not change anything you wrote, you are using a person as ornament — and
> people in this room may be descended from the people so used.

## What testimony is good at

It is authoritative on experience, on the texture of a day, and on what
official language concealed. It is weaker on dates, totals, and the sequence
of events distant from the speaker — memory reorders — and it is not
improved by the speaker's sincerity. Corroborate the checkable parts against
records, as [[Reading a Primary Source]] describes, and treat any conflict
as a finding rather than an embarrassment.

## Custodianship

Ask who holds the recording, on what terms, and who gave permission. Later
oral history projects usually have a written agreement between speaker and
archive, and it may restrict use. Honour it, and cite the custodian along
with the speaker — [[Citing Historical Sources]] shows the form.

Carry it into [[What Held the Union Together?]] and into
[[Historical Perspective]], where testimony is what stops an era being
described in the words of the people who ran it.
