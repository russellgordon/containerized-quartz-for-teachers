Most of what survives from the American past survives because a government
kept it. Statutes, treaties, executive orders, court decisions, census
returns, military service files, congressional debate, land patents,
commission reports, the minute book of a town meeting — this is the largest
single body of evidence in the course, and it is the state talking to
itself.

Hold on to that last point. These documents were not written for you, and
they were very often not written by the people they are about.

## How to read one

**Find the rule that made it exist.** Almost every government document was
required. A clerk filed a return because a statute obliged him to; a form
was completed because the form demanded it. The rule tells you what the
document had to contain — and therefore what its silences mean, which is
frequently nothing at all.

**Read the authority line.** Who signed, under what power, and who could
have refused. Executive Order 9066 is the standing example: two pages
authorising military commanders to designate areas from which "any or all
persons may be excluded," containing, as the National Archives points out,
neither the word "Japanese" nor the word "internment." The authority is
general; the application was not.

**Translate the vocabulary, and then keep the original.** Administrative
language exists to make decisions sound procedural. "Removal,"
"relocation," "assembly center," "allotment," "exclusion," "compromise" —
each conceals an action. Say what happened, and quote the file's own word,
because the word is evidence too.

## What it can and cannot tell you

It can tell you what was decided, when, by whom, on whose authority, and in
what words the state was willing to put in writing. That is a great deal,
and it is checkable.

It cannot tell you whether the policy worked, what it felt like, or what the
people subject to it thought. For that you need the press, community
publications, the record of protest, and [[Testimony and Narrative]].

> [!info] Where these records live
> The National Archives publishes images and transcripts of the Declaration
> of Independence, the Constitution and the Bill of Rights at
> [archives.gov/founding-docs](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs),
> noting that the Rotunda for the Charters of Freedom "is the permanent home
> of the original" three. Its **Milestone Documents** set runs from 1754 to
> 1965 and includes the Louisiana Purchase Treaty, the Emancipation
> Proclamation, the Thirteenth Amendment, the Chinese Exclusion Act,
> Executive Order 9066, the Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act, each
> with an image and a short note. Everything described here was reachable in
> August 2026; more addresses are in [[Where the Records Live]].

The reading method is in [[Reading a Primary Source]]; the citation rules,
which matter more for archival material than for anything else you will use,
are in [[Citing Historical Sources]].
