A number in a table looks like a fact that fell out of the world. It is
nothing of the kind. Somebody decided what to count, who would do the
counting, which boxes the form would have, and what to do with the people
who did not fit — and every one of those decisions is a historical source in
its own right.

The American census is the best example available to you, because its
purpose is written into the country's founding law. The Census Bureau's own
history explains that after independence there was an immediate need for a
national count because "both the number of seats each state was to have in
the U.S. House of Representatives, and the states' respective shares in
paying for the war were to be based on population." A census is not
curiosity. It is representation and taxation.

## The form is the argument

Read the questions before you read the answers. Article I of the
Constitution, whose text the National Archives publishes in full, set out
the original rule for apportionment: free persons counted in full,
"excluding Indians not taxed," and "three fifths of all other Persons" — a
formula that converted enslaved people into representation for the states
that held them, while giving them none. That is not a footnote to the
statistics. It is the statistics.

> [!question] Four questions for any historical table
> **Who was counted, and by whose definition?** Race, occupation, household
> and nationality categories change between censuses, so a series that looks
> continuous may not be. **Who did the counting?** Enumerators walked, asked,
> and sometimes guessed. **What happened to people the categories did not
> fit?** They were assigned somewhere, and the assignment is evidence.
> **What was the number used for?** Apportionment, taxation, quotas and
> policing all shape what gets collected and how carefully.

## Where the numbers are, and what they are

Individual returns and published aggregates are two different things and
live in two different places. The Census Bureau states that individual
census records from 1790 to 1950 are maintained by the National Archives;
the National Archives adds that "because of a 72-year restriction on access
to the Census, the most recent year available is 1950," that schedules from
1790 to 1950 have mostly been digitised, and that the 1950 census "was
released on April 1, 2022" and can be searched at **1950census.archives.gov**.

For aggregates across the whole span of this course, the standard compendium
is the Census Bureau's *Bicentennial Edition: Historical Statistics of the
United States, Colonial Times to 1970*, published in September 1975 in two
parts, carrying more than 12,500 time series and downloadable free as PDF
chapters from census.gov. Everything named here was reachable in August 2026.

## Using a figure honestly

Give the source, the year of collection, and the unit. Prefer rates to raw
totals when populations differ, and say which denominator you used. When two
published figures disagree, say so and explain why they might — different
definitions, different years, different coverage — rather than picking the
one that suits your paragraph.
