**The final evaluation. Three hours, in the examination period.**
Individual.

Unseen documents, and the whole of American history.

## What it looks like

You are given a dossier of five or six primary sources on a theme you
have studied — but sources you have not seen — spanning more than one
period of the course. They will disagree with each other, and the
disagreement is the point.

The theme is drawn from a short list announced in advance: technology
and the shape of American life; the United States beyond its own
borders; who counts as a citizen. Which one you get, you find out in the
room.

You answer in three parts.

1. **Read the evidence** (about 45 minutes). For each document: what it
   is, who made it, for whom, and what it is evidence of. Then say where
   two of them conflict, and what could explain the conflict besides one
   of them being wrong.
2. **Explain** (about 75 minutes). Using the documents and your
   knowledge of the course, account for what was happening and why. A
   strong answer crosses periods, because the dossier will.
3. **Judge** (about 45 minutes). A question is posed that the documents
   do not settle. Answer it, give your reasoning, and say what further
   evidence you would want and where you would look for it.

The remaining time is for reading and planning. Use it.

## What is being examined

| Criterion | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| Source criticism | Provenance and purpose established quickly and accurately |
| Corroboration | The documents used against each other rather than in a list |
| Knowledge | Course knowledge deployed where the documents fall silent |
| Concepts | Significance, cause and consequence, continuity and change, and perspective used to reason |
| Judgement | A defensible answer, with its uncertainty stated |

## Preparing for it

The three review periods work old dossiers against the clock. Bring
[[Your Inquiry Notebook]] — the source work you did in September is the
best preparation there is, and rereading your own first attempt is the
fastest way to see how much sharper you have become.
[[Skills You Are Building]] lists what the three parts are actually
testing.

> [!tip] Establish provenance before you read for content
> Who made this, when, and why — thirty seconds per document, before you
> read a word for meaning. Every year the strongest papers do this and
> the weakest ones do not. See [[Reading a Primary Source]] and
> [[Interpretation and Historiography]].
