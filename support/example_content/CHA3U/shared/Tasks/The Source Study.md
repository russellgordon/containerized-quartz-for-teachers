**Due: the third week of Unit 1.** Individual. About 600 words.

One primary source made before 1791, read properly. This is the task
that teaches the habit everything else in the course depends on.

## Choosing a source

A colonial charter, a page from a merchant's ledger, a treaty text, a
missionary's report, a runaway advertisement, a sermon, a probate
inventory, a map, an engraving, a muster roll, the minutes of a council.
It must be **primary** — made during the period, by someone with a
reason to make it — and you must be able to say where it is held.

## The problem you cannot walk past

Nearly every written source surviving from this period about Native
American nations was produced by Europeans: soldiers, traders,
missionaries, colonial officials. The people being described did not
write these documents and in most cases never saw them.

That is not a caution to add in your last paragraph. It is the
analytical problem of this task. A missionary's account of a council is
thin evidence about what that council decided and excellent evidence
about how a mission understood what it was watching. Say which of those
you are doing, in so many words.

If your source was made by or with a Native American nation — an oral
tradition recorded later, a treaty carrying clan marks, an object with a
documented history — trace how it reached the form you are reading, and
name who handled it on the way.

## What you produce

1. **The source, described.** What it physically is, who made it, when,
   for whom, and why. If you cannot establish one of those, say so.
2. **What it says**, including what it assumes and never argues for.
3. **What it does not say**, and who is absent from it.
4. **Whose account this is**, and what that does to every claim in it.
5. **One point of comparison.** Name one way the community in your
   source differed from another community of the period, and say where
   you learned that.
6. **What it is evidence of.** The heart of the task — and rarely the
   same as what it is *about*.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Provenance | Maker, date, audience, and purpose established or honestly flagged |
| Reading | You notice what the source assumes as well as what it states |
| Standpoint | Whose account this is, treated as analysis rather than apology |
| Evidence | You distinguish what it proves from what it merely suggests |
| Citation | Properly referenced, per [[Citing Historical Sources]] |

> [!warning] "Is it biased?" is the weakest question you can ask
> Every source was made by someone with reasons, and saying so tells the
> reader nothing. The question that pays is **reliable for what** — see
> [[Reading a Primary Source]].

[[Before 1492]] is what your source is being read against, and
[[Where the Records Live]] tells you which archives you can actually
reach from here.
