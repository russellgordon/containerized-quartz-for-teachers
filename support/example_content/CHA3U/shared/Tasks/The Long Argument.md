**The culminating task. Eight periods at the end of Unit 4, ending with
a presentation to a real audience.** Pairs or threes.

Some American arguments never finish. They are settled and then
reopened, in different words, by different people, in every generation.
Take one of them, follow it the whole length of the course, and say
where it stands now.

## The arguments on offer

- How much power the federal government should have, and over what.
- Who counts as a citizen, and what citizenship entitles you to.
- Race and rights: what equality before the law was taken to mean.
- Who may come, who may stay, and who decides.
- What the country owes, or claims, beyond its own borders.

You may propose your own, agreed with me first. It must be arguable in
all four periods — not merely mentionable in them.

## The eight periods

| Period | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Choosing, and narrowing until the argument is a single question |
| 2 | Scoping: what evidence from each period would actually answer it |
| 3 | Evidence: precontact to 1791, and 1791 to 1877 |
| 4 | Evidence: 1877 to 1945 |
| 5 | Evidence: since 1945, including who is making the argument now |
| 6 | Drafting: the through-line, and the place where it breaks |
| 7 | Rehearsal, and a hostile question from another group |
| 8 | Delivery to your audience, with questions taken live |

## What you deliver

- **A brief** of about 1,200 words: the question, its shape in each
  period with evidence, and where it stands today.
- **A presentation** of twelve minutes to an audience outside this class
  — another class, a library or historical society programme, a
  community organisation, or a recording published somewhere real.
- **A one-page leave-behind** that your audience keeps.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Question | One question, recognisably the same one in all four periods |
| Evidence | Sourced from every period, weighed, and cited |
| Synthesis | A through-line argued, including where it breaks or reverses |
| The present | The current state of the argument established from evidence, not opinion |
| Delivery | Twelve minutes pitched at the audience you actually have |

> [!tip]- What a through-line is, and what it is not
> Try writing your question in the same words for each period. If you
> have to change the words to make it fit one, you have two arguments
> rather than one — narrow it and start again.
> Then find the period where the pattern breaks: goes quiet, reverses,
> or is settled and then unsettled. Explaining that break is worth more
> than a smooth story, because a smooth story about four centuries is
> almost always one with the inconvenient period left out.

[[Historical Significance]] and [[Continuity and Change]] do the work
here; [[Building an Argument]] and [[Using Evidence]] are the craft. The
seminars [[Who Counts as American|Who Counts as American?]] and [[Is the United States an Empire|Is the United States an Empire?]]
are where two of these arguments have already been opened, and
[[Where History Leads]] is worth rereading before you choose.
