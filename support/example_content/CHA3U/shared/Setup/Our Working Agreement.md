This course studies slavery and the trade in human beings, the dispossession
and forced removal of Native nations, racial terror and lynching,
segregation in law and in practice, and the wartime incarceration of people
of Japanese ancestry. That is not a warning label attached to the course. It
is the course.

Students in this room have family history in some of it, usually without
saying so. Everything below follows from that.

## How we argue about the past

- **Evidence, not assertion.** You are entitled to your own view and not to
  your own facts. Name your source when you state one.
- **Disagree with the claim, not the person.** "That report was written by
  the agency being investigated" is an argument; dismissal is not.
- **Let people finish**, particularly when you are certain they are wrong,
  and **change your mind out loud** when the evidence moves you.
- **Nobody speaks for a group they do not belong to**, and you are never the
  class representative of your own background.

## This is not a spectacle

We study another country's history, from Canada, and that carries two
temptations worth naming on the first day.

The first is distance: treating American violence as a show to be watched
because it happened somewhere else. The people in these documents were not
characters. Read them as you would want the record of your own family read.

The second is comparison. Comparing the United States with Canada is welcome
and often necessary — the two countries share a border, a continent's
Indigenous nations, and a great deal of law. But a comparison is a historical
claim and needs evidence on both sides. "It was worse there" is not a
finding; it is a feeling, and in this room it will be asked for its sources
like any other claim.

## Precision is a form of respect

Where a figure is contested, we use what a responsible body published, with
its own wording attached. On the wartime incarceration of Japanese
Americans, the National Archives states that approximately 122,000 men,
women and children were forcibly moved after Executive Order 9066, nearly
70,000 of them American citizens; the National Park Service, writing about
Manzanar, states that the government ordered more than 110,000 people from
their homes into ten camps. Two federal bodies, two figures, both published.
Say which you are using and why. Rounded numbers that travel through social
media are not evidence.

## What I commit to

- **Advance notice** before the lessons that deal most directly with
  atrocity, in time for you to prepare or to arrange something else.
- **No surprise images.** Photographs of atrocity are used sparingly, named
  before they appear, and never to produce a reaction.
- **Testimony with its provenance.** The people in these records are
  sources, not illustrations: we say who spoke, to whom, when, and on what
  terms — see [[Testimony and Narrative]].
