History here is not a story you are told and repeat back. It is an argument
built from evidence, about a past whose own participants disagreed with each
other — and in this course, about a country that has never stopped arguing
about itself in public.

## A normal period

1. **A source.** A document, a photograph, a set of figures, a recorded
   voice. You meet it before it is explained.
2. **An argument.** Usually in threes, about what the source can and cannot
   show. "How do you know?" is the standard question here, and it is not an
   accusation.
3. **A judgement.** You commit to a position, in writing, with the evidence
   attached — and you say what would change your mind.

Naming the concept comes last. That is why [[Historical Significance]] and
[[Cause and Consequence]] assume you have already met the idea in practice.

## What the course asks of you

- **Read before class.** Most periods start from a source you were meant to
  have seen; arriving without it means catching up on what everyone else is
  already arguing about.
- **Separate what a source says from what you conclude.** That distinction
  is most of the discipline.
- **Name your source every time you state a fact.**
- **Represent fairly the positions you disagree with.** It is marked.

## The shape of the year

Four units, following the curriculum's own periods: to 1791, 1791 to 1877,
1877 to 1945, and 1945 to the present. Each asks the same three questions of
a different era — the conditions people lived under, the conflicts and
cooperation between groups, and the effect on identity, citizenship and
heritage.

The year ends with [[The Long Argument]]: one question about the American
past that is still being argued about now, investigated properly and settled
with evidence rather than with volume. Then [[The Document Examination]], on
sources you have not seen before.

> [!note] The hardest lessons are flagged in advance
> This course studies slavery, dispossession and removal, racial terror,
> internment and segregation, and you will be told before the lessons that
> deal with them most directly. [[Our Working Agreement]] sets out what you
> and I each commit to — read it in the first week, not the week you need
> it.

## If you fall behind

Say so early. [[Getting Help]] lists every way to reach me and
[[Help Sessions]] has the times I am in the room. A missed source is a
ten-minute fix in week two and a real problem in week ten.
