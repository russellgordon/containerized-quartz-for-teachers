One notebook, kept from the first week of the course to the last. It is not
a set of class notes and it is not a diary. It is the running record of what
you asked, what you found, where you found it, and how your thinking moved —
and it is the reason the culminating inquiry will not start from nothing.

Start it now, in Unit 1, while the questions are still small.

## What an entry holds

| Part | What goes in it | Why it earns its place |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The question | One sentence, in question form | [[Building an Argument]] cannot begin until the question is decided |
| The source | Author, title, date, repository, reference number | You will need it months later; see [[Citing Historical Sources\|how to record it]] |
| What it says | A short quotation or a figure, copied exactly | Paraphrasing too early loses the wording you will want |
| What it does not say | The gap, stated plainly | Half of historical judgement is knowing what the record omits |
| So what | Two sentences of your own thinking | This is the part nobody else can write for you |

Four of the five are mechanical. The fifth is the work.

## Three kinds of question

The curriculum asks you to "formulate different types of questions," and its
own examples for this course are worth copying into the front of the
notebook: a factual question — "What was the Monroe Doctrine?"; a
comparative question about the treatment of African Americans before the
abolition of slavery and during the Jim Crow era; and a causal question
about which criteria should determine the most important causes of the
American Revolution.

Label every entry with which kind you are asking, because a question that
changes type halfway through is the commonest way an inquiry stalls. A
factual question wants a document. A comparative question wants two cases
held to the same standard. A causal question wants a sequence you can
defend.

## Recording how your thinking changed

This is the entry students skip and the one worth most. When a source moves
you off a position, write three things: what you believed, what you read,
and what you believe now. Date it. By the end of the year you will have a
record of your own reasoning that you could not reconstruct from memory.

If nothing has moved you all term, that is a finding too — and it usually
means you have been reading sources that already agreed with you, which
[[Using Evidence]] calls corroboration that is not.

## What is marked

The notebook is assessed as a set, once, near the end of term, on
consistency, precision of sourcing, and evidence that your thinking actually
moved. It feeds [[Skills You Are Building]] and, in the end,
[[The Long Argument]] — where the question you settle on will very likely be
one you first wrote down in October.
