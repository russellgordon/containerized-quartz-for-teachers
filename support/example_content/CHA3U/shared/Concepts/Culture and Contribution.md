A new country argues with itself about what it is, and it does a good deal of
that arguing in poems, paintings, novels and newspapers. The cultural
production of this period is not decoration on top of the politics. Several
of these works changed what readers were willing to vote for, which makes
them evidence in a political history as much as an artistic one.

## Work that did something

| Work or movement | What it was doing |
| --- | --- |
| Hudson River School | Painting the landscape as sublime, and mostly unpeopled |
| Transcendentalism | Arguing that conscience outranks custom and, sometimes, law |
| Slave narratives | First-person testimony offered as evidence against denial |
| Spirituals | Worship, coded communication, and a musical tradition that outlived the system |
| *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, 1852 | Making the Fugitive Slave Act intolerable to readers who had ignored it |
| Civil War photography | Bringing the dead in front of civilians for the first time |

The landscape row rewards a second look. Those canvases present the continent
as empty wilderness at exactly the decades when it was being emptied. That is
not a conspiracy, but it is a choice, and it fed a story about vacant land
waiting for settlement.

The narratives row is the most contested at the time. Formerly enslaved
authors were routinely accused of exaggeration or of not having written their
own books, which is why so many were published with prefaces from white
sponsors vouching for them. Read the preface as part of the source: it tells
you what the author was expected to prove.

## Names, and what to do with them

The curriculum lists individuals from Andrew Jackson to Elizabeth Blackwell,
and the temptation is to convert the list into biographies. Resist it. Ask
instead what each person made possible or foreclosed for others. Frederick
Douglass turned his own life into an argument and then into a newspaper.
Sojourner Truth used a speaking platform that respectable opinion said she
should not have. Elizabeth Blackwell qualified in medicine in the 1840s and
then had to build institutions because existing ones would not employ her.
Clara Barton organized battlefield relief and afterward the work of
identifying the missing dead. Andrew Jackson expanded political participation
for white men and signed the removal of Native nations, and any honest
account of him holds both.

One name deserves particular care. The Lemhi Shoshone woman who travelled
with the Lewis and Clark expedition appears in the sources under several
spellings — Sacagawea, Sakakawea, Sacajawea — because it was written down by
men recording an unfamiliar language, and the nations concerned do not all
agree on the form. Say which spelling you are using and why. Getting a
person's name right is not a courtesy; it is accuracy.
