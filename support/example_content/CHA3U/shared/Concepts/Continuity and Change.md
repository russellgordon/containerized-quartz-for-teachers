The commonest weak sentence in a history essay is "things changed." Of
course they did. The concept asks the harder pair of questions: what changed,
what did not, and how you can tell the difference from the evidence in front
of you.

The curriculum puts it this way. The concept "requires students to determine
what has stayed the same and what has changed over a period of time," it can
be explored "with reference to ways of life, political policies, economic
practices, relationship with the environment, social values and beliefs, and
so on," and students "make judgements about continuity and change by making
comparisons between some point in the past and the present, or between two
points in the past."

That last clause is the instruction. A comparison needs **two dated points**
and **one thing being compared**. Miss either and you have an impression.

## Choose the two points before you write

Pick them because a document exists at each end. 1791 and 1877 are the outer
edges of Unit 2 for a reason: there is a constitutional text at one end and
a set of constitutional amendments at the other, so a claim about political
change can be shown rather than asserted.

## What to hold constant while you compare

| Dimension the curriculum names | The comparison it makes possible | The evidence it demands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ways of life | Whose day changed, whose did not | Household records, wages, testimony |
| Political policies | What the law permitted at each point | The statute or amendment, both dates |
| Economic practices | What was produced, by whom, for whose profit | Output figures, ledgers, census returns |
| Relationship with the environment | What land was used for, and by what claim | Surveys, land acts, maps |
| Social values and beliefs | What was sayable in public | Sermons, editorials, schoolbooks |

Work down one row at a time. A claim that a period was "transformed" usually
turns out to mean one row moved a long way and three did not.

## Change is not the same as progress

Change has a direction only if you supply one, and supplying one without
saying so is the commonest hidden argument in this subject. Say whether you
are measuring by law, by wealth, by who could vote, or by who was safe — and
expect the four measures to disagree. A legal change with no change in
practice is a finding, not a failure of your research.

## Continuity is a claim too

If you argue that something persisted, you owe evidence at both ends, not
just at the start. Persistence is easy to assume and surprisingly hard to
show, which is why [[Interpretation and Historiography]] spends its time on
who kept saying a thing stayed the same, and when.

Put it to work in [[What Did the Frontier Mean?]] and carry it into
[[Using Evidence]].
