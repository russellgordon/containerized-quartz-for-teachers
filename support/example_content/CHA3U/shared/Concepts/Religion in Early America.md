Colonial America is often described as founded on religious freedom. It is
more accurate, and more interesting, to say it was founded by people who
wanted freedom for their own religion and were frequently prepared to deny it
to others. Sorting out which was which is most of the work on this topic.

## Who came for what

- **Puritans in New England** left England to build a church they thought
  properly reformed, and a society governed accordingly. Membership carried
  political weight; dissent did not.
	- Roger Williams was banished from Massachusetts and founded Rhode
	  Island on the principle that the civil authority had no business
	  compelling belief — one of the earliest such arguments anywhere.
	- Anne Hutchinson was tried and expelled for teaching doctrine in her
	  own house, and the charges against her turned partly on her being a
	  woman doing it.
- **Quakers in Pennsylvania**, under William Penn, wrote toleration into the
  colony's founding, opened it to sects that were unwelcome elsewhere, and
  built a settlement whose founder actually paid the Lenape for land.
	- Quakers were persecuted elsewhere in the colonies, including hangings
	  in Massachusetts, and their pacifism made them suspect in wartime.
	- Quaker meetings later produced a disproportionate share of the first
	  organized opponents of slavery.
- **Catholics in Maryland** got a toleration statute in 1649 that protected
  Christians and prescribed death for denying the Trinity. When the colony's
  politics shifted, Catholics lost the vote and the right to worship openly.
- **Spanish missions** in New Mexico and later California were instruments of
  empire as much as of faith, and were resisted — the Pueblo Revolt of 1680
  drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for over a decade.

## Revival, and what it did to authority

From the 1730s a wave of revival preaching swept the colonies, later named
the Great Awakening. Itinerant preachers such as George Whitefield drew
enormous open-air crowds, preached conversion as a personal event, and
implied that an individual's experience could outrank an ordained minister's
authority.[^effect]

Among enslaved people, Christianity was often introduced as an instrument of
control and taken up as something else entirely — a text with an Exodus in
it. African spiritual traditions did not disappear on the crossing; they
persisted, blended and reshaped what was preached at them.

At the other end, several political leaders of the founding generation were
Deists or close to it, holding that reason revealed a creator and being
sceptical of revealed religion. A country containing both revival crowds and
Deist statesmen had a practical reason to keep the state out of the question.

[^effect]: That is the significance worth arguing: the Awakening trained
ordinary colonists to judge authority for themselves, in public, in large
numbers. Whether that habit transferred to politics a generation later is a
genuine historical question rather than a settled fact — make the case, do
not assume it.
