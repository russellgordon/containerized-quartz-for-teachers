A machine is not an event. It is a capability that arrives unevenly, is paid
for by someone, and changes different lives in opposite directions — which
is why "the telephone changed America" is a sentence with no history in it
yet.

The curriculum asks you to describe developments in science and technology
and analyse their impact on heritage and identity, and after 1945 to explain
their impact on Americans. Impact is the operative word, and impact is
always on somebody in particular.

## Four questions that turn a device into evidence

**Who could get it, and when?** Adoption is a curve, not a date. A city
apartment and a farm at the end of a dirt road did not receive electricity,
a telephone, or a paved road in the same decade, and the gap is usually the
most interesting fact available.

**Who paid, and who was paid?** Behind an assembly line is a wage, a shift
length, and a person doing the same motion for nine hours. Behind a
household appliance is a claim about whose labour it saved and whether that
person's day actually got shorter.

**What work did it destroy?** Every labour-saving device is somebody's job
description ending. Name the trade before you praise the machine.

**What did it let the state or a company do?** Records, identification,
broadcasting, and weapons all extend a capability that somebody directs.[^scope]

[^scope]: This is the question that connects Unit 3 to Unit 4. The same
    analysis applies whether the technology is a card-punch tabulator, a
    radio network, a national television broadcast, or a database — the
    historical question is not what the device could do but who was in a
    position to point it at whom, and with what authority.

## Culture follows capability, but not immediately

Motion pictures, sound recording and radio did more than entertain: they
made it possible for a person in one place to know what a person a thousand
miles away sounded like. That is a change in the raw material of a national
identity, and it is why the curriculum keeps technology and identity in the
same expectation rather than in separate ones. Whether the result was one
shared culture or a broadcast version of one region's culture is exactly the
argument [[Arts, Culture, and Consumer Society]] takes up.

## Writing about it without exaggerating

Give a date for availability, not for invention. Say who is in your claim —
"for households on rural routes," "for workers in the plant," "for the
company" — and give the evidence a boundary. A well-bounded claim about one
county beats a national claim you cannot support.

Sources are unusually good here: advertisements, catalogues, patent records,
company photographs, and the census returns discussed in
[[Statistics and the Census]]. Read them the way
[[Reading a Primary Source]] sets out, remembering that an advertisement is
excellent evidence about persuasion and poor evidence about ownership.
