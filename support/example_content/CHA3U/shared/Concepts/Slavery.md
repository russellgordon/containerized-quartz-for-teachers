Slavery in the United States was a legal system, an economic system, and a
system of violence, and it worked because all three reinforced one another.
It is not a dark chapter set apart from the main story. It is inside the main
story — in the Constitution, in the banks, in the westward expansion.

## What the law did

American slavery was racial, heritable and lifelong. A Virginia statute of
1662 made a child's status follow the mother's, so enslavement passed down
through generations and the children born of enslavers' sexual violence
increased their property. Slave codes restricted movement, assembly,
literacy, property, and the ability to testify against a white person.

The Constitution protected slavery without naming it: three-fifths of the
enslaved counted toward a state's representation, escaped people were to be
returned, and Congress could not touch the international trade until 1808.
Yet the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 had already written the formula that
would end it — "There shall be neither slavery nor involuntary servitude in
the said territory, otherwise than in the punishment of crimes whereof the
party shall have been duly convicted." Hold that clause.

## What it was worth, and to whom

After 1808 the trade inside the country grew, moving enslaved people from the
Upper South to the cotton lands of the Deep South. The mechanism was sale,
and the sale of one person is the destruction of a family. Enslaved people
were also collateral for loans and assets on balance sheets, and the cotton
they grew fed mills in England and New England alike; no account in which the
North was a bystander survives the financial records.

Expansion made this the central political question, because each new
territory set the balance of power in the Senate — which is why *Dred Scott*
in 1857, holding that Black Americans could not be citizens and that Congress
could not bar slavery from the territories, closed the political exits.

> [!warning] Handle the figures and the words with care
> The 1860 census counted **31,443,321** people in the United States. The
> enslaved were enumerated on a separate schedule recording age, sex and
> colour under the name of the person claiming them, and in most cases **not
> their names at all**. Cite that schedule and its date rather than a rounded
> figure, and say plainly that it was built to count property, not to
> identify people. Write *enslaved person*, not *slave*: property was the
> legal fiction the system ran on. See [[Statistics and the Census]].

## Refusal, in every form available

Resistance was constant and calculated against the odds of survival. Armed
revolt was rare and lethally punished: after Nat Turner's rising in Virginia
in 1831, southern legislatures tightened restrictions on literacy, assembly
and free Black residents rather than loosening anything. Far more common was
refusal that could be survived — working slowly, feigning illness, holding
families and worship together where the law recognised neither, and running,
with Harriet Tubman going back into country where her own capture was worth
money. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 then compelled northern citizens to
assist in captures, putting the argument on every doorstep.

## Ended, and not ended

The Emancipation Proclamation of 1 January 1863 declared enslaved people in
the rebelling states free but, in the National Archives' summary, "applied
only to states that had seceded from the Union, leaving slavery untouched in
the loyal border states," and depended "upon Union military victory."
Enslaved people made it real by leaving, in enormous numbers, and enlisting.

The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified 6 December 1865, abolished slavery, with
the clause borrowed from 1787: "except as a punishment for crime whereof the
party shall have been duly convicted." Southern states then wrote Black Codes
and leased convicts. Follow that into [[Jim Crow and Resistance]], and build
the argument in [[Slavery and the Nation]].
