Winning a war is one problem. Building a government that will not collapse or
turn into what you just overthrew is a different and harder one, and the
Americans took fifteen years over it. The interesting history here is not the
fighting. It is the sequence of documents, each written to fix a defect in
the one before.

## Four documents, in order

The **Declaration of Independence** of 4 July 1776 was an argument before it
was an announcement: a theory of legitimate government, a list of grievances
against the King, and a claim that a people may replace a ruler who breaks
the compact. Its most quoted sentence asserts an equality that the same
society denied to most of the people in it.

The **Articles of Confederation** made a league of sovereign states. Congress
could ask for money and troops but could not compel either, could not
regulate trade between the states, and could not amend the Articles without
unanimity. It won the war and then proved unable to pay for it.

The **Constitution**, drafted at Philadelphia in 1787, created a government
that acted directly on citizens rather than through the states. It divided
power three ways and set the branches against each other deliberately. It
also entrenched slavery without using the word — counting three-fifths of
"other persons" toward representation, requiring the return of people who
escaped bondage, and barring Congress from ending the international slave
trade for twenty years.

The **Bill of Rights**, ratified in 1791, was the price of ratification: the
list of things the new government was forbidden to do, demanded by those who
feared it would become the thing they had fought.

> "Assess their significance for different groups" is the actual
> instruction, and the answer changes group by group. For property-owning
> white men, these documents produced a genuine republic with real
> participation. For enslaved people, they produced a constitution that
> protected the system holding them. For Native nations, they produced a
> government with a stronger claim on western land than the Crown had ever
> managed to enforce. For women, they changed almost nothing in law. All

## The people and the symbols

Some names in this period were made famous by the Revolution; others made
arguments the Revolution refused. Phillis Wheatley, enslaved in Boston,
published a book of poetry in London in 1773 and was examined by prominent
Bostonians who doubted an African woman could have written it. Benjamin
Banneker, born free in Maryland, compiled almanacs and wrote to Thomas
Jefferson pressing him on the gap between his words and his slaveholding.
Alexander McGillivray negotiated for the Muscogee with Spain and with the new
United States, playing one against the other as long as it could be done.

Symbols do work too, and they keep working. The Boston Tea Party of 1773 has
been claimed by movements ever since as a licence for resistance to taxation.
Ask what such a claim needs to leave out, and take it to
[[Was the Revolution Inevitable?]].
