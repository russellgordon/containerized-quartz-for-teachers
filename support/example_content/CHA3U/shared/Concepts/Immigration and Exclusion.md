A country that describes itself as a nation of immigrants has also written
some of the most detailed exclusion law in the world. Both facts are in the
statute books, and the point of this page is that they are not a
contradiction to be resolved but a pattern to be explained: admission and
exclusion are the same policy, deciding who counts.

## Read the law, not the slogan

The Chinese Exclusion Act is the case to learn first, because the record is
precise. The National Archives records that it was "approved on May 6,
1882," signed by President Chester A. Arthur, and "provided an absolute
10-year ban on Chinese laborers immigrating to the United States." When it
expired in 1892 Congress extended it as the Geary Act; the extension was
"made permanent in 1902," adding a requirement that "each Chinese resident
… register and obtain a certificate of residence." Congress repealed the
exclusion acts in 1943, "when China was a member of the Allied Nations
during World War II" — and left a quota of 105 Chinese immigrants a year.

Four things worth noticing in that paragraph, all of them checkable.

**The category is written into the law.** Not "immigrants" but "laborers" of
one origin: the statute does the sorting.

**Exclusion escalates through registration.** The 1902 certificate turns a
border rule into a system for policing people already living there.

**Repeal came with a war and an alliance**, not with a change of mind about
the category.

**A quota of 105 is not an opening.** Ending a ban and admitting people are
different acts, and the number is the evidence.

> [!warning] The vocabulary is part of the evidence
> Immigration law is written in words that make sorting sound technical:
> "quota," "national origins," "assimilability," "public charge,"
> "illegal." Quote the term the statute uses, then say plainly what it did
> to a person trying to enter or stay. Never adopt a period's category as
> your own description of a human being; the curriculum's own examples name
> the Emergency Quota Act of 1921 and the Immigration Act of 1924, and both
> reward this treatment. Students in this room have families who have met
> such a category on a form.

## The long arc, and how to argue it

The curriculum's list for the years after 1945 — the Displaced Persons Act
of 1948, the Hart-Cellar Act of 1965, policy on immigration without status —
invites the obvious claim that exclusion ended and openness followed. Test
it rather than assert it. Compare what each statute said, who it admitted in
practice, and who continued to do agricultural and domestic work under rules
that made complaint impossible.

Take the numbers from [[Statistics and the Census]], the wording from
[[Government Records]], and the argument into [[Who Counts as American|Who Counts as American?]].
