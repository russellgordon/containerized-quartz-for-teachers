This is the concept most often quoted to shut an argument down, and it does
not do that. Read the definition carefully, because the second half is the
part people forget.

The curriculum says the concept "requires students to analyse past actions,
events, developments, and issues within the context of the time in which
they occurred," meaning "the social, cultural, political, economic, and
intellectual context, and the personal values and beliefs, that shaped
people's lives and actions." Students "need to be conscious of not imposing
today's values and ethical standards on the past." And then: students "also
learn that, in any given historical period, people may have diverse
perspectives on the same event, development, or issue."

## Two obligations, not one

**Reconstruct the context.** What did this person know, when, and what
options did they actually have? A decision looks different once you know
what the alternatives were and how long the news took to arrive.

**Refuse the single mind of an age.** There is no such thing as what
"people at the time" thought. Somebody at the time disagreed, usually in
writing, and very often the person disagreeing was the person the decision
was being made about. If your evidence for a period's beliefs comes only
from the people in charge of it, you have found the perspective of the
governing class and mislabelled it the perspective of an era.

## The move this concept does not license

"It was normal then" is a description, not a defence, and the curriculum
does not ask you to supply one. Explaining why a policy made sense to those
who made it is history. Concluding that it therefore cannot be judged is a
separate claim, and you would have to argue for it — against the people
alive at the time who judged it and said so.

> [!example]- What contemporaries disagreeing looks like (click to expand)
> When the Ontario document illustrates this concept, one of the sample
> questions it prints for this course is: "How did colonists view various
> Native American nations during this period? Did all colonists have the
> same view? If not, what factors might account for the differences?" The
> question assumes disagreement and asks you to explain its pattern. Try the
> same shape on any period in this course: name two people who lived through
> it and did not agree, then account for the difference by their position,
> interest, and information rather than by their character.

## How it shows up in your writing

Date the belief. "By the 1850s, in the northern press" is a historical
claim; "back then" is not. Quote the period's own words for things, then
translate them — the wording is evidence about the speaker, and your
translation is evidence that you understood it.

Practise it in [[Testimony and Narrative]] and in
[[Could the Civil War Have Been Avoided|Could the Civil War Have Been Avoided?]], where the strongest positions
are the ones argued in 1858 rather than invented now.
