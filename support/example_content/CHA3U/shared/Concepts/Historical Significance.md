American history arrives pre-sorted. Some events are famous before you meet
them, and fame is not the same thing as importance. Deciding what actually
mattered, and saying on what grounds, is the whole of this concept.

The Ontario curriculum states it plainly. Historical significance "requires
students to determine the importance of something (e.g., an issue, event,
development, person, place, interaction, etc.) in the past," and "historical
importance is determined generally by the impact of something on a group of
people and whether its effects are long lasting."

Read that twice. It carries two tests, and the paragraph it comes from adds
two more that students routinely skip.

## Four tests, in the Ministry's own words

**Impact on a group of people.** How many, and how deeply? A measure that
changed where a family could live, what they were paid, or whether they
could vote outweighs one that filled a month of newspapers and then stopped.

**Whether its effects are long lasting.** Did anything still differ ten
years later? Fifty? The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 imposed what the
National Archives calls "an absolute 10-year ban on Chinese laborers
immigrating to the United States"; Congress extended it in 1892, made it
permanent in 1902, and repealed the exclusion acts only in 1943. Sixty-one
years is durability, and you can show it.

**Significant for whom.** The curriculum is blunt: "something that is
historically significant for one group may not be significant for another."
The same decade is a frontier opening to one group and a removal to another.

**Relevance now.** Significance "may also be determined by the relevance of
something from the past and how it connects to a current issue or event."
That is not permission to judge the past by today's standards — see
[[Historical Perspective]] — but it is permission to say why a thing still
matters.

> [!question] The question to put to a source
> **Who was affected by this, how deeply, for how long — and who is saying
> so?** Ask it of one document at a time. The Emancipation Proclamation is
> the standing lesson: the National Archives records that it applied only to
> states that had seceded, expressly exempted parts of the Confederacy
> already under Union control, and that "the freedom it promised depended
> upon Union military victory." Its fame and its immediate legal reach are
> two different measurements, and the gap between them is where an argument
> lives.

## Significance is argued, not discovered

No source announces its own importance. You assign it, using criteria you
can state out loud, and a reader should be able to disagree with your
criteria rather than guess at them. Write them down before you rank
anything.

That is the move [[Building an Argument]] runs on, and the one
[[Whose Story Gets Taught?]] asks you to make about a curriculum, including
this one.
