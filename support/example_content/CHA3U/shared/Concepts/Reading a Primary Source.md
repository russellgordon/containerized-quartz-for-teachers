A primary source is not a window onto the past. It is an object somebody
made, for a reason, for someone, at a moment — and every one of those four
facts changes what it can be used to prove.

The curriculum asks you to assess credibility "by considering emphasis and
omission as well as the perspective, bias, accuracy, purpose, and context of
the source and the values and expertise of its author." Below is the order
that works.

## Six questions, in the order they pay off

**Who made it, and what did they know?** Expertise is not authority. A clerk
copying returns and a general writing a memoir are unreliable in opposite
directions.

**For whom?** A letter to a brother, a report to a superior, and a printed
appeal to strangers say different things about the same afternoon.

**Why?** Almost every document was made to produce an effect: to persuade,
to comply with a rule, to be on the record, to sell something.

**When, relative to the events?** Same-day, same-year and
thirty-years-later are three different kinds of evidence, and the last is
evidence about memory as much as about the event.

**What is emphasised, and what is missing?** The curriculum names emphasis
and omission first for a reason. A silence is not automatically meaningful —
a form only records what its boxes ask for — but a silence in a document
that had every reason to speak is a finding.

**What can this actually establish?** Say the narrowest true thing before
you say the interesting one.

## Annotate on the page

Mark up a printed copy every time, and copy this template into
[[Your Inquiry Notebook]] before you write a sentence of argument.

```
SOURCE
  Creator ......... (what were they in a position to know?)
  Date made ....... (and date of the events described)
  Audience ........ (who was meant to read or see this?)
  Purpose ......... (what effect was it made to produce?)
  Found at ........ (repository, collection, number, date seen)

READING
  Exact words ..... (copy them; do not paraphrase yet)
  Emphasis ........ (what it spends its space on)
  Omission ........ (what it had reason to say and did not)

USE
  It can show ..... (the narrow, defensible claim)
  It cannot show .. (the claim it is often used for)
  Next source ..... (the one that would test it)
```

The last three lines turn a source into an argument.

## Corroborate before you commit

One document establishes what one document says. Two made independently,
agreeing on a detail neither had reason to invent, is evidence. Two copied
from a third are one document. Take the method into [[Government Records]]
and [[Photographs]], then use what you find in [[Building an Argument]].
