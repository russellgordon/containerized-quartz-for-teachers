States do not act abroad and then fall silent about it. Every intervention,
purchase, annexation and war arrives with a reason attached, published for a
domestic audience, and the reason is a document you can study exactly as you
would study any other. This page is about reading the justification and the
action as two separate pieces of evidence.

Start with the one everybody cites. On 2 December 1823, in what the National
Archives describes as "President James Monroe's seventh annual message to
Congress," the United States declared that "the American continents … are
henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any
European powers." Note the form: not a treaty, not a law, but a passage in
an annual message. It acquired its authority afterwards, from being invoked.

## The justification is a source about the audience

A doctrine tells you what its authors believed would persuade the people at
home. That is a fact about American politics whether or not it is a fact
about the world. So ask of each justification: who had to be convinced, and
what were they anxious about?

| The justification offered | What it claims | What to check in the record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Security | We act because we are threatened | When the threat was first named, and by whom |
| Trade and investment | Our commerce must be protected | Whose property, and who profited afterwards |
| Sphere of influence | This region is properly ours to order | Whether the region's own governments agreed |
| Liberation | We act on behalf of a people | What that people asked for, in their own sources |
| Non-entanglement | We stay out of others' quarrels | What was still being done through trade and loans |

Two of those can be true at once. Historians argue about weighting, not
about whether motives come singly — see [[Cause and Consequence]].

## Continuity is the harder claim

It is tempting to draw a straight line from 1823 through the Roosevelt
Corollary, the Panama Canal, and the Cold War, and call it one policy. The
line is real enough to be worth arguing about, but a policy invoked in
different decades for different purposes is not the same policy. Test any
such line the way [[Continuity and Change]] requires: two dated points, one
thing compared, evidence at both ends.

## What to do with the gap

When the stated reason and the action do not match, you have three
possibilities and must choose between them with evidence: the reason was a
cover, the reason was sincere and the action outran it, or the reason was
sincere and the situation changed. The third is the one students never
consider, and it is often the right one.

Argue it in [[Is the United States an Empire|Is the United States an Empire?]], and set the country's
account of itself beside its record in [[Government Records]].
