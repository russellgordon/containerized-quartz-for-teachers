The curriculum's requirement here is short — use appropriate terminology
when communicating the results of your investigations, both the vocabulary
of your topic and the vocabulary of the concepts of historical thinking. In
American history that requirement does more work than it looks like, because
so much of the standard vocabulary was coined by one side of a live argument
and has been quietly carrying that argument ever since.

## Three kinds of word, and what to do with each

**Technical terms** — primary source, corroboration, provenance,
significance, continuity, perspective — are precise, and using them
correctly is a mark of competence. Use them exactly, or not at all.

**Period terms** are the words people used at the time: "removal,"
"relocation," "compromise," "reconstruction," "exclusion," "the negro
question," "manifest destiny." These belong in quotation marks, attributed
and dated, because they are evidence. Adopting one as your own description
is not neutrality; it is agreeing with whoever coined it.

**Loaded shorthand** is the vocabulary that has arrived since, often from
politics rather than scholarship. It is not forbidden, but it must be
defined before use and defended if challenged.

> [!abstract]- A worked example of the difference (click to expand)
> "Manifest destiny" was a nineteenth-century phrase asserting that a
> particular expansion was both inevitable and right. A student who writes
> *manifest destiny drove settlers west* has adopted the claim. A student
> who writes *contemporaries justified expansion by appealing to what they
> called manifest destiny, a phrase that made a political choice sound like
> a prophecy* has reported it and analysed it in the same sentence — and has
> also, incidentally, demonstrated [[Historical Perspective]] without
> announcing the concept by name.

## Precision about people

Name groups as they name themselves, and use the specific name where you
have it: a nation rather than a region, a person rather than a category.
Where the record uses a slur or an administrative label, quote it once with
its source and then use accurate language for the rest of your work. Where
terminology is contested and both usages are current, say which you have
chosen and why, in a sentence, and then be consistent.

## Hedges that mean something, and hedges that do not

"Suggests," "indicates," "is consistent with," and "the record does not
show" are useful because they measure your confidence. "Arguably,"
"clearly," "obviously," and "most historians agree" measure nothing — the
first two are volume, and the last one is a citation you have not made.

## Before you submit

Read your own work looking only for adopted vocabulary: any word that a
person in the period would have used approvingly and that you have used
flatly. That single pass improves more papers than any other edit, and it is
the last check before [[Building an Argument]] becomes a finished piece.
