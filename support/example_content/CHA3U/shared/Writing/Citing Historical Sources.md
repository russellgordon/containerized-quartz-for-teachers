A citation is not a penalty for using someone else's work. It is the
instruction that lets a reader go and check you — and in history, where two
researchers can read the same file and reach different conclusions, it is
the whole basis on which anyone believes you.

The curriculum asks for "accepted forms of documentation," naming footnotes
or endnotes, author-date citations, reference lists, bibliographies and
credits, and expects them for "archival sources, articles, art works, blogs,
books, films or videos, oral evidence, websites." Your teacher names the
style. Below is what every style needs.

## What a historical citation has to carry

Published work needs author, title, publisher and year. Archival material
needs more, because the point is to send someone to the same piece of paper:
the **repository**, the **collection or record group**, the **reference
number**, the **item and its date**, and the date you consulted it. Copy the
reference number exactly as the archive prints it — a search will not
reconstruct it. Testimony needs the speaker, the project that recorded it,
the custodian holding it now, the date recorded and the date accessed: two
dates, for the reasons set out in [[Testimony and Narrative]].

```
Archival document, note form:
  1. Executive Order 9066, 19 February 1942, Milestone Documents,
     National Archives and Records Administration, accessed
     [date you looked].

Newspaper page:
  2. [Newspaper title], [city], [date], [page], [column], via
     Chronicling America, Library of Congress, accessed
     [date you looked].

Statistical series, reference list:
  U.S. Bureau of the Census. "[Series title and number]."
     Bicentennial Edition: Historical Statistics of the United
     States, Colonial Times to 1970. Washington, 1975. Accessed
     [date you looked].
```

Two conveniences worth knowing. Most large repositories print a suggested
citation on the item page — use it, and add your access date. And a
digitised item has two locations, the physical one and the online one; cite
both, because the online address may change and the reference number will
not.

## Two things that are not sources

An encyclopedia entry is a fine place to check a date and the wrong thing to
call a primary source. Cite it as what it is, then go to the archive.

Text produced by generative artificial intelligence has no author, no date
of observation, and no obligation to have checked anything. It can point you
towards a source; it can never be one. If you cannot trace a claim to
something published with a name and a year on it, you do not have it yet.

Record every reference as you collect it — in [[Your Inquiry Notebook]], at
the moment you find the item — and then use it in [[Building an Argument]].
