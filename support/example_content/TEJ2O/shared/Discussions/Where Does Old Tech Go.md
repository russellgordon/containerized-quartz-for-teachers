Every machine you will ever build, fix, or love has an afterlife.
Some retired hardware is genuinely recycled — shredded, sorted,
smelted for its copper and gold. Some is "recycled" — shipped in
containers to places with cheaper labour and looser rules, where
the burning and the acid baths happen next to homes and schools.
The people who take hardware apart at the end of its life rarely
live near the people who enjoyed it in the middle.
[[E-Waste and the Environment]] carries the background; this
discussion carries the argument.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. A tonne of circuit boards holds more gold than a tonne of gold
   ore — yet most boards are never mined for it. What makes
   recovering it so hard that landfill keeps winning?
2. Who should pay for a machine's afterlife: the company that
   made it, the person who bought it, or the city that collects
   it? Defend one — then say what behaviour your choice rewards.
3. "Shipped overseas for recycling" can mean a real facility or
   an open burn site, and the container looks identical leaving
   port. What proof should we demand before believing the good
   version?
4. Ontario has depots, deposit programs, and certified recyclers
   — yet drawers full of dead phones outnumber them all. Why do
   we hoard old tech instead of returning it?
5. Does doing [[Take It Apart]] change your answers? What does
   knowing how to disassemble a machine make you responsible for?

The argument has a job attached: the drives, boards, and cables in
our own bins have to go somewhere too. Before [[The Refurb Report]]
asks you to give one machine a second life, decide what you think
a first life owes the world at the end of it.
