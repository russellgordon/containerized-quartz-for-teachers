Half this course happens at the bench; the other half happens when you
put technical work into words someone else can follow — in your
[[Tech Journal]], in build notes, and in the written parts of every
task. Writing about technology has one rule that does most of the work:

> [!important] Write for the next technician, who might be you
> Service notes, symptom reports, and build sheets are addressed to
> someone who cannot see your bench — including future-you, who in
> three weeks will remember nothing. If it only makes sense with you
> standing beside it, it is not finished.

## Precision is kindness

| Instead of… | Try… |
| --- | --- |
| "It's broken" | "Powers on, fans spin, three beeps, no display" |
| "I fixed it" | "Reseated the memory in slot 2; POST completes" |
| "Used a guide" | "Followed the maker's install guide; steps 4–6, credited in my notes" |
| "It's done" | "Meets the brief; known issue: front USB untested" |

The same standard runs through [[Reading a Spec Sheet|spec sheets]] and
the [[The Build Sheet|build sheet task]]: exact part names, real
numbers with units, and honest notes about what is unverified.

## Sentence stems that unlock a stuck entry

- The symptom was… and the first thing I checked was… because…
- I expected… but measured… which tells me…
- The fix was… and the way to prevent it next time is…
- A client who is not me would need… before this is genuinely done.

## Claims about technology need evidence too

When we write about [[Where Does Old Tech Go|e-waste]],
[[Locked Down or Wide Open|security]], or
[[The Trades Are Tech|the trades]], the standard is the bench's own:
specific beats sweeping, sources get named, and "I read somewhere" is
a loose connection. Strong technology writing reads like a good
service log — symptom, evidence, action, and an honest note about
what you do not yet know.
