Walk across carpet and touch a doorknob: the snap you feel is at
least 3,000 volts. The chips on a stick of RAM can be wounded by 30.
That gap is why ESD — electrostatic discharge — is the quietest
hazard in this lab: a spark far too small to feel, see, or hear can
damage a component that then fails next month, in someone else's
build, with no mark on it anywhere. You cannot sense the danger, so
the only defence is habit — the discipline [[Safety in the Lab]]
promises we practise until it is the only way that feels normal.

## The three habits

1. **Strap on before the case opens.** The anti-static wrist strap
   clips to bare metal on the chassis and keeps you and the machine
   at the same charge — no difference, no spark. Strap first, then
   tools; off when you leave the bench, hung with the tools at
   reset.
2. **The bag rule.** Components live in their silver anti-static
   bags until the moment they are seated, and go straight back when
   removed. The bench is not a resting place for bare boards — a RAM
   stick set "just for a second" on a fleece sleeve is a coin flip.
3. **Touch the case first.** No strap on — just looking, or handing
   a part across? Touch bare chassis metal first, every time you
   reach in. It drains your charge through the case instead of
   through a chip. Make it automatic, like reaching for a seatbelt.

## What a spark costs

A fried stick of RAM is real money from a real shop budget, and the
worse cost is time: ESD damage is often *latent*, so the machine in
[[Build a Workstation]] boots fine today and fails intermittently in
week five — and no troubleshooting flowchart finds a wound that
leaves no mark. Handle parts by their edges, never the gold
contacts, and give every bare board the respect
[[The CPU and Memory]] explains it has earned.
