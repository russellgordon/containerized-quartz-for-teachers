A professional bench has a tell: you can trace any cable from end to
end without moving anything. Cabling is not tidiness for its own
sake — half of troubleshooting is the question "which wire is this?",
and the habits below make the answer instant. They pay off first in
[[Build a Workstation]], where a side panel has to close over
everything you routed, and again in [[The Network Job]], where eight
identical blue cables leave the same wall plate.

## The four habits

1. **Route with intention.** Power on one side, data on the other,
   nothing crossing a fan. Inside a case, cables follow the channels
   the designer left; on a bench, they follow the edge, not the
   middle. The long, flat way around beats a short diagonal every
   time.
2. **Label both ends.** A label on one end answers half a question.
   Masking tape and a marker are enough: what it is, where it goes.
   Future-you, kneeling behind a rack with a flashlight, can only
   read the end they can see.
3. **Strain relief.** The connector is the weakest point, so the
   cable — not the plug — should take any pull. A tie or a strap
   near each end means a snagged foot yanks against the anchor
   instead of the port. Ports broken by sideways tugs are a repair;
   a hand's width of slack is free.
4. **Leave a service loop.** A little extra length, coiled where the
   cable lands, lets the next person pull the device forward to work
   on it without unplugging anything. Cut-to-exact-length looks
   elegant and services terribly.

## The glance test

Stand back and pick any cable on your bench: can you say what it is,
where both ends are, and what would happen if you unplugged it? If
yes, your bench passes the same inspection a working shop's does. If
no, five minutes now is cheaper than the mystery later — and mystery
cables fail [[Safety in the Lab]], which needs every bench clear
enough to see every cable.
