Technicians search constantly — the skill is not avoiding the search
bar but using it like a professional: asking precisely, judging what
comes back, and crediting what you follow.

## Ask precisely

Hardware searches live or die on the exact model number and the
exact symptom. The model number is on the label, not in your memory:

- Weak: `computer won't turn on`
- Strong: `OptiPlex 7050 three beeps no display`

The beep count, the blink pattern, the exact on-screen wording —
that is the machine telling you what to type. Add the word `manual`
or `service` to reach the documents technicians actually use.

## Manuals first, forums second

- **The manufacturer's manual and spec page** are the authoritative
  answer to "what is this and how does it work?" — dry, current, and
  correct about their own hardware. Start there; it is the habit of
  [[Reading a Spec Sheet]] carried online.
- **Forum threads** are where your exact symptom has usually already
  happened to someone. Judge before trusting: check the date (old
  advice rots as parts change), whether others confirmed the fix
  worked, and whether the answer explains *why* — an answer that
  explains teaches; "just do this" is a lottery ticket.
- **AI assistants** are fast, fluent, and confidently wrong just
  often enough to matter. Treat a generated procedure as a
  hypothesis to check against the manual, never as instructions to
  follow blind on real hardware.

## Judgement and credit

Two standing rules. First: online advice that means opening a power
supply, defeating a fuse or interlock, or touching anything
energized is stop-and-ask, full stop — strangers on a forum did not
sign [[Safety in the Lab]], and you did. Second: credit what you
follow. A [[Tech Journal]] line — "fix from the service manual,
p. 38" — is professional honesty, as [[Our Classroom Norms]] asks.
