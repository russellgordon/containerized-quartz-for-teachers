## What you are making

Somewhere near you, a parabola is happening: a basketball shot, the
water from a fountain, the arch of a bridge. Photograph one, overlay a
grid, and model it with a quadratic in vertex form. Then interrogate
the model: where would the ball land if the net were not in the way?
How high did the water really rise — in metres, not in grid squares?

You finish with the **annotated photo** — curve, grid, and equation
laid over the image — and a one-page account of what the model says,
what it predicts beyond the frame, and where it should not be trusted.

## How to work

1. Capture an arc. Freeze a frame of a shot, catch a fountain in still
   air, find an arch downtown. [[Quadratic Relations]] is the test of
   whether your shape qualifies.
2. Bring the photo into [[Using Desmos]] and lay a grid over it.
   Decide what one grid unit means in real metres — a known height in
   the photo, a door, a person — and defend that choice in writing.
3. Fit the model. One route: slide $a$, $h$, and $k$ in
   $y = a(x - h)^2 + k$ until the curve hugs the arc, the way
   [[The Vertex Form]] reads a graph. Another: pick three good points
   and work out the equation. Use either route — or both, and compare.
4. Interrogate it. The vertex is the real maximum — convert it to
   metres. The zeros say where the arc lands, even where the photo
   cannot see — [[Maximum Enclosure]]'s question, aimed at the world.
5. Be honest about error. The lens distorts, the scale was a
   judgement call, and some points refuse to sit on the curve. Say
   where model and photo disagree, and give heights as a range.
6. Annotate the photo and write the page.

The working periods are class time, and using them well is part of the
task — [[How Marks Work]] explains how.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your page |
| --- | --- |
| A real arc | The photo is yours, and the shape genuinely curves |
| A defended scale | The metres-per-unit choice is stated and justified |
| A fitted model | The equation demonstrably hugs the arc |
| Real-unit answers | Heights and distances arrive in metres |
| Questions beyond the frame | The model predicts what the photo cannot |
| Honest error | Disagreements are measured, and answers carry a range |

## Reflect

End the page with a short reflection: what did the model see that your
eye missed, and where did your eye beat the model? Fitting a curve to
the world teaches you what a model is — and what it is not.

> [!success]- If the curve will not fit (click to expand)
> Check the shape: a hanging chain is nearly a parabola but not quite,
> and photos taken at an angle distort. A model that *almost* fits,
> with the mismatch explained, beats a perfect fit to an easy photo.
