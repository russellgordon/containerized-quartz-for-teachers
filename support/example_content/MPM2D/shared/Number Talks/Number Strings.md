A string is a chain of related problems, served one at a time:
$30 \times 30$, then $29 \times 31$, then $28 \times 32$, then
$25 \times 35$. Each answer is a stepping stone to the next — and in
Grade 10, the strings are quietly doing algebra.

## How we play

1. One problem at a time. Solve it in your head; thumb when ready.
2. A few people defend their methods; each goes on the board.
3. Before computing the next from scratch, ask: what can I reuse?

> [!example]- Four methods for $29 \times 31$ (click to expand)
> - "One less than 30 times one more: $900 - 1 = 899$."
> - "Split the 31: $29 \times 30 + 29 = 870 + 29 = 899$."
> - "Split the 29: $30 \times 31 - 31 = 930 - 31 = 899$."
> - "It is $(30 - 1)(30 + 1)$ — the square minus the little square."
>
> Then $25 \times 35 = 900 - 25$ fell in seconds, and someone wrote
> $(30 - d)(30 + d) = 900 - d^2$ on the board. Nobody argued.

## One variation

Run the string in reverse: $41 \times 39$, then $x^2 - 1$, then
$x^2 - 49$ — which products were these, before someone multiplied
them out? Recognizing a difference of squares on sight is the engine
of [[Expanding and Factoring]].

> [!tip] Lazy, in the best way
> Mathematicians refuse to compute what they can deduce. If a problem
> feels brand new, look back along the string — it rarely is.
