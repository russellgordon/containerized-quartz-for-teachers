One algebraic claim on the board — say, $(x+3)^2 = x^2 + 9$ — and one
job: put it on trial. Is it ever true? Always true? An equation is not
a fact just because it is written down; it is a claim about numbers,
and claims earn their verdicts in court.

## How we play

1. Vote first — true, false, or "it depends" — before any working.
2. Prosecute and defend: test values, expand, sketch, whatever bites.
3. Deliver a verdict with evidence: *always*, *never*, or *exactly when*.

> [!example]- The trial of $(x+3)^2 = x^2 + 9$ (click to expand)
> - "Try $x = 1$: left side 16, right side 10. False."
> - "Try $x = 0$: both sides are 9. So it is not *never* true."
> - "Expand: $(x+3)^2 = x^2 + 6x + 9$. The sides differ by $6x$, so
>   they agree exactly when $6x = 0$."
> - "Graph both: two parabolas crossing at a single point, $x = 0$."
>
> Verdict: true precisely once — the middle term $6x$ was the whole case.

## One variation

Claims about graphs: "the vertex of $y = (x+5)^2$ is at $(5, 0)$".
Tempting, false, and one test point settles it — the sign trap that
[[The Vertex Form]] warns about, caught in open court.

> [!tip] One witness is not a proof
> A single counter-example kills an "always". A single confirming
> example proves nothing — to speak about *all* numbers you need
> algebra or a graph, the standard of [[What Makes a Proof Convincing]].
