Desmos is free, runs in any browser, and is the fastest way in the
world to ask a graph a question. Treat it as a thinking tool — a place
to test conjectures — not an answer machine you obey.

## Sliders on the vertex form

Type $y = a(x-h)^2 + k$ and Desmos offers to make $a$, $h$, and $k$
adjustable. Before you drag anything, **predict aloud**: what will $h$
do? Which way will the parabola slide when $h$ grows? Then slide, and
watch the graph agree or object. Two minutes of sliding builds the
intuition behind [[Transformations of Parabolas]] faster than an hour
of hand-plotting — and when the graph surprises you (the $(x - h)$
sign trap from [[The Vertex Form]] surprises everyone), you have found
the exact edge of your understanding. Prediction first is what keeps
Desmos a thinking tool; sliding without predicting is just television.

## Two equations, one screen

Type in both equations of a linear system and the solution is simply
the point where the lines cross — the entire geometric meaning of
[[Linear Systems]], sitting there on screen. Zoom in on the
intersection to read the coordinates, then let the algebra from
[[Solving Systems Algebraically]] earn the *exact* values. Graph
first, solve second, and the two methods referee each other — the
same double-check [[Crossing Paths]] demanded at the boards.

## When your head is faster

Desmos shines when the question is about *shape and behaviour*:
families of parabolas, a circle meeting a line, a conjecture checked
on ten cases in ten seconds. It is slower than your head for
$30 \times 40$, for judging whether an answer is the right size, and
for everything [[Estimation Duels]] trains. Reaching for a tool you do
not need is its own kind of slow — the skill is knowing which moment
you are in.
