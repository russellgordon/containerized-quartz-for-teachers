Being stuck is not an emergency — in this course it is the expected
state at the start of every thinking task. But there are two kinds of
stuck. **Stuck-and-thinking** is still generating moves; it feels
uncomfortable and is quietly productive. **Stuck-and-spinning** is
rereading the same line while feeling worse; nothing is moving except
the feeling. [[Why Struggle Is the Point]] is the why; this page is
the how — five moves for turning spinning back into thinking.

## Five moves, in rough order

1. **Re-read the problem aloud.** Actually aloud, or at least moving
   your lips. A shocking share of stuckness is a misread question —
   "maximise the area" is not "find the area" — and the ear catches
   what the eye skims.
2. **Try a smaller case.** Thirty people shaking hands too many? Try
   four. [[The Handshake Problem]] and [[Visual Patterns]] both
   surrender to whoever shrinks them first, and so does nearly every
   quadratic pattern in this course.
3. **Sketch it.** A rough parabola with its vertex guessed, a
   labelled triangle, four vertices on a grid. Paper holds the
   picture so your head can hold the thinking — no proof in
   [[The Quadrilateral Case File]] ever started without a diagram.
4. **Estimate the size of the answer.** Before solving, ask: is this
   height closer to 5 m or 500 m? An expected size gives you a wall
   to bounce ideas off — and flags nonsense the moment it appears.
5. **Explain the stuck-point to someone.** Not "I don't get it" but
   "I completed the square and the vertex came out below the
   $x$-axis, which cannot be right because…". Half the time you hear
   the way forward as it leaves your mouth.

## When to step away

If all five moves are spent and you are spinning, stepping away is a
strategy, not a surrender — brains keep working on problems in the
background, which is why solutions famously arrive in showers. Leave
yourself a note about exactly where it broke, come back after a walk
or a sleep, and if the wall is still solid, that is what
[[Getting Help]] is for. Arriving with a well-described stuck-point is
not weakness; it is the most efficient question a student can ask.
