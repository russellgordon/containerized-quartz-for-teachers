Here is the discovery that makes trigonometry possible: in any right
triangle with a $35°$ angle, the side opposite that angle divided by
the hypotenuse gives the *same number* — no matter how large the
triangle is. All right triangles sharing that acute angle are
similar, and [[Similar Triangles]] keep their ratios. The ratio
belongs to the *angle*, not to any particular triangle — so someone
could measure the ratios once for every angle and hand you the
results. That is exactly what the sin, cos, and tan buttons on a
calculator are.

## Three ratios, named from the angle

Stand at the acute angle $A$ and name the sides from there: the
*hypotenuse* is always opposite the right angle; the *opposite*
side is across from $A$; the *adjacent* side is beside it.

| Ratio | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| $\sin A$ | opposite $\div$ hypotenuse |
| $\cos A$ | adjacent $\div$ hypotenuse |
| $\tan A$ | opposite $\div$ adjacent |

Move to the other acute angle and the labels swap — opposite
becomes adjacent — so name the sides *after* choosing the angle.

## Finding a side

A 12 m ladder leans at $70°$ to the ground. How high up the wall
does it reach? The hypotenuse is known and the height is the
opposite side — the two sides involved choose the ratio:

$$
\sin 70° = \frac{h}{12} \implies h = 12 \sin 70° \approx 11.3
\text{ m}
$$

## Finding an angle

A ramp rises 1.2 m over a horizontal run of 9 m. Opposite and
adjacent are involved, so tangent is the ratio, and the inverse
function recovers the angle from it:

$$
\tan A = \frac{1.2}{9} \implies
A = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1.2}{9}\right) \approx 7.6°
$$

With the three ratios and the Pythagorean theorem, any right
triangle surrenders every side and angle from just two clues.
Estimate first — an [[Estimation Duels]] instinct that flags 11.3 m
as sensible and 113 m as absurd. [[Trig Ratios and Laws Practice]]
builds the fluency; [[Inaccessible Heights]] puts it to work.
