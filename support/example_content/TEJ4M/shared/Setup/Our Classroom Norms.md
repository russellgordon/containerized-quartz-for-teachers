This course hands you instruments, components, and a blank page, and
asks you to make decisions in public that other people will then pick
apart. That takes norms, and they bind everyone in the room, including
your teacher.

> [!danger] The core agreement
> Nobody's dead circuit gets laughed at, and nobody's rejected design
> gets laughed at either. Every engineer you would trust has destroyed
> a component, wired something backwards, defended a choice that turned
> out to be wrong, and asked for help with something they felt they
> should already know. That is not the exception in this profession.
> That is how the profession is learned.

## What we agree to

- **Specify before you build.** Say what the thing has to do, in terms
  somebody could test, before you choose a single part. A requirement
  written after the result is not a requirement, and a design that met
  it has not met anything.
- **Predict before you power, with a number and a condition.** Third
  year, same rule, one more clause. "It should work" is not a
  prediction and never was.
- **Measure before you argue.** Two benches disagreeing about what a
  circuit does is a question with an answer sitting on the bench. Get
  the instrument. It settles it, and neither of you loses.
- **Bring the alternative you rejected.** A choice you can only defend
  is a preference. When you present a decision, name what else you
  considered and why it lost. This is the single habit that separates
  this year from last.
- **Critique the design, never the designer, and always name the
  requirement.** "That will not meet the 500 mA budget" is a review
  comment. "I would have done it differently" is noise. Receiving a
  hard question well is a skill, and it is one we practise on purpose
  before [[The Engineering Review]] makes it count.
- **Symptoms are information.** A hot component, a rail that sags, a
  reading that will not settle — the circuit is being as helpful as it
  knows how to be. We read them before we groan at them.
- **Roles rotate.** Hands, instrument, recorder. No exceptions for "but
  I am faster". Somebody who never drives the analyzer never learns to
  drive the analyzer.
- **Nothing is finished until somebody else could build it.** A design
  that works and cannot be reproduced scores below a documented one
  with an honest known-issues list. See
  [[Writing Documentation Somebody Can Build From]].
- **Honest about help.** A classmate's suggestion, a datasheet, an
  application note, a forum thread, an AI assistant — all completely
  fine **when named**, in your journal and in your write-ups, exactly
  the way professionals credit their sources. Presenting someone else's
  work or reasoning as your own is the one unforgivable fault in this
  room.
- **Technology serves people.** When we argue about repairability,
  ownership, security, and pathways in
  [[Discussions/index|Discussions]], we argue with ideas and never at
  people. Come ready to change your mind; it is a strength.

## Why this is not just being nice

A lab that shares half-working designs learns faster, because faults
get found while they are still cheap to fix. A lab where people admit
confusion catches design errors before they are soldered in. A lab
where criticism names a requirement produces engineers who can be told
something is wrong without hearing that they are.

And there is a harder reason. In a few years, one of you will be the
person who has to say "this is not ready" while everyone else wants to
ship. That is not a personality trait. It is a habit, and it is built
in rooms where saying the uncomfortable true thing is normal and
survivable. That is what these norms are for, and it is the same
argument [[When Good Enough Is Not Safe]] makes from the other
direction.
