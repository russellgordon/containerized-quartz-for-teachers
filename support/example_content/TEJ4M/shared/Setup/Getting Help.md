Stuck is a normal working condition in this profession, and a good part
of this course is learning to be stuck *well*. The order to work
through:

1. **Measure before you guess.** Grade 10 said look, listen, and read.
   Grade 11 added the meter. This year the first question is often
   "measure it under what condition?" — a rail that is fine at idle and
   collapses under load will pass every static check you run.
   [[Getting Unstuck]] has the full method.
2. **Check the site.** The class page says what we did; the
   [[Concepts/index|concept pages]] state every idea precisely; every
   practice set in [[Exercises/index|Exercises]] has worked answers
   folded below; and every tutorial in [[Tutorials/index|Tutorials]]
   covers a bench skill properly rather than in passing.
3. **Read the datasheet — and then the errata.** If the question is
   "what is this part supposed to do", the manufacturer has answered it
   in writing, and if the answer is "it should work but it does not",
   there is often a second document that says why.
   [[Reading a Datasheet Like an Engineer]] is how to find both.
4. **Ask your bench, then ask another bench.** Explaining a fault out
   loud fixes a startling proportion of it before anyone replies.
   Remember the norm: helpers ask questions, they do not grab the
   probes.
5. **Bring it to a design review.** Some problems are not faults at
   all — they are a design that cannot meet its own requirement, and no
   amount of debugging will fix that. Five minutes in front of another
   bench will tell you which kind you have.
6. **Ask me in class.** Bench time is what it is for. Anything
   involving powering something up for the first time, an unfamiliar
   instrument, supplies in series, heat, or a smell — ask **first**,
   not after.
7. **Come to a help session.** Bring the specific broken thing. "My
   circuit does not work" is hard to help with; "this rail reads 4.1 V
   at the board under load and 5.00 V at the supply" is easy.

![[Help Sessions]]

> [!tip] Name where it broke, with a number and a condition
> "No output. Rail reads 5.02 V at idle and 4.10 V with the fan
> running, measured at the board. Pin 7 reads 0 V, pin 14 reads
> 4.98 V. Firmware is the commit tagged for last week's review." That
> is a symptom report a working engineer would be proud of, and it is
> already most of the way to the fix. Half the value of asking well is
> that you often answer yourself in the middle of the sentence — the
> five-part fault report in [[Getting Unstuck]] is exactly this, in a
> form you can write down.

One more thing, and it matters more than any of the above. If the
trouble is bigger than the course — time, workload, something at home,
decisions about next year, or anything happening online that worries
you — tell me straight away, in whatever way is easiest for you. And
any safety incident, however small it feels, gets reported the moment
it happens: see [[Safety in the Lab]]. Reporting is information, never
trouble.
