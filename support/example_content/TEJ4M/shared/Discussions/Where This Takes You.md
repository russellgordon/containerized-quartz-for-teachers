Three years ago this argument was about respect: who gets called a
technology worker, and why "good with your hands" still gets said like
a consolation prize. That argument is settled in this room. You take
measurements with conditions attached. You read a datasheet to find a
limit rather than guessing at one. You design something, defend the
margins, and write it up so a stranger could build it.

So the question changes. Not *is this a real career* — it obviously is
— but **which door, and what actually gets you through it**.

## The pathways, honestly compared

| Route | Roughly how long | Who certifies you | What it costs while you train |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Apprenticeship | Several years of on-the-job hours plus in-school terms | The province, through a Certificate of Qualification; many trades add a Red Seal endorsement | You earn wages throughout, at a rising percentage of journeyperson rate |
| College — technician diploma | About two years | The college, plus professional certification you apply for afterwards | Tuition, with a shorter time to full earnings |
| College — technologist advanced diploma | About three years | As above, at a higher designation | Tuition, and a year longer out of the workforce |
| University engineering | Four years, plus experience and exams for a licence | An accredited programme, then the provincial regulator | Tuition for four years before earnings begin |
| Straight to work, with a portfolio | Immediately | Nobody — your evidence has to do all the work | Nothing, and you carry the risk |

Two things in that table are worth stating plainly rather than
implying. First, in Ontario the technologist route has its own
professional certification, applied for after graduation and
experience, with designations that mean something to employers.
Second, the licence to call yourself a professional engineer is not
granted by a degree. It comes from the provincial regulator after an
accredited education, a period of supervised engineering experience,
and a professional practice examination. The degree is the entry
ticket, not the destination — a detail that surprises most people who
have only heard the word "engineer" used loosely.

There are also routes that start before you graduate: a youth
apprenticeship placement that turns co-op time into registered
apprenticeship hours, a dual credit at a nearby college, a Specialist
High Skills Major bundling your technology credits with certifications
employers recognise. Ask early. These have deadlines and limited
seats, and nobody comes to find you.

## The portfolio that actually gets read

Here is the part nobody tells you, and it applies to every row of that
table. Admissions committees, apprenticeship sponsors, and hiring
managers are all reading quickly, and they are all reading for the same
thing: **evidence that you can do the work and explain it**.

What does not get read: a list of technologies you have "worked with".
A page of enthusiasm. A gallery of photos of things that light up.

What does get read, every time, is one project explained properly.

- **What it had to do**, stated as requirements with numbers.
- **What you decided**, with the alternative you rejected and why it
  lost. This single move separates a builder from a designer, and it
  is the reason your [[Tech Journal]] has been asking for it all
  semester.
- **What you measured**, with units and conditions, including the
  measurement that did not agree with your prediction.
- **What is still wrong with it**, honestly. An empty known-issues list
  is not believed by anybody who has ever built anything.
- **Enough that somebody could rebuild it** — schematic, bill of
  materials, test procedure. The standard in
  [[Writing Documentation Somebody Can Build From]] is not a school
  standard; it is the deliverable.

One project at that depth beats six at gallery depth, and it is worth
practising the ninety-second spoken version too: what it is, what you
decided, what you measured, what you would do differently. That is
exactly the shape of [[The Engineering Review]], which is not a
performance exercise dressed up as engineering — it is the interview,
rehearsed early.

## Questions worth arguing about

1. Name three things you did at this bench this semester that a
   first-year apprentice does in their first month, and three that a
   first-year engineering student does that you have not done. What
   does the comparison show, and what does it fail to show?
2. One route pays you from the start and one charges you for four
   years first. Both end in skilled technical work. Why is only one of
   them ever described as "settling", and who benefits from that
   framing?
3. Every route in the table has a gate — apprenticeship hours,
   accreditation, certification, licensure. Are those gates protecting
   the public, protecting the people already inside, or both at once?
   What evidence would distinguish them?
4. "Automation will take the trades" against "somebody has to install,
   calibrate, and repair the automation." Which claim survives contact
   with evidence? Be specific about *tasks*, not job titles — the
   claim is much easier to test that way.
5. Suppose you have a strong portfolio and no credential. Which doors
   open, which stay shut, and which open for a while and then close
   later in a career? What does that tell you about what a credential
   is actually for?

The habits this course banked are the first rungs of every route in
that table. A student who can produce a specification with acceptance
criteria, defend a component choice against its alternatives, take a
measurement with its conditions, and hand over documentation somebody
else could build from has not been preparing for a technical career.
They have started one, and the paperwork is a formality that comes
later.[^1]

[^1]: One practical detail worth knowing before you choose where to
    train: a Certificate of Qualification is provincial, but many
    trades carry a Red Seal endorsement that is recognised across
    Canadian provinces and territories. The same credential travels
    with you if the work does — which matters more than it sounds like
    it should when you are twenty-three and the work is somewhere
    else.
