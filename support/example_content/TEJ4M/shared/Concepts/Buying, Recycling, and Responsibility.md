A technologist decides what gets bought and what happens to it
afterwards. Both decisions have consequences well beyond the budget
line, and a Grade 12 course is the right place to say so plainly.

## What the technology has been worth

The benefits are real and should not be waved away:

- **Access.** Information, education, and services reach people and
  places that had none of them, at a cost that keeps falling.
- **Efficiency.** Design that took a room of drafters takes one
  workstation; logistics that wasted fuel now do not.
- **Safety.** Monitoring, modelling, and control systems keep people out
  of places that used to kill them.
- **Connection.** Distance costs less than it ever has, for families,
  for medicine, and for work.
- **New work.** Whole trades and professions exist that did not.

## What it has cost

The drawbacks are equally real, and mostly fall on people other than the
buyer:

- **Extraction.** Cobalt, tin, tantalum, and rare earths are mined in
  conditions that include child labour and armed control of mine sites.
- **Manufacture.** Long hours, poor protection from solvents, and
  supply chains deliberately hard to audit.
- **Energy and water.** Fabrication and data centres consume both at
  industrial scale.
- **Waste.** Electronics are the fastest-growing waste stream on earth,
  and much of what is "recycled" is exported and dismantled by hand.
- **Human effects.** Privacy loss, surveillance, addictive design,
  sedentary lives, and the concentration of decisions in a few
  companies.

Neither list cancels the other. A professional holds both and decides
anyway — which is what a purchasing policy is for.

## A purchasing policy that means something

Write it as criteria a purchase must meet, not as sentiments:

| Criterion | What to require | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fitness** | Meets the stated requirements, with headroom named | The specification from [[Writing a Specification]] |
| **Total cost** | Purchase, power, consumables, support, and disposal | Energy rating; expected service life |
| **Repairability** | Replaceable battery and storage; available parts and manuals | Manufacturer's parts policy; teardown scores |
| **Longevity** | Security updates promised for a stated number of years | The vendor's published support window |
| **Labour and materials** | Supplier's published supply-chain audit; conflict-minerals reporting | The vendor's own reports, read sceptically |
| **End of life** | Manufacturer take-back, or a certified local recycler | Certification of the recycler, not a claim on a web page |

> [!important] The uncomfortable part
> Applying this policy honestly sometimes means buying the more
> expensive machine, or refusing the cheapest quote. If a policy never
> costs anything, it is decoration. Say in your recommendation what the
> policy cost and why it was worth it.

## Reuse before recycling

Recycling recovers some material and destroys the rest of the value.
Reuse keeps all of it. In order of preference: repair it; upgrade it;
redeploy it for a lighter job; donate it, wiped and documented;
harvest parts; and only then recycle through a certified processor.

A school-scale reuse programme is a real project and a small one:
collect, triage, wipe to a documented standard, repair what is
economic, place the machines where they are needed, and record where
every unit went. The record is the part people skip and the part that
makes it defensible — a machine given away with a drive still full of
somebody's data is not a donation, it is a breach.
