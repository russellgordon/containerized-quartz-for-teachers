Writing about what you have read has conventions, and they are worth
knowing for the same reason that road markings are: everybody expects
them, so following them makes you easier to follow.

## The seven that matter most

1. **Present tense for what happens in a text.** *Frenchie survives.*
   Not *survived*. The book is happening now, every time it is read.
   Use past tense only for what happened in the world: *Dimaline
   published the novel in 2017.*
2. **Titles.** Books, films, and plays are in *italics*; short stories,
   poems, articles, and episodes are in "quotation marks". So:
   *The Marrow Thieves*, but "I Lost My Talk".
3. **Name the writer, not "the author".** Rita Joe wrote it. Using her
   name is more precise and more respectful, and it stops sentences from
   sounding like they were made by a machine.
4. **Quote to prove, not to fill.** A quotation is evidence for a claim
   you have already made. If you cannot say what a quotation proves,
   delete it.
5. **Introduce and follow every quotation.** Claim, quotation,
   explanation — in that order. The explanation is the part students
   skip, and it is the part that carries the marks.
6. **Short quotations run inside your sentence**, in quotation marks,
   with the page or line in brackets afterwards. Anything longer than
   about three lines is set as a block, indented, with no quotation
   marks.
7. **Do not summarise the plot.** Your reader has read it. Every
   sentence should be doing analysis, not narration.

## The sandwich, which actually works

> Frenchie's decision to travel north is less about safety than about
> belonging. When he says the north is "where the stories still live", he
> is naming the thing the raiders cannot take: not blood, but memory.
> The word *still* does the work — it admits how much has already been
> lost.

Claim, then evidence, then the sentence that explains what the evidence
shows. Three moves. Most weak paragraphs are missing the third.

## What "voice" means when we mark it

Not slang, and not flourishes. Voice means the writing sounds like a
person made deliberate choices: short sentences for weight, long ones for
momentum, a word chosen because it is right rather than because it is
big. See [[Voice]].

> [!warning] The two habits that flatten a piece fastest
> **Padding** — "in today's modern society", "since the dawn of time" —
> which delays your point and tells your reader you are stalling. And
> **thesaurus words** used without hearing them: *utilize* for *use*,
> *individuals* for *people*. Say the sentence aloud. If you would not
> say it, do not write it.
