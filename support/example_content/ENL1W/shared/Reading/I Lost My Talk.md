> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Rita Joe**, Mi'kmaw poet (1932–2007) · from *Poems of Rita Joe*
> (1978) · four short stanzas · about the Shubenacadie Indian
> Residential School in Nova Scotia

> [!warning] What this text deals with
> Residential school, and the loss of a language. Rita Joe was orphaned
> at ten and sent to Shubenacadie, where speaking Mi'kmaw was punished.

## Who wrote it

Rita Joe was born in Whycocomagh, Nova Scotia, in 1932. She began writing
in her forties, in part because she was tired of reading what other
people had written about Mi'kmaw people. She published several
collections, was appointed to the Order of Canada in 1989, and became a
member of the Queen's Privy Council for Canada in 1992.

She called herself a gentle warrior, and this poem is the clearest
demonstration of what she meant by it.

## Before you read

Two things to hold:

1. **"Talk" is not slang for "language" by accident.** Read the title
   again after you finish and ask what "talk" includes that "language"
   leaves out.
2. **The poem is addressed to someone.** Find out who, and notice what it
   asks of them at the end.

## What to notice

- **The verbs in the first stanza.** Not *lost* only — look at what the
  other person does. There is a difference between losing something and
  having it taken, and the poem uses both.
- **The second stanza's smallness.** Two lines. Ask why the poem gets
  quieter exactly there.
- **The turn.** Something changes in the last stanza. Most students
  expect anger and find something harder to name.
- **"Let me find my talk."** A request, made to the person who took it.
  That is the line the whole poem is built to arrive at, and it is the
  one worth sitting with.

## Questions we will argue about

1. Is the ending forgiveness, an invitation, a demand, or something the
   English words we have do not quite cover?
2. The poem is short. What does its shortness do that a longer poem could
   not?
3. Rita Joe wrote it in English — the language of the school. What does
   that choice do to the poem?

## Beside the poem

We will also read her own short account of writing it, and listen to the
poem read aloud in Mi'kmaw and in English. Hearing it in both is not a
bonus activity; it is most of the point.
