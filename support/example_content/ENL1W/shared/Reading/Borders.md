> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Thomas King** · short story, from *One Good Story, That One* (1993) ·
> a mother and son at the Canada–United States border · told by the son,
> years later

## The situation

A Blackfoot mother and her son drive from Alberta toward Salt Lake City
to visit his sister. At the border, an officer asks the mother's
citizenship. She answers "Blackfoot." The officers want "Canadian" or
"American." She will not say either. Nobody shouts; nobody moves.

The whole story is what happens in the space between that question and
that answer.

## What to notice

- **The narrator is a child**, remembering. What he understands at the
  time and what the reader understands are not the same, and the gap is
  where the story lives — see [[Point of View]].
- **Repetition.** Count how many times "Blackfoot" is said, and by whom.
  Nothing repeated that often is accidental.
- **The duty-free shop**, which is nowhere: not Canada, not the United
  States. Ask what King gets from setting most of the story there.
- **Humour.** The story is funny in places. Ask what the comedy is doing
  — it is not there to lighten the mood.
- **The mother never explains herself.** To anyone. That includes the
  reader.

## Questions we will argue about

1. Is the mother being stubborn, or is she being precise? Can she be
   both?
2. Who has power in this story, at which moments? Trace it: it moves.
3. The story ends with the border being crossed. Is that a victory, and
   for whom?
4. What does the story say about the line itself — a border drawn across
   territory that had nations on it before the border existed?

## The craft worth stealing

King writes almost no description and almost no interior monologue. The
story is dialogue and small actions, and it still delivers a whole
argument about sovereignty. When you next think a piece needs more
explaining, look at what he achieves by refusing to explain.
