## How the book is built

A student interviews an Elder for a school assignment. She tells him her
story, and the book moves between the present-day conversation and the
girl she was — Betsy, taken at eight years old, holding on to what her
father told her before she went.

That frame matters. The story is being *given*, by a person, to a
listener who asked properly. The book is showing you how to receive it.

## Reading a graphic novel closely

| Element | What to read it for |
| --- | --- |
| **Panel size** | A big panel slows time; a row of small ones speeds it |
| **The gutter** | The white space between panels, where the reader does the work |
| **Colour** | Watch how the palette shifts between the present and the past |
| **Faces** | What a drawing can do in one expression that a paragraph needs a page for |
| **Text and image together** | Where the words and the picture say different things — that gap is deliberate |

## Questions we will argue about

1. Why did Robertson and Henderson choose a graphic novel? What can this
   form carry that prose could not?
2. The book is dedicated to survivors and built from one Elder's account.
   What responsibilities does that place on the people who made it — and
   on us?
3. What does Betsy's father's promise do across the whole book? Track
   every time it returns.

## The history beside it

We will read the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's summary of what
residential schools were and how long they ran, and several of the 94
Calls to Action — including the ones about what students in this province
are taught. This is not background to the novel. The novel is one
person's account of the thing those documents describe.
