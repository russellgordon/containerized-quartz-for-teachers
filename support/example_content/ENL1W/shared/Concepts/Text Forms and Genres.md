A **form** is the shape a text takes: a short story, an essay, a poem, a
news report, a podcast, a graphic novel. A **genre** is the kind of thing
it is within that shape: horror, memoir, satire, romance.

Both come with expectations, and every writer either meets them or breaks
them on purpose.

## The forms this course reads and writes

| Form | You can expect | The reader wants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Short story** | One main character, a compressed time frame | To be pulled in fast and paid off |
| **Novel** | Room for change, subplots, a world | To live somewhere for a while |
| **Poem** | Line breaks doing work, compression, sound | To feel more than the words say |
| **Spoken word** | Written to be heard: rhythm, repetition, direct address | To be spoken to, not read at |
| **Graphic novel** | Words and images sharing the telling | To read the gaps between panels |
| **Memoir** | A true story, shaped | To trust that it happened, and be moved anyway |
| **News report** | Most important first, sources named | To know what happened without being told what to think |
| **Opinion piece** | A claim, defended | To be argued with honestly |
| **Review** | A judgement with reasons | To decide whether to bother |
| **Podcast or interview** | Voice, pace, question and answer | To listen while doing something else |

## Cultural forms

Not every form arrives from the same tradition. Oral storytelling, the
speech, the land acknowledgement, the letter to an editor, the protest
song — each has its own conventions and its own community that owns them.
When we read one, we ask who it belongs to and what it is for.
[[Indigenous Storywork]] is where this course takes that question
seriously rather than in passing.

## Breaking form on purpose

A news report written as a poem, or a short story told entirely in text
messages, is making a point by breaking a pattern. That only works if the
reader knows the pattern — which is the practical reason to learn what
each form usually does.

> [!question] Worth arguing about
> Is a thread of social posts a text form of its own, with its own
> conventions, or a broken version of an older one? Defend either. Then
> say what the conventions ARE, which is the harder half.
