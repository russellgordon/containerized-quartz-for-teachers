A paragraph is one idea, developed. If you cannot say what the idea is in
a phrase, the paragraph is either two paragraphs or none.

## The shape that works for analysis

1. **Claim** — the sentence you could argue with.
2. **Evidence** — a quotation or a specific detail.
3. **Explanation** — what the evidence shows, in your words. Two
   sentences, not one.
4. **Link** — a sentence that connects it back to the larger argument.

That is the pattern in [[Writing About Texts]], and it is worth learning
because it makes the *thinking* visible rather than assumed.

## Cohesion: how sentences hold hands

Sentences in a strong paragraph are connected by more than proximity:

| Tie | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Repeated key word** | …the border. That border is… |
| **Pronoun with a clear owner** | Joe writes about the school. She was ten when… |
| **Transition word** | However, Instead, As a result, Even so |
| **Old before new** | End a sentence with the idea the next one starts from |

The last one is the professional move. Readers hold a sentence's ending
in mind, so starting the next sentence there makes prose feel effortless.

## Length

There is no rule, and the "five sentences" you may have been taught is
scaffolding, not law. But two signs are reliable: a paragraph over about
200 words is usually two ideas, and a page of one-sentence paragraphs is
usually a list pretending to be an argument.

## Openings and endings

The first sentence of a paragraph orients the reader. The last one should
do something rather than restate the first. In student writing, the
weakest sentence in the piece is almost always the last sentence of a
paragraph, because it is where people stop thinking and start rounding
off.

> [!tip] The test that finds every weak paragraph
> Cover everything but the first sentence of each paragraph and read just
> those, in order. If they tell the argument on their own, the piece is
> organised. If they read as a list of topics, it is not — yet.
