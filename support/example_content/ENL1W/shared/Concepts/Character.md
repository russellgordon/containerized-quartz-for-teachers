A character is not a description. A character is a set of choices under
pressure, and everything else — the hair, the jacket, the accent — is
scenery.

## How writers build one

| Method | Looks like | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What they do** | An action, especially a costly one | The strongest evidence there is |
| **What they say** | Dialogue, and the way they say it | People give themselves away in speech |
| **What they think** | Interior narration | Only available in some points of view |
| **What others say** | Reactions and gossip | Tells you about the speaker too |
| **What they refuse** | A silence, a walk-out, a "no" | Often the loudest thing in a story |

## The two questions worth asking

**What does this character want?** — the surface goal: to win the race,
to get home, to be left alone.

**What do they need?** — the deeper thing, usually invisible to them: to
be believed, to forgive somebody, to stop pretending.

When want and need pull in different directions, you have a novel. When
a character finally chooses the need over the want — or refuses to — that
moment is the climax, whatever else is happening.

## Round, flat, and why flat is not an insult

A **round** character has contradictions and can surprise you. A **flat**
character has one function and does it. A story full of round characters
would be exhausting; the bus driver does not need an inner life. Ask what
each character is *for*.

**Dynamic** means changed by the story. **Static** means unchanged — and
in a story about a system that does not bend, an unchanged character can
be the whole argument.

## Writing about character without summarising

Weak: *Frenchie is brave because he does brave things.*

Better: *Frenchie's bravery is mostly refusal. He almost never attacks;
he stays, when leaving would be reasonable, and the novel treats staying
as the harder act.*

The second one has a claim you could argue with. That is the test.

> [!tip] The fastest way into a character essay
> Find the moment the character does something the reader did not expect,
> and explain why it was in character after all. That paragraph is
> usually the best one in the piece.
