A theme is not a topic. "Family" is a topic — every text has one, and
naming it tells a reader nothing. A theme is what the text *says* about
the topic, in a full sentence somebody could disagree with.

## Topic, theme, moral

| | Example | Why it is that |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Topic** | Language | One word; no claim |
| **Theme** | Losing a language is not the same as losing words — it takes the way you think with you | A claim, arguable, drawn from the text |
| **Moral** | Always speak your own language | An instruction; texts rarely give these, and when they do they are usually weaker for it |

## Building a theme statement

1. **Name the topic** in one or two words.
2. **Ask what the text shows about it** — look at what happens to the
   character who holds each position.
3. **Write it as a sentence with no "should" in it.**
4. **Test it against the ending.** A theme statement the ending
   contradicts is a theme statement about the middle.

> [!example] Doing it on "I Lost My Talk"
> Topic: language and school.
> First attempt: *School is bad.* Too broad, and the poem is more precise
> than that.
> Better: *Rita Joe's poem treats the loss of a language as theft rather
> than accident — and its last stanza asks the person who took it to
> help her speak again, which turns the poem from grief into a demand.*
> That is arguable, specific, and built on the shape of the poem.

## A text can carry several

Themes are not single and not owned by the teacher. Two readers can find
different themes in one text and both be right, as long as both can point
at evidence. What is *not* right is a theme with no evidence, or one
contradicted by half the book.

## Where themes hide

- **In the ending**, especially in what the character finally chooses.
- **In repetition** — an image or phrase that returns is being pointed
  at.
- **In the title.** Writers agonise over titles; read them as claims.
- **In what the text refuses.** A book that never lets a character
  apologise is saying something about apology.
