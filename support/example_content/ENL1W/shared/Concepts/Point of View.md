Point of view is the decision about who is telling this and how much they
can see. It is made before the first sentence, and it changes everything
that follows.

## The options

| Point of view | Pronouns | Sees | Costs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **First person** | I, we | Only what the narrator knows | You cannot leave that head, and you cannot be sure it is honest |
| **Second person** | you | Puts the reader in it | Hard to sustain; rare outside instructions and some poems |
| **Third person limited** | he, she, they | One character's inside, everything else from outside | The most common choice in modern fiction |
| **Third person omniscient** | he, she, they | Everyone's inside | Powerful, and easy to make feel distant |
| **Third person objective** | he, she, they | Only what a camera would see | Cold on purpose — the reader has to infer everything |

## Unreliable narrators

A narrator can be wrong. They can be lying, mistaken, too young to
understand, or too invested to admit something. The signals are usually:

- The narrator's account and other characters' reactions do not match.
- They insist too hard: "it did not bother me at all", three times.
- Their explanation requires everyone else to be unreasonable.

An unreliable narrator is not a trick. It makes the reader do the work of
judging, which is a different and better kind of reading.

## Whose story is being told, and by whom

Point of view is also a question about power. Whose thoughts do we get?
Whose are we never given? A story about a community told entirely from
outside it will teach you as much about the teller as about the
community — which is why [[Whose Story Gets Told]] is a discussion in
this course and not a footnote.

> [!example] The same event, twice
> Write four sentences about a school argument from the point of view of
> the person who started it. Now write the same four in third-person
> objective, where only what a camera saw may appear. Read them side by
> side. The second one lets a reader judge; the first tells them how.
