Everything you write is made of sentences, and most writing problems are
sentence problems wearing a disguise. This page is the toolkit; the
practice is in [[Sentence Craft Practice]].

## The four kinds, and what each is for

| Kind | Shape | Use it to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Simple** | One independent clause | Land a point. Stop a paragraph dead |
| **Compound** | Two independent clauses joined | Balance two equal ideas |
| **Complex** | One independent + one or more dependent | Show which idea depends on which |
| **Compound-complex** | Both at once | Handle something genuinely complicated |

A paragraph of only simple sentences reads like a list. A paragraph with
no simple sentences has nowhere for the reader to breathe. The mix is the
craft.

## Joining clauses without breaking them

Two independent clauses can be joined three ways, and only three:

1. **Comma + joining word** (*and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor*):
   She read it twice, but she still could not say why it worked.
2. **Semicolon**, when the two are closely related:
   She read it twice; the second time was slower.
3. **Full stop.** Two sentences. Always available, always correct.

A comma alone will not hold two sentences together. That is the
comma splice — the most common error in Grade 9 writing and the easiest
to fix once you can hear it.

## Fragments

A fragment is a piece of a sentence punctuated as a whole one. *Because
she left.* — that is not finished, and a reader waits for the rest.

Fragments used **on purpose** are a real technique. *He said he would
come back. Never did.* The difference between craft and error is control:
you can only break a rule you can name.

## Making a sentence better

- **Put the subject and verb close together**, and near the start.
- **Prefer a strong verb to an adverb**: *sprinted* beats *ran quickly*.
- **Cut the throat-clearing**: *It is important to note that* — delete
  and start with the note.
- **Vary the opening.** Three sentences in a row starting *The* is a
  drumbeat you did not intend.
- **Read it aloud.** If you run out of breath, it is too long. If it
  sounds like a robot, the verbs are weak.

> [!example] One sentence, three ways
> *The room was quiet and everyone was waiting and nobody wanted to speak
> first.*
> → *The room was quiet. Everyone was waiting; nobody wanted to speak
> first.*
> → *In the quiet, nobody wanted to speak first.*
> The third is not automatically best. It is the shortest, and it loses
> the waiting. Choosing between them is writing.
