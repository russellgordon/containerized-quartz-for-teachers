The reference is [[Paragraphs That Hold Together]] and the conventions
are on [[Writing About Texts]].

**1.** Here is a claim. Write the three sentences that should follow it:
*Thomas King makes the border feel arbitrary by refusing to describe it.*

> [!success]- Answer 1
> One good version: *The crossing is never given a fence, a line, or a
> landscape — only officers and questions. When the mother is sent back,
> she is sent to a duty-free shop that belongs to neither country, and
> the story lets her stay there for days. By keeping the border invisible
> and the waiting concrete, King makes the line look like something
> people decided rather than something that is there.*

**2.** Find the sentence that does not belong:
*Frenchie's decision to stay is the novel's turning point. Dimaline
withholds his reasoning for two chapters. She was born in Ontario and is
Métis. When he finally explains, the explanation is one line long, and it
is about somebody else.*

> [!success]- Answer 2
> The biographical sentence. True, and irrelevant here: the paragraph is
> about a narrative choice. Facts about a writer belong where they do
> work, not wherever they are known.

**3.** This paragraph is two paragraphs. Where does it split, and what is
each about?
*Olemaun asks to go to school, which is the detail everything else in the
book has to be read against. Her father resists. The photographs at the
back of the book change how the story reads: they make Olemaun a person
who is still alive rather than a character, and the reader's obligation
shifts.*

> [!success]- Answer 3
> Split before *The photographs*. The first idea is her choosing to go;
> the second is what the photographs do to the reader. Two ideas, two
> paragraphs.

**4.** Improve the last sentence, which currently restates the first:
*The repetition of "Blackfoot" is the engine of the story. Every time the
mother says it, the officers hear a refusal and she means a fact. By the
fourth repetition the reader has stopped waiting for her to say something
else. Repetition is therefore very important in this story.*

> [!success]- Answer 4
> Replace it with something that moves: *What the repetition finally
> proves is that she was never negotiating.* The paragraph now ends with
> a claim rather than with its own beginning.

**5.** Write a paragraph of exactly four sentences — claim, evidence,
explanation, link — about any text we have read. Then read it aloud and
cut ten words.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> No single right answer. Check yours: does sentence one make a claim
> somebody could argue with? Is sentence two a quotation or a specific
> detail? Does sentence three say what the evidence *does* rather than
> repeat it? Does sentence four point outward?
