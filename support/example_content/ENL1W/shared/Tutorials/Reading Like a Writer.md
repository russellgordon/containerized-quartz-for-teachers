There are two ways to read: for what happens, and for how it was made.
The first is why people read. The second is what this course adds, and it
is a habit rather than a talent.

## Annotating, which is not highlighting

Highlighting marks a line as important and tells you nothing later.
Annotating means writing *why* — four words in the margin beats a page of
yellow.

Four marks are enough for most texts:

| Mark | Means | Example note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `?` | I do not understand this | "who is speaking here?" |
| `!` | This surprised me | "she agrees with him now?" |
| `↺` | This connects to something earlier | "same phrase as page 12" |
| `✎` | The writing itself is doing something | "one-word sentence — stops you" |

## The three passes

1. **Read it through.** No pen. Find out what happens.
2. **Read it again with a pen**, using the four marks. This pass is
   slower and does the work.
3. **Read only your marks.** Two minutes. What pattern do they make? The
   pattern is usually your idea for the discussion or the essay.

## What to notice on the second pass

- **A word that seems too strong for the moment.** Writers do that on
  purpose.
- **A repeated word or image.** Nothing appears three times by accident.
- **A sentence that breaks the pattern** — a very short one among long
  ones, or the reverse.
- **What is not said.** A character who does not answer is doing
  something.
- **Where time speeds up or slows down.** Two years in a sentence, then
  four pages on one afternoon: that afternoon is the point.

> [!example] Annotating one line
> From Rita Joe's "I Lost My Talk": *"You snatched it away."*
> A student note that works: **✎ "snatched" not "took" — a snatch is fast
> and rude, and it names a person doing it.** That note is already the
> first sentence of a paragraph.

## Doing it on a screen

If the text is digital, use comments or a notes app in a split window —
but keep the four marks. The method is the point; the paper is not.
