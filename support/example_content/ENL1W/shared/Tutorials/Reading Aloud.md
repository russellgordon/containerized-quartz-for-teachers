Reading aloud is a skill, not a personality trait. Everything below can
be practised in ten minutes, and all of it is assessed in
[[Say It Out Loud]].

## Before you read

- **Read it silently twice**, and mark where you will breathe. Breath
  marks are the single biggest improvement available to most readers.
- **Underline the word in each sentence that carries the meaning.** You
  will lean on it slightly. That is what "expression" actually is.
- **Decide your pace.** Almost everyone reads too fast when nervous, so
  plan to go slower than feels right — it will sound normal from the
  outside.

## While you read

| Move | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Pause after a full stop, longer than feels natural | Lets the sentence land |
| Drop your volume for something intimate | Makes the room lean in — much stronger than shouting |
| Look up at the end of a sentence, not the middle | Keeps you in contact without losing your place |
| Let a question actually rise at the end | Otherwise it lands as a statement and confuses the room |
| Slow down for the most important line | The line everyone remembers is the one you gave time to |

## If you are nervous

Nerves are not a flaw in your presentation; they are what a body does
when something matters. Two things genuinely help: knowing your first
sentence by heart so the start is automatic, and breathing out slowly
before you begin rather than in.

If speaking in front of the class is more than nerves — if it is the
thing you dread most about school — tell me in the first week. We will
build to it: to me, then to a partner, then to four people, then to the
room, or we will record it. What we will not do is skip it, because
[[B1. Oral and Non-Verbal Communication|the course asks for it]] and
because it gets easier only one way.

## Non-verbal, which counts as much

Where you stand, what your hands do, whether you look at people. A reader
who looks at the floor is telling the room the text does not matter. Feet
still, hands visible, eyes up at the ends of sentences — that is most of
it.

Bear in mind that eye contact and gesture mean different things in
different cultures, and a room like this one holds several. What we are
learning is one convention, not the only correct one.
