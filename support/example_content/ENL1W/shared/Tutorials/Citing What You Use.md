A citation answers one question: *where would I go to check this?* Get
that right and the formatting details matter far less than people think.

## In your writing

Name the source in the sentence where you use it. That is more readable
than a bracket, and it tells the reader how much weight to give the
claim:

> A 2021 report from the Canadian Institute for Health Information found
> that…

For a text we are studying, put the page or line in brackets after the
quotation:

> Frenchie calls the north "where the stories still live" (162).

## At the end

A list called **Works Cited**, alphabetical by the first thing in each
entry. The pattern for the three sources you will actually use:

**A book**
> Dimaline, Cherie. *The Marrow Thieves*. Dancing Cat Books, 2017.

**A page on a website**
> Smith, Jordan. "Why the River Flooded." *Toronto Star*, 14 May 2024,
> thestar.com/news/river-flood.

**A video**
> Koyczan, Shane. "To This Day." YouTube, uploaded by TED, 2013.

## Three habits that prevent the whole problem

1. **Record the source the moment you use it**, not at the end. The end
   is when links go missing and titles get forgotten.
2. **Write one sentence in your own words** beside every source you save.
   That sentence stops accidental copying, because you have already
   translated the idea.
3. **If you cannot tell whose words they are, they are not yours yet.**
   Close the tab and write the sentence from memory.

> [!important] Plagiarism, in plain terms
> Handing in words or ideas as though you thought of them. It includes
> pasting from a site, rewording somebody's argument without naming them,
> and having a language model write a passage. The consequence here is
> not dramatic: you rewrite it, properly, with me, and we talk about what
> made it feel necessary. But it has to be rewritten, because a mark for
> work that is not yours means nothing.
