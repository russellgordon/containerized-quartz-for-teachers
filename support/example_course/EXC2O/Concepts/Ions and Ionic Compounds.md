## The idea

Atoms gain or lose electrons to reach a full outer shell. A charged atom is an
**ion**, and oppositely charged ions stick together.

- Lose electrons → fewer negatives → **positive** ion (**cation**). Metals do this.
- Gain electrons → **negative** ion (**anion**). Non-metals do this.

$$
\ce{Na -> Na+ + e-} \qquad \ce{Cl + e- -> Cl-}
$$

Put them together and the charges must cancel exactly:

$$
\ce{Na+ + Cl- -> NaCl}
$$

## Balancing charges

Magnesium forms $\ce{Mg^2+}$; chlorine forms $\ce{Cl-}$. One
magnesium needs **two** chlorides:

$$
\ce{MgCl2}
$$

| Cation | Anion | Formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ce{Na+}$ | $\ce{Cl-}$ | $\ce{NaCl}$ |
| $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | $\ce{Cl-}$ | $\ce{CaCl2}$ |
| $\ce{Al^3+}$ | $\ce{O^2-}$ | $\ce{Al2O3}$ |

> [!tip] The crossover trick — and why it works
> Swapping the charges to become subscripts works because it guarantees the
> total positive and total negative charge are equal. Use it, but be able to
> explain *why* it works; that is what a test question will ask.

## Where this is being used right now

Ions moving is what a battery is. In a lithium-ion cell, $\ce{Li+}$ ions
shuttle from one electrode to the other as it discharges, and back again
as it charges — the whole electric vehicle industry rests on that one
sentence of chemistry.

That makes battery chemistry one of the fastest-moving fields there is,
and it is not only a laboratory field:

| Emerging technology | The chemistry | Who works on it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lithium iron phosphate cells | A cheaper, safer cathode using no cobalt | Chemical engineers; battery plant technicians |
| Sodium-ion cells | $\ce{Na+}$ in place of $\ce{Li+}$ — heavier, but sodium is everywhere | Materials chemists |
| Battery recycling | Dissolving cells to recover lithium, nickel, and cobalt as salts | Process operators; industrial mechanics |
| Heat pumps and refrigerants | New working fluids replacing high-warming ones | **Refrigeration mechanics and HVAC technicians** — a licensed skilled trade |

The last row is worth noticing. Ontario is short of the tradespeople who
install and service this equipment, the training is an apprenticeship
rather than a degree, and the chemistry on that page is the chemistry on
this one.
