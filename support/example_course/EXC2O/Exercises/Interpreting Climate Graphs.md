Climate data is usually presented as *anomalies* — differences from a long-term
average — rather than absolute temperatures. Understanding why is half the skill.

## Questions

1. Why plot the **difference** from an average instead of the temperature
   itself? What does that make easier to see?
2. A graph of global temperature rises overall but falls in several individual
   years. Does a falling year contradict the trend? Explain using the difference
   between weather and climate.
3. Two graphs of the same data, one with a vertical axis from $-1$ to
   $+1\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and one from $-20$ to $+20\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$,
   look completely different. Is either dishonest? What should a reader check?
4. A graph starting in 1998 — an unusually warm year — shows a flatter trend
   than one starting in 1970. Name the technique being used and say why it
   misleads.
5. Which of the indicators on [[Indicators of Climate Change]] would be hardest
   to misrepresent this way? Why?

> [!important] The skill being practised
> Not "is this graph true?" but "what is this graph *for*, and what has it left
> out?" That question is the whole of [[A2.4]].
